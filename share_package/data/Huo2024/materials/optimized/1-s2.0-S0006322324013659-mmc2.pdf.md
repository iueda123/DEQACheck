## Supplementary Material Supplementary methods

## Methods S1. Subjects

Subjects from three datasets were utilized in the present study, including two public datasets (Dataset 1 and Dataset 3) and one in-house dataset (Dataset 2).

## Dataset 1

All  the  subjects  were  normal  controls  and  were  obtained  from  the  Cambridge  Centre  for  Ageing  and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN, www.cam-can.org). The Cam-CAN is a large-scale collaborative research project at the University of Cambridge, launched in October 2010. This project used epidemiological, cognitive, and neuroimaging data to understand how individuals can best retain cognitive abilities into old age. A detailed description of the dataset and pre-processing pipeline can be found in (1). A more general overview of the Cam-CAN dataset can be found in (2). Demographic information can be seen in Table S1.

## Dataset 2

The  Multi-Center  Alzheimer  Disease  Imaging  Consortium  Dataset  (MCAD)  was  used  in  our  previous studies to evaluate altered spontaneous activity in Alzheimer's disease (AD) (3,4). To minimize the variation in multicenter data, we included the following quality controls: (1) exclusion of subjects with poor functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data quality, subjects without complete demographic information, and subjects without Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores; (2) removal of subjects with large head motion in any direction corresponding to &gt;3 mm or any rotation &gt;3 ° ; and (3) matching the age and sex of the  subjects  at  each  center.  More  details  about  the  dataset  were  in  our  previous  study  (3).  Here,  with permission, we have rewritten the information to maintain the integrity of the present study as follows:

## PL\_G and PL\_S

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of PLA General Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from each enrolled subject or his/her authorized guardian. All of the participants were recruited by an advertisement (http://www.301ad.com.cn, Chinese version). Prior to selection for this study, all of the participants were given free physical, psychological, and laboratory examinations. All patients received professional suggestions for further treatment.

All of the subjects were right-handed and underwent a battery of neuropsychological tests, including the MMSE, the auditory verbal learning test (AVLT), Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) (5), Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) (6) and Activities of Daily Living (ADL) Scale. In brief, the AVLT consisted of 1 learning trial in which a list of 10 Chinese double-character words was read, and the subject was asked to immediately recall as many items as possible. The trial was repeated twice, and the immediate recall score was the average of 3 accurate recalls. After a 5-minute delay, each subject was asked to recall the words from the initial list

(AVLT-delayed recall). The subjects were then told to identify the 10 studied words that were inter-mixed with 10 novel words (AVLT-recognition).

The recruited AD patients fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosed using the National Institute of  Neurological  and  Communicative  Disorders  and  Stroke  and  the  Alzheimer  Disease's  and  Related Disorders Association criteria for probable AD; (2) CDR = 1 or 2; (3) currently receiving no tropic drugs, such  as  cholinesterase  inhibitors;  and  (4)  able  to  perform  the  neuropsychological  test  and  tolerate  MR scanning.

The diagnostic criteria for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) were determined as previously described (7) and included the following: (1) memory complaints lasting at least 6 months; (2) CDR = 0.5; (3) intact functional status and ADL&lt; 26; and (4) lack of dementia. The criteria for normal control (NC) included the following: (1) normal physical status; (2) CDR = 0; and (3) without memory complaints.

The following exclusion criteria were used in this study: (1) metabolic conditions such as hypothyroidism or vitamin B12/folic acid deficiencies; (2) psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia or depression; (3) infarction or brain hemorrhaging, as indicated by MR/CT imaging; and (4) Parkinsonian syndrome, epilepsy and other nervous system diseases that can influence cognitive function. In addition, patients with a metallic foreign body, such as a cochlear implant, heart stent or other relevant MR scanning contraindications, were excluded from the study.

Related publications can be found elsewhere (8-17).

The control subjects were recruited from the  local  community  using  advertisements.  Written  informed  consent  was  obtained  from  each  enrolled subject  or  his/her  authorized  guardian. The participants didn't undergo the auditory verbal learning test. ## XW\_H

The study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee and Institutional Review Board of Xuanwu Hospital (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT02353884 and NCT02225964). Part of the data have been used in several previous studies, and detail information can be found elsewhere (18,19).

All subjects underwent a series of standardized clinical evaluations, including a medical history interview, neurologic examination, and a battery of neuropsychological tests. The neuropsychological tests included the Chinese version of the MMSE, the Beijing version of Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (20), the CDR (6), the AVLT (21), an ADL assessment, the Hachinski Ischemic Scale, the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) (22), and The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (23). Confirmation of  diagnosis  for  all  subjects  was  made  by  the  consensus  of  at  least  two  experienced  neurologists  in  the Neurology Department  of  Xuanwu  Hospital.  The  diagnoses  were  based  on  the  available  data  from  the neuropsychological  assessment  evaluation,  a  battery  of  general  neurological  examinations,  and  subject symptoms as well as functional capacity reports.

The inclusion criteria for amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) diagnosis included the following (24): (a)  memory  complaints,  confirmed  by  an  informant;  (b)  objectively  impaired  memory  confirmed  by neuropsychological tests; (c) a definite history of cognitive decline; (d) not meeting the criteria for dementia according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition, Revised (DSM-IVR); and (e) a CDR score of 0.5.

AD subjects were diagnosed according to the National Institute of Aging-Alzheimer's Association (NIAAA) criteria for clinically probable AD (25,26): (a) meeting the criteria for dementia; (b) insidious and gradual onset (not sudden) over more than 6 months; (c) definite history of declining cognition; (d) initial and  most  prominent  cognitive  deficits  evident  in  amnestic  or  non-amnestic  performance;  and  (e) hippocampal atrophy confirmed by structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

## XW\_Z

The NC patients were required to meet the following research criteria: (a) no memory concerns; (b) MMSE and MoCA scores within the normal range (adjusted for age, sex, and education); and (c) a CDR score of 0.

The exclusion criteria  applied  to  all  subjects  included  the  following:  (a)  vascular  cognitive  impairment (Hachinski Ischemic Scale score &gt; 4 points); (b) severe depression (HAMD score &gt; 24 points or The Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale score &gt; 21 points); (c) other central nervous system diseases that could cause cognitive decline (e.g., epilepsy, brain tumors, Parkinson's disease, or encephalitis); (d) systemic diseases that could cause cognitive impairments (e.g., anthracemia, syphilis, thyroid dysfunctions, severe  anemia,  or  HIV);  (e)  a  history  of  psychosis  or  congenital  mental  growth  retardation;  (f)  severe hypopsia or dysacusis; (g) cognitive decline caused by traumatic brain injury; (h) severe end-stage disease or severe diseases in acute stages; (i) a history of stroke; or (j) unable to complete neuropsychological tests or with a contraindication for MRI.

All the participants were recruited by advertisement and supported throughout the testing procedures in a specialist neuropsychological research facility at Xuanwu Hospital, Beijing, China. Patients and informants (usually a family member) were interviewed clinically by a senior psychiatrist (X. Zhang). Written consent forms were obtained from all subjects or their legal guardians (usually a family member). The study was approved  by  the  Ethics  Committee  of  Xuanwu  Hospital.  AD  subjects  were  diagnosed  using  standard operationalized criteria (DSM-IVR [American Psychiatric Association, 1994] and NINCDS-ADRDA(26)]).

The inclusion criteria for AD diagnosis included the following: severity of dementia was assessed using the CDR scale (6). Patients with a diagnosis of AD and CDR score of 1 were classified as mild AD; patients with a CDR score of 2 or 3 were diagnosed as severe AD.

MCI was diagnosed according to standard criteria (27,28), which included subjective memory loss with objective evidence of memory impairment in the context of normal or near-normal performance on other domains of cognitive functioning; minimal impairment of activities of daily living; and a CDR score of 0.5. Normal volunteers have a CDR score of 0.

All participants satisfied the following inclusion criteria: (1) no history of an affective disorder within one month prior to assessment; (2) normal vision and audition; (3) able to cooperate with cognitive testing; (4) aged between 50 and 90 years; (5) no clinical history of stroke or other severe cerebrovascular disease; and (6)  no  more  than  one  lacunar  infarction,  without  patchy  or  diffuse  leukoaraiosis,  on  neuroradiological assessment of conventional MR images.

The  exclusion  criteria  included  the  following:  (1)  severe  general  medical  disorders  of  cardiovascular, endocrine, renal or hepatic systems; neurological disorders associated with potential cognitive dysfunction, including local brain lesions, traumatic brain injury with loss of consciousness or confusion, and dementia associated  with  neurosyphilis,  Parkinsonism  or  Lewy  body  disease;  psychiatric  disorders  including depression, alcohol or drug abuse; (2) concomitant use of psychotropic medication in a large quantity; and (3) insufficient cognitive capacity to understand and cooperate with study procedures.

All  patients  underwent  a  complete  physical  and  neurological  examination,  an  extensive  battery  of neuropsychological assessments, and standard laboratory tests. Healthy volunteers underwent a brief clinical interview and MMSE to confirm that they satisfied the exclusion criteria for cognitive deficits, psychoactive drug use, and clinical disorders.

## Method S2. Image preprocessing

The  preprocessing  of  all  fMRI  scans  from  three  datasets  includes  the  following  steps:  (1)  slice  timing correction; (2) realignment to the first volume; (3) spatial normalization to MNI space at 2 mm× 2 mm× 2 mm; (4) regression of nuisance signals, including linear trends, six motion parameters, and their first-order differences,  and  signals  representing  white  matter  and  cerebrospinal  fluid;  and  (5)  temporal  bandpass filtering (0.01-0.08 Hz) to reduce high-frequency noise.

The  structural MRI  images  were  preprocessed  using  the standard steps in  the  CAT12  toolbox (http://dbm.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/, r1450). All structural MRI data were bias-corrected; segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF); and registered to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using a sequential linear (affine) transformation and a standard DARTEL registration (33).  As  described  in  our  previous  study  (33),  the  cortex  and  subcortex  were  parcellated  based  on  the Brainnetome Atlas (34). The mean gray matter volume and cortical thickness of each brain region were extracted by averaging the gray matter volume and cortical thickness in the Brainnetome Atlas.

## Method S3. Identification of dynamic functional connectivity features

Dynamic functional connectivity matrices for each individual were identified using Pearson's correlation coefficients combined with the sliding time window method (window length = 60s) between all pairs of 246 regions. Based on the sliding time window method, we obtained n windows for each time series. Then, functional  brain  networks  were  constructed  in  each  time  window.  Connectivity  strength  (i.e.,  Pearson correlation coefficients) was normalized using Fisher's r-to-z transformation. Each functional connectivity matrix was decomposed into 17 networks based on the Yeo-17 network template. ## Method S4. Functional connections differences

We calculated the degree of the weight of functional connectivity strengths to investigate the differences in the functional connection between the subtypes. Specifically, we identified functional connectivity matrices using Pearson's correlation coefficients between all pairs of 246 regions. Connectivity strength (i.e., Pearson correlation coefficients) was normalized using Fisher's r-to-z transformation. We used graph theory to get a

profile of each brain region and its connectivity strength. In graph theory, the weighted degree for each node represents the nodal strength within a network, calculated as the sum of the weighted edges linked to each region. Large ones show the region-hub connections with greater functional connectivity strength. Then, the resultant region-based data were mapped to volume images for statistical analysis between the subtypes.

## Method S5. Statistical analysis

To  investigate  demographic  and  cognitive  differences  among  the  subtypes  determined  by  the  research institute, we compiled data on the MMSE for all individuals and MoCA scores in Dataset 2. Furthermore, we analyzed variance (ANOVA) and post-hoc two-sample two-tailed t-tests ( P &lt; .05) to assess differences among the subtypes. All features in both datasets were z-scored individually and in combination.

Additionally, we analyzed MMSE and everyday cognitive measurements of longitudinal changes among MCI  subtypes  using  4-year  follow-up  data  from  Dataset  3  (Table  S8).  Linear  regression  models  were employed  to  assess  differences  in  longitudinal  changes  among  subtypes.  The  model  included  baseline follow-up months, subtype, age, sex, and the interaction term between months and subtype to determine whether subtypes exhibit distinct cognitive trajectories ( P &lt; .05).
