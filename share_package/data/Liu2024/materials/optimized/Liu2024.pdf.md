## 1. Introduction

Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) is a highly heterogeneous disorder. Patients with OCD show tremendous intersubject heterogeneity in symptom presentations and responses to treatment (Alexander et al., 1986; McKay et al., 2004). For example, cases with OCD might exhibit non-overlapping symptom profiles (Mataix-Cols et al., 2005). To address the heterogeneity, patients with OCD are often stratified into subtypes based on clinical symptoms (Mataix-Cols et al., 2004). Despite the great success, symptom-based subtyping has some limitations, such as no universally accepted taxonomy (McKay et al., 2004), low sensitivity and handing sub-threshold symptoms (Okada et al., 2015). Moreover, clinical symptoms interact with biological substrates in complex ways and may change over the course of the disease (Haas et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023). A B S T R A C T

The high heterogeneity observed among patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) underscores the need to identify neurophysiological OCD subtypes to facilitate personalized diagnosis and treatment. In this study, our aim was to identify potential OCD subtypes based on individualized functional connectome abnormalities. We recruited a total of 99 patients with OCD and 104 healthy controls (HCs) matched for demographic characteristics. Individualized functional connectome abnormalities were obtained using normative models of functional connectivity strength (FCS) and used as features to unveil OCD subtypes. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the reproducibility and robustness of the clustering outcomes. Patients exhibited significant intersubject heterogeneity in individualized functional connectome abnormalities. Two subtypes with distinct patterns of FCS abnormalities relative to HCs were identified. Subtype 1 patients primarily exhibited significantly decreased FCS in regions including the frontal gyrus, insula, hippocampus, and precentral/postcentral gyrus, whereas subtype 2 patients demonstrated increased FCS in widespread brain regions. When all patients were combined, no significant differences were observed. Additionally, the identified subtypes showed significant differences in age of onset. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses confirmed the reproducibility of our subtyping results. In conclusion, the identified OCD subtypes shed new light on the taxonomy and neurophysiological heterogeneity of OCD.

Using modern neuroimaging methods like resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), researchers have consistently linked the etiology of psychiatric disorders, including OCD, to imbalances in large-scale interconnected brain networks (Fornito et al., 2015; Han et al., 2023b). Most neuroimaging studies still rely on traditional case-control approaches, which only capture effects observable at the group level. It ' s becoming increasingly clear that such approaches may miss neuroimaging characteristics unique to individuals (Lv et al., 2021a; Marquand et al., 2016; Wolfers et al., 2018). To address this heterogeneity, the normative model has been proposed (Marquand et al., 2016). This approach infers individualized neuroimaging abnormalities by assessing deviations from the normal distribution (Marquand et al., 2016). Using the normative model, neuroimaging studies have successfully identified subject-level structural brain alterations in mental disorders such as autism, schizophrenia, and depression (Han et al., 2023a; Wolfers and Beckmann, 2020; Wolfers et al., 2018; Zabihi et al., 2019). These individual deviations from the normative model can inform neurobiological subtypes with distinct clinical manifestations in psychiatric disorders (Sun et al., 2023).

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive investigation into the neurobiological heterogeneity and subtypes of OCD using a dataset comprising 99 patients with OCD and 104 healthy controls. We obtained individualized functional connectome abnormalities, measured with functional connectivity strength (FCS), using the normative model framework. Subsequently, we identified potential subtypes based on individualized FCS abnormalities as features. Finally, we evaluated differences in clinical variables and functional connectomes between the identified OCD subtypes.

## 2. Methods

## 2.1. Participants

This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and obtained approval from the research ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Prior to participating, each individual provided informed consent.

Atotal of ninety-eight first-episode and untreated patients diagnosed with OCD were recruited from outpatient services within the Department of Psychiatry at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Diagnosis was established independently by two experienced psychiatrists in accordance with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria for OCD. The severity of symptoms was assessed using the Yale -Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) (Goodman et al., 1989). Exclusion criteria ensured that none of the patients had a history of neurological disorders, brain trauma, concurrent mental or psychotic disorders, or first-degree relatives with similar conditions.

Healthy controls (HCs, n = 104) were enlisted from the community via poster advertisements. All participants were of Han Chinese ethnicity, right-handed, and underwent screening to ensure they met specified exclusion criteria. These criteria included abstaining from medications such as anesthetics and analgesics within the preceding month, no history of substance abuse, brain tumors, traumatic brain injury, surgeries, or organic body diseases, as well as the absence of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, or hypertension. Additionally, individuals with contraindications for MRI scanning or other structural brain abnormalities were excluded from the study.

## 2.2. Scan acquisition

During the scan, participants were instructed to recline, close their eyes, and remain at rest without focusing on any specific thoughts, while ensuring they did not drift into sleep. A total of 242 contiguous wholebrain resting-state functional images were acquired over an 8-min period using gradient echo echo-planar-imaging (GRE-EPI) sequences. The imaging parameters included 32 slices, a repetition time (TR) of

2000 milliseconds and an echo time (TE) of 30 milliseconds, a flip angle of 90 ◦ , a field of view (FOV) measuring 220 × 220 mm, a slice thickness of 3 mm with a 1 mm gap between slices, and a voxel size of 3.4 × 3.4 × 4 mm 3 .

## 2.3. Imaging data processing

The functional images underwent preprocessing using Data Processing &amp; Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI, http://rfmri.org/dpabi) (Dong et al., 2023a; Yan et al., 2016). This involved several steps: removal of the first 10 volumes, slice timing correction, and realignment. Subsequently, the images were normalized to the standard EPI template, resampled into a voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm 3 . Subjects were excluded if their translational or rotational displacement exceeded 3.0 mm or 3.0 ◦ . None of subjects was excluded according to this standard. The images were then smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 6 × 6 × 6 mm 3 and detrended to mitigate low-frequency drift. A band-pass filter (0.01 -0.1 Hz) was applied to remove high-frequency physiological noise. Friston 24 motion parameters (Satterthwaite et al., 2012), white matter signal and cerebrospinal fluid signal were regressed out as nuisance covariates. Specially, the global signal was not regressed in the current study for the reason that the variance of global signal along with its topography were found altered mental disease (Han et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2019a). Additionally, despiking was employed to replace outliers with the best estimate using a third-order spline fit, based on the detection of outliers using the median absolute deviation method, as implemented in 3dDespike (http://afni.nimh.nih.gov/afni) (Allen et al., 2014). The mean frame-wise displacement (FD) was also calculated to assess its effects on our results (see below)(Dong et al., 2023b; Han et al., 2020; Han et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023).

## 2.4. Functional connectivity strength analysis

Initially, a functional brain network was constructed for each participant. The network nodes were delineated based on a predefined functional parcellation (Fan et al., 2016). Connectivity within the network was estimated by computing Pearson ' s correlation coefficients between the time series of each pair of nodes, followed by Fisher ' s r-to-z transformation to enhance normality. Subsequently, the Functional Connectivity Strength (FCS) values for each brain region were calculated as the aggregate of connectivity between the given region and all other regions. It ' s noteworthy that our analysis was constrained to correlations surpassing a threshold of r = 0.2, aiming to exclude weak correlations potentially stemming from noise (Sun et al., 2023). Furthermore, whole-brain FCS values underwent z-score normalization (subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation) to ensure comparability across participants.

## 2.5. Individualized functional connectivity strength abnormalities

First, we evaluated the performance of the Gaussian process regression model in predicting FCS values within HCs through 10-fold crossvalidation. The standardized mean squared error (MSE) between the true FCS values and predicted values was computed to assess the model ' s accuracy (Marquand et al., 2016). Subsequently, for each brain region, a Gaussian process regression model was trained to estimate the normative range of FCS values as a function of age and sex within HCs. This model was then applied to each patient, yielding a predicted FCS value. A Z -score was calculated to quantify the deviation of predicted FCS value from the normal distribution. A positive Z-score indicated a higher FCS compared to HCs, while a negative Z-score indicated the opposite. This process resulted in a Z-score vector (246 × 1) for each patient, representing individualized FCS abnormalities.

To further explore the intersubject heterogeneity of extreme deviations, a spatial overlap map was generated. This map computed the

percentage of participants exhibiting extreme deviations, defined as Zscores exceeding ± 1.96 (thus surpassing the two-tailed 95% confidence interval), thereby delineating extreme positive or negative deviations from the normative model (Haas et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023).

## 2.6. Identifying OCD subtypes based on individualized functional connectivity strength abnormalities

We employed a data-driven k-means clustering algorithm to investigate potential subtypes within OCD. In this approach, individualized FCS abnormalities served as the clustering feature, with the squared Euclidean distance utilized as the distance metric. To determine the optimal number of subtypes, ranging from 2 to 10, we utilized silhouette values (Han et al., 2022b). For each candidate number of subtypes, the k-means algorithm was iterated 100 times to mitigate the risk of converging to local minima during the initialization of centroid positions (Allen et al., 2014).

## 2.7. Characterizing subtype-related imaging and clinical differences

Subsequently, we derived distinct patterns of FCS within each subtype and across all patients using a two-sample t -test while controlling for age, sex, and mean FD. Significance was established at p &lt; 0.05 after Bonferroni correction. Additionally, we explored demographic and clinical disparities among the identified subtypes, encompassing age, sex, educational level, symptom severity as measured by the total score of the Y-BOCS, illness duration, and age of onset. To assess whether head motion influenced the subtyping outcomes, we compared mean FD between the identified subtypes. This analysis aimed to ascertain the impact of motion artifacts on subtype differentiation.

## 2.8. Reproducibility analysis

A series of sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness and reproducibility of the clustering outcomes. Firstly, to evaluate the stability of our results and minimize the influence of small subsets of patients, we randomly selected 90% of the patients and performed k-means clustering on this subsample, following the same procedure as before. Subsequently, we calculated the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) between the subtyping results obtained from this subsample and those derived from the entire patient cohort. This process was repeated 1000 times to obtain a comprehensive assessment of the clustering consistency. Secondly, we validated our primary findings by employing another brain atlas with different resolutions, specifically the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas. The AAL atlas contains 90 cortical and subcortical regions (excluding the cerebellum) (TzourioMazoyer et al., 2002). Considering the important role of the cerebellum in OCD, we also validated our main clustering results using all brain regions in the AAL (including the cerebellum)(Xu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019b). This validation step ensured that our results were not dependent on the specific brain parcellation scheme used in the analysis, thus enhancing the generalizability of our findings.

## 3.2. Highly heterogenous individualized functional connectivity strength abnormalities in patients with OCD

The 10-fold cross-validation performed on HCs demonstrated a high level of generalizability in the fitting performance of normative models for FCS, as evidenced by a mean MSE close to 1 (99.94e GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 2 ± 5.84e GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 2 ). We also examined the distribution of Z scores across brain regions and subjects and found most of them fall between GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1.96 and 1.96. The spatial distribution of MSE is depicted in Fig. S1 and Z scores across brain regions and subjects is depicted in Fig. S2. The differential pattern of Z scores between patients with OCD and HCs was showed in Fig. S3.

Following confirmation of the normative model ' s performance, we applied the trained model derived from HCs to patients with OCD to ascertain individualized FCS abnormalities. Patients with OCD exhibited heterogeneous patterns of individualized FCS abnormalities. Specifically, 44.44% ( n = 44) of OCD patients demonstrated extreme negative deviations from the normative model in at least one brain region. These extreme negative deviations were predominantly observed in regions such as the precentral and postcentral gyrus, superior medial frontal gyrus, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, superior temporal gyrus, thalamus, insula, and hippocampus (Fig. 1). Additionally, 21.21% ( n = 21) of OCD patients displayed extreme positive deviations from the normative model in at least one brain region, spanning across various regions of the brain (Fig. 1). However, the percentage of patients exhibiting extreme deviations from the normative range in any single brain region was notably low, whether in positive or negative deviations (Fig. 1). These findings underscore the substantial heterogeneity in individualized FCS abnormalities among patients with OCD.

## 3.3. Individualized functional connectivity strength abnormalities identify two OCD subtypes

The K-means clustering analysis identified two distinct subtypes of OCD, each exhibiting contrasting FCS abnormalities compared to HCs. The silhouette values representing the clustering quality are illustrated in Fig. 2A. Specifically, patients belonging to subtype 1 (73.74%, n = 73) displayed significantly decreased FCS levels (Bonferroni correction corrected p &lt; 0.05) primarily localized in regions including the superior medial frontal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, temporal gyrus, insula, parahippocampal gyrus, hippocampus, precentral/postcentral gyrus, and middle occipital gyrus (Fig. 2B). Conversely, patients categorized as subtype 2 (26.26%, n = 26) demonstrated increased FCS (Bonferroni correction corrected p &lt; 0.05) across distributed brain regions, such as the superior medial frontal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, thalamus, striatum, and middle occipital gyrus (Fig. 2B). We also obtained extreme deviation maps for the two subtypes for comparison with the overall patient population (Fig. S4). Interestingly, when all patients with OCD were pooled together, no significant FCS abnormalities relative to HCs were observed (Bonferroni correction corrected p &lt; 0.05). This finding suggests that the differences in FCS patterns are obscured when considering the entire OCD cohort collectively.

## 4. Discussion

In this study, we have elucidated the neurophysiological heterogeneity and subtypes present among patients with OCD by uncovering individualized functional connectome abnormalities. Our findings underscore the substantial intersubject variability in the spatial distribution of functional connectome abnormalities among OCD patients.

Moreover, our results emphasize not only differences in the spatial distribution of these abnormalities but also significant variations in demographic and clinical characteristics between the two identified neurobiological subtypes of OCD. Collectively, our study delineates two distinct OCD subtypes and offers novel insights into the taxonomy of OCD, thereby paving the way for future personalized diagnosis and treatment strategies.

Patients with OCD demonstrated heterogenous in individualized functional connectome abnormalities. The use of normative models has become increasingly prevalent in addressing intersubject heterogeneity of structural neuroimaging metrics, such as cortical thickness and gray/ white matter morphology, particularly in heterogeneous disorders like mental disorders (Han et al., 2023a; Li et al., 2024; Marquand et al., 2016). Unlike traditional statistical models focusing on group-level effects, normative models do not necessitate assumptions about change trajectories and provide statistical inferences at the individual level. A recent study affirmed the efficacy of this model in predicting FCS across multiple datasets with large sample sizes (Sun et al., 2023). Prior to applying the normative model to patients, we first evaluated its feasibility in our own center ' s dataset. Consistent with previous findings, the normative model demonstrated reliable performance in predicting FCS values in HCs. Patients with OCD exhibited both increased and decreased individualized FCS in distributed brain regions, including the precentral and postcentral gyrus, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, thalamus, insula, and hippocampus, all of which have been implicated in the pathophysiology of OCD (Piras et al., 2015). For instance, previous studies have reported larger volumes in the precentral/postcentral gyrus among patients with OCD (Kim et al., 2020; Rus et al., 2017), along with documented functional connectivity alterations in this region (Lulu et al., 2023). Dysfunction of the precentral/postcentral gyrus has been linked to abnormalities in habit control, goal-directed behavior, and sensorimotor gating in OCD (Ahmari et al., 2012; Gillan et al., 2011; Hoenig et al., 2005; Jahanshahi et al., 2015; Voon et al., 2020). However, we found the overlap rates among patients win these regions were very low. However, we observed low overlap rates among patients within these regions, consistent with findings from studies on individualized gray matter morphological abnormalities in psychiatric disorders (Segal and Parkes, 2023; Wolfers and Beckmann, 2020; Wolfers et al., 2018; Zabihi et al., 2019). Taken together, these results suggest that alterations in FCS represent an important neuropathological feature of OCD, indicating the presence of multiple subtypes within the disorder.

Individualized functional connectome abnormalities uncover two OCD subtypes with opposite differential patterns. Patients with subtype 1 mainly showed decreased FCS in the frontal gyrus, insula, hippocampus and precentral/postcentral gyrus while patients with subtype 2 demonstrated increased FCS in widespread brain regions. Previous studies investigating functional connectome abnormalities in OCD have reported inconsistent or even conflicting findings. For instance, Lv et al. identified increased functional connectivity in the thalamus and decreased functional connectivity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and precuneus (Lv et al., 2021b), while Shan et al. only observed decreased functional connectivity in the temporal gyrus (Shan et al., 2019). Interestingly, in our study, we did not detect any significant differences in functional connectome when considering all patients as a single group. This incongruity among findings could potentially be attributed to the varying proportions of the two subtypes within the sample. In clinical practice, patients with OCD are usually further divided into various subtypes according to symptom profiles (e.g. checking and washing), disease courses (e.g. early-onset and late-onset) or treatment responses (normal and refractory) (Mataix-Cols et al., 2004). Subtypes based on symptomatology are found to distinctly response to treatment (Starcevic and Brakoulias, 2008). Although promising, symptom-based subtyping has some limitations as we mentioned before. Recent years, there a trend to identify subtypes from neuroimaging metrics. In our prior studies, we have also found similar subtypes with discrepancies in structural abnormalities in OCD and other psychiatric disorders (Han et al., 2023a; Han et al., 2022a). However, these relevant studies also suffer limitations. First, psychiatric disorders including OCD are brain connectome disorders (Fornito et al., 2015; Kaiser et al., 2015), while most of them just use structural or functional metrics as subtyping features, ignoring connectomes between brain regions (Ding et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023). Second, these studies identify subtypes directly using neuroimaging features but not diseaserelated neuroimaging features (differences from normal population). In this study, we unveiled subtypes from individualized FCS differences by leveraging a newly proposed method (normative model).As a result, we succeed in identifying two subtypes with opposite patterns of abnormal FCS compared with HCs.

Furthermore, the two identified subtypes exhibited significant differences in terms of age of onset, which is a crucial feature associated with clinical subtypes of OCD. The two critical stages of onset with the first manifests in late childhood and early adolesces (Anholt et al., 2014). Male patients with often have a younger age of onset and are associated with greater severity and chronicity of symptoms (Stewart et al., 2004). Early-onset patients likely have a stronger genetic than late-onset patients (Bolton et al., 2007) and often demonstrate differential responses to treatment (van Roessel et al., 2023) and distinct abnormal patterns of brain connectivity (Cao et al., 2022). For instance, early age of onset has been linked to a poorer response to serotonin reuptake inhibitors pharmacotherapy (van Roessel et al., 2023). In this study, we find the identified two subtypes with opposite patterns of FCS abnormalities. A recent study has showed that early-onset and late-onset patients have divergent direction of abnormal structural brain network with early-onset patients primarily showing higher structural integrity and late-onset patients showing lower structural integrity relative to healthy controls (Vriend et al., 2024). These results suggest that earlyonset and late-onset patients with OCD have divergent brain connectome abnormalities compared to HCs, providing new insights into the neuropathology of them. Taken together, the identified subtypes reconcile the inconsistencies observed in previous imaging studies and hold promise for future personalized diagnosis and treatment strategies in OCD.

The current study has several limitations that warrant further consideration. Firstly, our study was conducted using a cross-sectional sample, which raises questions about the stability and generalizability of the identified subtypes. Validation of our results should be pursued using an independent dataset to confirm the robustness of our findings. Secondly, the clinical information available for patients was limited, which restricted our ability to explore potential differences in other clinical aspects between the identified subtypes. Future studies should aim to gather more comprehensive clinical data to gain a deeper understanding of subtype characteristics. Thirdly, factors such as body mass index and alcohol/cigarette use were not adequately controlled for in our study. It is important for future research to assess the potential influence of these factors on our results to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the findings. Lastly, patients with OCD in our study had lower educational levels compared to HCs. However, we did not observe significant differences in educational level between the identified subtypes, suggesting that educational level may not have had a substantial impact on the clustering results. Nonetheless, further investigation into the potential effects of educational level on our findings would be valuable.
