## Neurodevelopmental model of schizophrenia revisited: similarity in individual deviation and idiosyncrasy from the normative model of whole-brain white matter tracts and shared brain-cognition covariation with ADHD and ASD

Yi-Ling Chien 1,7 , Hsiang-Yuan Lin 2,7 , Yu-Hung Tung 3,7 , Tzung-Jeng Hwang 1,4,5 , Chang-Le Chen 6 , Chi-Shin Wu 1 , Chi-Yung Shang 1 , Hai-Gwo Hwu 1 , Wen-Yih Isaac Tseng 4,5,6 ✉ , Chih-Min Liu 1 ✉ and Susan Shur-Fen Gau 1,4,5 ✉

© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature Limited 2022

The neurodevelopmental model of schizophrenia is supported by multi-level impairments shared among schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders. Despite schizophrenia and typical neurodevelopmental disorders, i.e., autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-de /uniFB01 cit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), as disorders of brain dysconnectivity, no study has ever elucidated whether whole-brain white matter (WM) tracts integrity alterations overlap or diverge between these three disorders. Moreover, whether the linked dimensions of cognition and brain metrics per the Research Domain Criteria framework cut across diagnostic boundaries remains unknown. We aimed to map deviations from normative ranges of whole-brain major WM tracts for individual patients to investigate the similarity and differences among schizophrenia (281 patients subgrouped into the /uniFB01 rst-episode, subchronic and chronic phases), ASD (175 patients), and ADHD (279 patients). Sex-speci /uniFB01 c WM tract normative development was modeled from diffusion spectrum imaging of 626 typically developing controls (5 -40 years). There were three signi /uniFB01 cant /uniFB01 ndings. First, the patterns of deviation and idiosyncrasy of WM tracts were similar between schizophrenia and ADHD alongside ASD, particularly at the earlier stages of schizophrenia relative to chronic stages. Second, using the WM deviation patterns as features, schizophrenia cannot be separated from neurodevelopmental disorders in the unsupervised machine learning algorithm. Lastly, the canonical correlation analysis showed schizophrenia, ADHD, and ASD shared linked cognitive dimensions driven by WM deviations. Together, our results provide new insights into the neurodevelopmental facet of schizophrenia and its brain basis. Individual ' s WM deviations may contribute to diverse arrays of cognitive function along a continuum with phenotypic expressions from typical neurodevelopmental disorders to schizophrenia.

## INTRODUCTION

Neurobiology underlying schizophrenia is elusive, limiting the development of effective treatment [1]. Among many hypotheses posited, emerging evidence from genetic, transcriptomic, epigenetic, and epidemiological data mainly converges on the neurodevelopmental origins of schizophrenia [2]. This neurodevelopmental model postulates that schizophrenic genetic risk compositions, alongside exogenous environmental input, subtly perturb early brain development. With these developmental insults, psychotic symptoms could develop later in late adolescence/early adulthood when maturational events impose increasing functional demands, leading to decompensation of the neural systems [2, 3]. This model may account for clinical heterogeneity given inter-individual differences in developmental risk factors [2]. Nonetheless, scarce neuroimaging studies have substantiated this heuristic speculation [2 -4].

The neurodevelopmental model considers schizophrenia as on a neurodevelopmental continuum [2], supported by the multifaceted relationships of schizophrenia with more typical, early-onset neurodevelopmental disorders, such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-de /uniFB01 cit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Speci /uniFB01 -cally, both schizophrenia and ASD feature similar impairments in social cognition [5]. A subset of individuals with schizophrenia shows a prior history of ADHD before the onset of psychotic symptoms [6]. ADHD or ASD is diagnosed in many children at risk for schizophrenia [7]. impairments in numerous domains of executive functions across clinical stages [1, 8, 9]. A prior genome-wide association study indicates signi /uniFB01 cant genetic correlations between schizophrenia and ASD or ADHD, respectively [10]. Moreover, schizophreniaassociated copy number variants show a pleiotropy associated with ADHD and ASD [2, 11]. A resting-state fMRI study [12] also suggests that global brain network organizations are highly similar across ASD, ADHD, and schizophrenia, with subtle differences in local network metrics associated with diagnostic divergence.

Nonetheless, some contrasting reports against the close links between schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders exist. For example, a recent study testing structural models of psychopathology at the genomic level suggests ASD and ADHD in the neurodevelopmental factor while schizophrenia in the thoughtproblems factor [13]. A secondary analysis from the ENIGMA consortium [14] has reported that the brain morphology is highly correlated between schizophrenia, major depressive, bipolar, and obsessive-compulsive disorders. Still, those in ASD and ADHD are independent of the common factor aggregating morphological alterations among these disorders. The inconsistency may be explained by the continuing brain and symptom changes at different stages of schizophrenia [1, 3, 4]. But these earlier studies did not consider the illness progression. Moreover, particularly for neuroimaging studies, brain maps of the ' average patient ' inferred from casecontrol differences might not be capable of capturing any particular individual, further resulting in heterogeneity across studies [15]. Therefore, normative modeling [15], combined with strati /uniFB01 cation based on disease stages of schizophrenia, is needed to better model individual variation in brain alterations and explain the relationships between schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders.

Based on recent endeavors in schizophrenia [16] and neurodevelopmental disorders [17], the current study leveraged normative modeling to further investigate similarity and distinctness in deviations (to the age/sex norm) of white matter (WM) property, alongside the extent of inter-individual variations ( ' idiosyncrasy ' ), among different stages of schizophrenia, ASD, and ADHD. WM was explicitly targeted because it is the brain structural backbone essential in restraining functional network organization [18] and gray matter coupling [19], given schizophrenia, ASD, and ADHD as disorders of brain dysconnectivity [1, 8, 9]. Patients with schizophrenia show stable patterns of diffuse de /uniFB01 cits in WM connections [20]. The speci /uniFB01 c dysconnectivity may precede symptoms and is associated with cognitive de /uniFB01 cits [21 -30]. Similarly, cumulative evidence supports that individuals with ADHD exhibit distributed patterns of reduced WM connectivity along association and projection pathways, as well as altered microstructural integrity of frontal, striatal, and cerebellar circuits that are critical to behavioral control and motor function [31, 32]. Several altered tracts, such as superior and inferior longitudinal fasciculi, cingulum, and corpus callosum, reported in schizophrenia and ADHD are also impaired in ASD [33 -35]. Moreover, given the knowledge of WM lifespan trajectories, the investigation of WM is proposed as the frontline lore, particularly to reconcile the con /uniFB02 icting evidence in heuristic neurodevelopmental and other (e.g., neurodegenerative) models of schizophrenia [4]. Moving beyond the categorical characterization, the linked dimensions of cognition and brain metrics per the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework [36], cutting across diagnostic boundaries may substantiate the neurodevelopmental facet of schizophrenia. Nonetheless, there has been no report using WM metrics on either categorical collations or dimensional brainbehavior relationships across schizophrenia, ADHD, and ASD.

Herein, we developed the normative model of WM microstructural property (generalized fractional anisotropy, GFA), as a function of age, across the lifespan using large-sampled single-site harmonized cross-sectional MRI data from more than 600 typically developing individuals (5 -40 yrs) [17]. This model established the normative ranges of variations in GFA, represented as functions of age and sex. Individual deviations from the norm, alongside variations in individual deviations, were then determined for around 300 individuals with schizophrenia, 300 with ADHD, and 200 with ASD. Further, we delineated linked dimensions of cognition and WM tract /uniFB01 ngerprints, as quanti /uniFB01 ed by the degree of deviation in each tract [36], re /uniFB02 ecting the potential shared neural correlates underlying cognitive impairments across schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders [1, 8, 9]. We hypothesized that the WM pro /uniFB01 les of deviance and idiosyncrasy in schizophrenia, especially in the earlier phase of the illness, would be similar to ASD and ADHD. However, as schizophrenia progressed from the /uniFB01 rst episode to the chronic phase (based on the strati /uniFB01 cation), the level of this similarity among schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders would decrease. We also hypothesized that there would exist different linked dimensions of cognitive function and WM deviation pro /uniFB01 les, cutting cross schizophrenia, and neurodevelopmental disorders.

## METHODS AND MATERIALS

## Participants

This study recruited 281 patients with schizophrenia (aged 16 -40 yrs), 175 children and adults with ASD (9 -35 yrs), 279 children and adults with ADHD (7 -38 yrs), and 626 typically developing controls (TDC) (Table 1 and STable 1 -2). The three patient groups were recruited from the Department of Psychiatry, National Taiwan University Hospital. The clinical diagnoses were made by senior psychiatrists per both DSM-IV [37] and DSM-5 [38] criteria of schizophrenia, ASD, and ADHD. The diagnosis of ASD was validated by the standard diagnostic assessment of the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised [39], while the ADHD diagnosis was con /uniFB01 rmed by the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia epidemiological version (K-SADS-E) [40, 41] and modi /uniFB01 ed adult ADHD supplement of the K-SADS-E for ages older than 18 [42, 43]. The schizophrenia cohort included patients in different stages per the duration-based de /uniFB01 nition [44], i.e., ' /uniFB01 rst-episode ' ( /uniFB01 rst diagnosed with duration of illness &lt;1 yr, n = 75, chlorpromazine equivalent dose 211.0 ± 223.6 mg/d), ' subchronic ' (1 -10 yrs of illness, n = 161, 268.6 ± 231.8 mg/d) and ' chronic ' ( ≥ 10 yrs, n = 45, 370.6 ± 256.1 mg/day) (STable 1 -2). All participants had full-scale IQ &gt; 70 based on Wechsler intelligence scores [45, 46]. None of the ASD or ADHD participants had any co-occurring psychotic disorders, and none of the schizophrenia participants had a childhood diagnosis of ASD or ADHD. We recruited TDC from the same community of the patient groups, either from schools or via advertisement, who then received the K-SADS-E or SADS [42, 47] interview to exclude any psychiatric diagnoses (Supplementary Methods).

Neurocognitive assessment . Neuropsychological functions were assessed by the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB) [17, 47 -49] (STable 3) and Wechsler intelligence scores [45, 46]. Among CANTAB, One-Touch Stockings of Cambridge, Rapid Visual Information Processing, Reaction Time, Spatial Working Memory, Motor Screening Task, Intra-Extra Dimensional Set Shift, and Paired Associates Learning were assessed. As for IQ pro /uniFB01 les, the subscales on Digit Span (forward, backward), Digit Symbol, Arithmetic, Information, Similarity, Block Design, and Working Memory were selected. (Supplementary Methods).

## Image acquisition and analysis

Participants received MRI scans on the same 3-Tesla Siemens TIM Trio scanner with a 32-channel phased-array head coil. The diffusion spectrum imaging (DSI) protocol was detailed elsewhere [17], with 102 diffusionencoding directions with a maximal b value of 4000 s/mm 2 , using a singleshot spin-echo echo-planar imaging sequence with twice-refocused diffusion-sensitive gradients to reduce distortion caused by eddy currents [50]. Before analysis, the rigorous quality control of MRI data was implemented by excluding those with low signal-noise ratio (SNR) or high signal-dropout counts (SDC), a proxy of in-scanner motion levels. As a result, SNR and SDC were comparable between these groups. The diffusion pro /uniFB01 les of each person included GFA of 45 major WM tracts ([17]; STable 4), prede /uniFB01 ned on an open-sourced DSI template [51] through deterministic streamline-based tractography [52]. GFA, providing a broad measure of WM axonal health in the DSI framework, was computed via mean-apparent propagator models [53] and sampled in the native space along each WM tract. ## Statistical analysis, standardized normative models

Individualized tract-deviation pro /uniFB01 les comprised GFA Z -scores of each WM tract derived from the sex-speci /uniFB01 c normative age models, constructed from 626 TDCs (Male/Female: 376/250; age range 5 -40, 20.16 ± 8.5 yrs) [17]. A moving average approach was used to obtain the unbiased mean and variance estimates for the GFA metric in each age range. Next, GFA was transformed into Z -scores, yielding individualized tract-alteration pro /uniFB01 les. Each group ' s distributions of GFA Z -scores of WM tracts are presented in STable 5. The derived Z -scores of clinical groups and TDC were not correlated with age, SNR, or in-scanner motion levels (SFig. 1).

Within the schizophrenia subgroups, the effect sizes of one-sample t -tests on GFA Z -scores of each WM tract for each subgroup re /uniFB02 ect the deviation extent (relative to the TDC norm) of this speci /uniFB01 c tract in this particular group. When the FDR-corrected alpha of a one-sample test on a speci /uniFB01 c tract of a schizophrenia subgroup is &lt;0.05, it denotes that this tract signi /uniFB01 cantly deviates from the norm in this subgroup (SFig. 2). We used the F -ratio (a tract GFA Z -score variance of a subgroup divided by this tract variance of the TDC norm) of the Ftest (of equality of variances) [54] to estimate idiosyncrasy pro /uniFB01 les of WM tracts for each subgroup [17]. The idiosyncrasy of a tract of a subgroup indicates the variance of this tract ' s GFA Z -score distribution in this subgroup relative to that in the norm (SFig. 3).

To investigate the categorical relationships between schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders, between-group similarity and difference in the extents of GFA deviation, alongside idiosyncrasy, were evaluated. Strati /uniFB01 ed analyses of /uniFB01 rst-episode schizophrenia (FES), subchronic, and chronic schizophrenia were implemented to specify whether people in the early course of schizophrenia present WM alterations more similar to neurodevelopmental disorders than patients with later-stage schizophrenia. Betweengroup differences (e.g., schizophrenia vs. ASD, schizophrenia vs. ADHD, etc.) of deviation and idiosyncrasy based on GFA Z -scores were evaluated with a twosample t -test and F -test (schizophrenia ' s WM variances divided by neurodevelopmental ' s WM variances), respectively. Although the normative modeling has taken the age and sex effects into account when yielding WM deviation pro /uniFB01 les, we performed the propensity score weights matching for age [55] between schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders as the main results. We repeated this categorical analysis based on the entire sample of neurodevelopmental disorders to complement the main /uniFB01 ndings.

Spearman ' s correlation ( ρ ) of the WM tract deviation and idiosyncrasy pro /uniFB01 les were calculated to explore between-group similarity further. High correlations of WM tract pro /uniFB01 les between groups indicate similarity regardless of absolute values. The false-discovery rate was employed to correct for multiple comparisons of 45 WM tracts for each clinical group of interest, separately for deviation, idiosyncrasy, and correlation metrics. This between-diagnosis correlation was also carried out separately between each stage of schizophrenia and their age-matched counterparts of ASD or ADHD. The details regarding data distributions and the rationale for the statistical test selection are described elsewhere [17].

## Clustering

We conducted unsupervised machine learning based on the individual ' s brain features to investigate whether schizophrenia, ASD, or ADHD (using the entire sample) could be clustered into distinct groups that correspond to clinical diagnoses or hidden features. Both Ward ' s hierarchical clustering and k-means clustering were applied to the /uniFB01 rst 12 components extracted from the GFA Z -score pro /uniFB01 les of each individual using the principal component analysis Fig. 3A). These 12 components explained at least 1% of the variation separately; about 86% of the variance was explained by these components together. The optimal number of clustering was de /uniFB01 ned by Gap statistics [56], whereby the largest jump in within-cluster distance relative to a null reference distribution occurred.

## Linked dimensions of cognition and WM

Echoing the RDoC framework that mental disorders could be addressed as brain circuit disorders breaking free of categorical diagnostic constraints [36], we investigated underlying brain-cognition relationships cutting across schizophrenia, ASD, and ADHD (using the entire sample). We computed two canonical correlation analyses (CCA) separately for CANTAB and IQ because some participants only had either measure. These cognitive measures extensively cover different domains of cognitive and executive functions. The CCA [57] identi /uniFB01 es multiple orthogonal ' modes ' of population

covariation between GFA Z-scores and cognitive measures [57]. Each mode consists of canonical variables of GFA-Z and cognitive measures that maximally correlate. Each original variable ' s relative contributions in each mode are re /uniFB02 ected by the canonical loadings (Fig. 4), the linear correlations ( r ) between the canonical variates, and the original variables in this set [17]. Weused100,000 permutations of the rows of one matrix relative to the other to estimate statistical signi /uniFB01 cance at FWE-corrected alpha &lt;0.05.

Using the feature of the /uniFB01 rst 12 components extracted from the principal component analysis (Fig. 3A), both k-means and Ward ' s clustering algorithms yielded that the optimal cluster number was 1 based on Gap statistics (Fig. 3B, C). ## DISCUSSION

As the /uniFB01 rst study to investigate the similarity and difference between schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders based on the whole-brain WM tracts deviation to the age norm, we found, generally, the similarity of deviance and idiosyncrasy of WM tracts between early-stage schizophrenia and ADHD or ASD, respectively, reduced as the disease progressed to subchronic or chronic schizophrenia. Using the WM deviation patterns as features, schizophrenia cannot be separated from neurodevelopmental disorders in the unsupervised machine learning algorithm. Moreover, schizophrenia, ADHD, and ASD shared linked cognitive dimensions driven by WM deviations. Together, our results provide novel evidence to support the neurodevelopmental model, whereby schizophrenia lies on a neurodevelopmental continuum [2].

The correlation analysis showed that the deviance of WM tracts in FES and subchronic schizophrenia was similar to that in ADHD and ASD. The effect sizes of diagnostic differences between chronic schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders were generally more prominent than the differences between earlier stages of schizophrenia and ASD/ADHD. These /uniFB01 ndings suggest that the whole-brain WM microstructural composition in the earlier stages of schizophrenia is closer to that of neurodevelopmental disorders. Unsupervised machine learning algorithms also could not differentiate schizophrenia from neurodevelopmental disorders based on the WM deviation features. Together, these /uniFB01 ndings provide evidence that brain features of schizophrenia are mainly similar to neurodevelopmental disorders, consistent with a prior resting-state fMRI study [12]. In other words, schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders shared altered composition of WM tracts in terms of deviation from the norm to some extent. The levels of similarity (particularly to ASD) vary depending on the stages of schizophrenia. Speci /uniFB01 cally, relative to the neurodevelopmental disorders, FES had the least differences in WM deviation; subchronic schizophrenia showed the most diffuse WM tracts differences; chronic schizophrenia had intermediate numbers of the involved WM tracts, while the effect size of chronic schizophrenia-ADHD/ASD differences was generally the largest.

This ' neurodevelopmental-ness ' might be driven by some shared common genetic risk among these disorders [2, 10, 11]. The /uniFB01 nding of the loss of neurodevelopmental similarity in chronic schizophrenia might re /uniFB02 ect another notion in the neurodevelopmental model [2] that the progressive deterioration of connectome pathology in schizophrenia [63] drives varying phenotypic expression. Nonetheless, the WM idiosyncrasy in /uniFB01 rst-episode and chronic schizophrenia was better dimensionally correlated with that of ASD than that of ADHD. Notably, the categorical comparisons showed that the WM microstructural composition in ASD/ADHD was less idiosyncratic than that in schizophrenia as the illness progressed (STable 6C). This dimensional vs. categorical dissonance between neurodevelopmental disorders-schizophrenia comparison might indicate that the idiosyncratic distribution of each WM tract of neurodevelopmental disorders and schizophrenia is still largely similar, but the extent is more varying in schizophrenia at the later stages, consistent with the overall picture of the current /uniFB01 ndings. Notably, neurodevelopmental disorders share several domains of social impairment and executive dysfunction with schizophrenia at later stages [1, 8, 9]. This phenotype similarity may be attributable to other neuropathology rather than WM changes alone.

Our /uniFB01 ndings suggest that speci /uniFB01 c /uniFB01 ber bundles are selectively unique in schizophrenia, particularly at the later stage, from neurodevelopmental disorders, including the thalamic radiations, frontostriatal, hippocampal connections (fornix and cingulumhippocampal part), long association /uniFB01 bers interconnecting frontal and primary sensory processing regions, e.g., IFOF. These WM tracts are separately involved in sensory integration [64] and neurocognitive functions, such as executive functions [65, 66], and reward prediction errors [67, 68]. These hippocampal-prefrontalstriatal and sensory relay-integration neurocircuits also play a prominent role in information processing and involve the aberrant attribution of salience to irrelevant sensory stimuli and internal representations, leading to aberrant predictive coding [68]. Thus, alterations of this set of WM tracts might constitute distinct neural correlates that separate schizophrenia from typical neurodevelopmental disorders. This speculation awaits corroboration from longitudinal studies.

This study has several limitations. First, psychotropic medications ' effect on the current /uniFB01 ndings is unknown. Still, it has less impact on our neurodevelopmental conclusion because of more similar WM deviation patterns and idiosyncrasy in earlier schizophrenia and ASD/ADHD. Second, the number of psychotic relapses may also in /uniFB02 uence brain structures [70] in addition to illness duration. Third, some children with ADHD or ASD were under the common age of schizophrenia onset. Whether these children would develop co-occurring schizophrenia later in life was unknown. Moreover, the analysis using the propensity score weights matching for age also indirectly addressed this concern by showing similar patterns to the main /uniFB01 ndings. This study provides new insights into the neurodevelopmental facet of schizophrenia and its brain basis.
