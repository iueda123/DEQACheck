## 1. Background

In  contemporary diagnostic  practices,  individuals  presenting  with psychiatric symptom profiles are typically assigned distinct clinical diagnoses. ## A B S T R A C T

Psychiatric disorders exhibit a shared neuropathology, yet the diverse presentations among patients necessitate the identification of transdiagnostic subtypes to enhance diagnostic and treatment strategies. This study aims to unveil  potential  transdiagnostic  subtypes  based  on  personalized  gray  matter  morphological  abnormalities.  A total of 496 patients with psychiatric disorders and 255 healthy controls (HCs) from three distinct datasets (one for discovery and two for validation) were enrolled. Individualized gray matter morphological abnormalities were determined using normative modeling to identify transdiagnostic subtypes. In the discovery dataset, two transdiagnostic subtypes with contrasting patterns of structural abnormalities compared to HCs were identified. Reproducibility and generalizability analyses  demonstrated that these  subtypes could be generalized to  new patients and even to new disorders in the validation datasets. These subtypes were characterized by distinct disease epicenters. The gray matter abnormal pattern in subtype 1 was mainly linked to excitatory receptors, whereas subtype 2 showed a predominant association with inhibitory receptors. Furthermore, we observed that the gray matter abnormal pattern in subtype 2 was correlated with transcriptional profiles of inflammationrelated genes, while subtype 1 did not show this association. Our findings reveal two robust transdiagnostic biotypes, offering novel insights into psychiatric nosology.

Even within a single diagnosis, neuroimaging studies have reported reduced, increased, or even no differential gray matter volumes (L ´ azaro et al., 2011; Okasha et al., 2000). These results suggest the existence of distinct neuroanatomical subtypes in psychiatric disorders. Indeed, studies have identified subtypes with opposing patterns of structural abnormalities within a single diagnosis, such  as schizophrenia and  obsessivecompulsive  disorder  (Chand  et  al.,  2020;  Han  et  al.,  2022b).  To address this heterogeneity, normative modeling has been proposed to characterize structural abnormalities at the individual level by assessing deviations from the normal distribution (Marquand et al., 2016). Using these  individualized  structural  deviations  as  features,  a  recent  study successfully identified two depression biotypes with distinct patterns of cortical thinning and treatment responses (Li et al., 2024). However, whether individualized structural deviations could reveal transdiagnostic subtypes remains unknown.

Recent neuroimaging studies suggest that neuropsychiatric disorders target intrinsic brain networks. Pathological perturbations, measured by structural  abnormalities,  typically  originate  in  focal  brain  regions (known  as  'disease  epicenters ' )  and  spread  to  other  brain  regions following  normal  brain  network  architecture,  supporting  a  networkbased  spreading  hypothesis  (Shafiei  et  al.,  2020b;  Wannan  et  al., 2019; Yau et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2012). A recent study further proposes that regional structural abnormalities could be inferred from topologically  connected  neighbors  (Shafiei  et  al.,  2020a).  Psychiatric disorders and their subtypes are characterized by distinct disease epicenters, reflecting their diverse biological underpinnings and progression trajectories (Hansen et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024). Moreover, with the availability  of  whole-brain  transcriptional  atlas  (Hawrylycz  et  al., 2012a) and neurotransmitter receptors/transporters atlas (Hansen and Shafiei, 2022b), researchers have investigated the transcriptional and molecular substrates underlying structural and functional abnormalities in psychiatric disorders. For instance, the inflammation hypothesis in psychiatric  disorders  posits  that  an  elevated  inflammatory  response induced by factors such as stress exerts direct neurotoxic effects on the brain,  leading  to  structural  and  functional  changes  in  patients  (Berk et al., 2013; Eisenberger et al., 2010; Han and Ham, 2021; Troubat et al., 2021). In this context, a prior study found that transcriptional profiles of inflammation-related  genes  are  associated  with  cortical  thickness decrease in schizophrenia (Cui et al., 2023). Additionally, neurotransmitter dysfunction contributes to cortical abnormalities in psychiatric disorders (Hansen et al., 2022). Investigating the transcriptional and molecular bases  of  structural  abnormalities  helps  bridge  the  gap  between neuroimaging findings and biological mechanisms in psychiatric disorders.

In this study, we aimed to identify distinct transdiagnostic subtypes based  on  individualized  gray  matter  morphological  abnormalities compared to normal expectations. We included a total of 496 patients with psychiatric disorders and 255 healthy controls from three independent datasets (one discovery dataset and two validation datasets). First,  we  derived  individualized  deviation  maps  (individualized  gray matter  morphological  abnormalities)  using  normative  modeling  and used  them  to  determine  transdiagnostic  subtypes  in  the  discovery dataset. We then conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to examine the  reproducibility  and  generalizability  of  the  identified  subtypes  in unseen patients and even in unseen disorders in the validation datasets. Subsequently, we investigated the disease epicenters of the identified subtypes. To gain deeper insights into the molecular and genetic underpinnings of the subtypes, we further investigated the associations between structural abnormalities in these subtypes and the distribution of neurotransmitter receptors/transporters, as well as the transcriptional profiles of inflammation-related genes. The workflow of this study is shown in Fig. 1.

## 2.2. Subtyping analysis based on individualized gray matter morphological abnormalities

First,  we  estimated  individualized  gray  matter  morphological  abnormalities using the normative modeling. Following methods used in previous  studies  (Han  et  al.,  2023a;  Marquand  et  al.,  2016;  Wolfers et al., 2018), we trained a Gaussian process regression model to estimate the normative range of regional gray matter volumes (GMVs) based on age and sex in HCs. This trained model was then applied to each patient, generating predicted GMVs. Subsequently, Z scores (representing individualized abnormalities) were calculated to quantify deviations of the predicted GMVs from normal distributions. Positive Z scores indicated higher GMVs in patients compared to HCs, and vice versa. This process resulted in a Z -score vector (246 × 1) for each patient, representing their individualized gray matter morphological abnormalities.

Next,  we  used  the  k-means  algorithm  to  identify  transdiagnostic subtypes, with the Z scores serving as features and the squared Euclidean distance  as  the  distance  metric.  The  optimal  number  of  subtypes, ranging from 2 to 10, was determined using silhouette values (Han et al., 2022a).  For  each  number,  the  k-means  algorithm  was  repeated  100 times to avoid local minima during the initialization of centroid positions (Allen et al., 2014). Subsequently, for each subtype, we examined the  gray matter morphological abnormalities relative to HCs using a two-sample t -test, with sex, age, and total intracranial volume (TIV) as covariates.

## 2.3. Reproducibility and generalizability analysis

A series of reproducibility and generalizability analyses were conducted to assess the reproducibility and generalizability of the clustering outcomes. Firstly, to ensure that our results were not influenced by a small subset of patients, we randomly selected 90 % of the patients and performed k-means clustering on this subsample, as done previously. We then calculated the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) between the subtyping results based on this subsample and those based on all patients. This process was repeated 1000 times. Secondly, we validated the subtyping results  using  a  different  clustering  algorithm  called  heterogeneity through  discriminative  analysis  (HYDRA),  which  uses  regional  gray matter volumes (GMVs) as features. HYDRA is a semi-supervised method that identifies distinct subtypes in psychiatric disorders based on differences  between  patients  and  HCs  rather  than  clustering  patients directly  (Varol  et  al.,  2017).  It  adaptively  determines  the  optimal number of subtypes based on the ARI, which quantifies the similarity between  clustering  results  of  cross-validation  (Varol  et  al.,  2017). Thirdly, we assessed whether the identified subtypes could be replicated in independent datasets. We used the cluster centroids of the two subtypes  to  infer  subtype  labels  for  patients  with  schizophrenia  from another independent dataset. Each patient was assigned to one of the two subtypes based on its Euclidean distance to the cluster centroids. We then calculated the ARI between the predicted subtype labels and the true labels obtained using the aforementioned procedures. Furthermore, although  the  subtypes  were  based  on  patients  with  schizophrenia, ADHD, and BD, we investigated whether they could inform subtypes in unseen psychiatric disorders, such as depression. In another dataset of depression,  we  predicted  subtype  labels  for  patients  based  on  their Euclidean distance to the cluster centroids of the two identified subtypes. We then calculated the ARI between the predicted subtype labels and the true labels obtained using the aforementioned procedures.

## 2.4. Disease epicenter mapping for each subtype

Neuroimaging research has shed light on the impact of psychiatric disorders on brain networks. Gray matter morphological abnormalities, signaling  pathological  progression,  are  bounded  by  the  underlying normal brain network (Shafiei et al., 2020a; Wannan et al., 2019).

In our study, we aimed to investigate whether structural abnormalities of the identified subtypes were also influenced by the normal brain network, as assessed through the structural covariance network. This network delineates the coordination of regional volumes among various brain  regions,  potentially  reflecting  shared  developmental  pathways (Alexander-Bloch et al., 2013a; Han et al., 2023c; Yun et al., 2015) and its dysfunction was implicated in the pathology of depression (Kaiser et al., 2015; Lima-Ojeda et al., 2018; Sotiras et al., 2017; Yun et al., 2020). Importantly, previous research has indicated that the structural covariance  network  exhibits  a  stronger  association  with  gray  matter morphological abnormalities in depression compared to the functional network (Han et al., 2023b).

To construct the normal structural covariance network, we utilized another  extensive  dataset  from  a  single  site  (SALD),  comprising  492 healthy individuals aged 19 -80 years. Further details about this dataset are  available  elsewhere  (Wei  et  al.,  2018).  The  normal  structural covariance network was established by computing pairwise correlation coefficients of regional gray matter volumes across subjects, resulting in a 246 × 246 structural covariance (SC) network where negative correlations were adjusted to zero (Dong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). We assessed the relationship between the SC network and the gray matter morphological abnormalities of the identified subtypes, employing methodologies established in prior studies (Li et al., 2023; Shafiei et al., 2020a). ## 2.5. Association between neurotransmitter receptors/transporters with gray matter morphological abnormalities of the identified subtypes

Then, we investigated the association between neurotransmitter receptors/transporters profiles and gray matter gray matter morphological abnormalities of the identified subtypes.  Neurotransmitter receptors/ transporters  were  obtained  from  PET-derived  neurotransmitter  receptors/transporters atlas collected and shared by Hansen and Shafiei (2022b). The neurotransmitter receptors/transporters include serotonin (5HT1A  (Savli et al., 2012), 5HT1B  (Baldassarri et al., 2018; Gallezot et al., 2010; Matuskey et al., 2014; Murrough et al., 2011a; Murrough et al., 2011b; Saricicek et al., 2015; Savli et al., 2012), 5HT2A (Beliveau and Ganz, 2017), 5HT4 (Beliveau and Ganz, 2017), 5HT6 (Radhakrishnan  et  al.,  2020;  Radhakrishnan  et  al.,  2018),  5HTT (Beliveau and Ganz, 2017)), norepinephrine ( α 4 β 2  (Baldassarri et al., 2018;  Hillmer  et  al.,  2016),  M1 (Naganawa  et  al.,  2021),  VAChT (Aghourian  et  al.,  2017;  Bedard  et  al.,  2019)),  cannabinoid  (CB1 (D ' Souza et al., 2016; Neumeister et al., 2012; Normandin et al., 2015; Ranganathan  et  al.,  2016)),  dopamine  (D1 (Kaller  et  al.,  2017),  D2 (Sandiego et al., 2015; Slifstein et al., 2015; Smith et al., 2019; Zakiniaeiz et al., 2019), DAT (Dukart et al., 2018)), GABA (GABAa (N ø rgaard et al., 2021)), histamine (H3 (Gallezot et al., 2017)), glutamate (mGluR5 (DuBois et al., 2016; Smart et al., 2019), NMDA (Galovic et al., 2021; McGinnity et al., 2014)), opioid (MOR (Kantonen et al., 2020)), and norepinephrine (NET (Belfort-DeAguiar et al., 2018; Ding et al., 2010; Li et al., 2014; Sanchez-Rangel et al., 2020)). PET images were averaged across participants within each study, normalized to the MNI-ICBM 152 non-linear 2009 template, and then parcellated into 246 brain regions. The  average  regional  neurotransmitter  receptor/transporter  densities were then Z -scored (Hansen and Shafiei, 2022a, 2022b). For each subtype,  we  constructed  a  multilinear  model  between  neurotransmitter receptors/transporters profiles and gray matter differential pattern. The significance  of  these  multilinear  models  was  evaluated  through  permutation testing (10,000 times), false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Subsequently, to better understand the relative importance of each predictor (in this case, neurotransmitter receptors/transporters profiles) in contributing to the model ' s overall predictive power, we conducted a dominance  analysis  for  each  multilinear  model.  Dominance  analysis helps determine how each predictor impacts the model ' s fit by considering all possible predictor combinations within the model (Budescu and David, 1993). This is achieved by applying the multilinear model to every potential subset of predictors, resulting in 2 n -1 subset models for a model with n predictors. In our study, we utilized the concept of total dominance to gauge each predictor ' s relative significance (Budescu and David, 1993; Hansen and Shafiei, 2022a). Total dominance is calculated as  the  mean  increase  in  explained  variance  (R 2 )  that  each  predictor contributes across all subset models in which it is included (Azen and Budescu, 2003; Budescu and David, 1993).

## 2.6. Association between gray matter morphological abnormalities of the identified subtypes with transcriptional profiles of genes of interest

Finally, we delved into the potential transcriptional underpinnings of gray matter morphological irregularities observed within each subtype. Our aim was to ascertain whether these abnormalities were correlated with gene expression patterns associated with inflammation. To achieve this,  we  selected  fourteen inflammation-related genes based on prior literature (Keller et al., 2014). Transcriptional data for these genes were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) (http://human.brai nmap.org/) (Hawrylycz et al., 2012b). The raw expression data underwent processing using the AHBA pipeline (Dong et al., 2023; Hawrylycz et al., 2012b), involving averaging across six donors and normalization to  z-scores  across  246  brain  regions.  Consequently,  the  average expression levels of inflammation-related genes were consolidated into a

246 × 1 expression data vector. We then computed Pearson ' s correlation coefficients between the gray matter morphological abnormalities for each  subtype  and  the  transcriptional  profiles  of  the  selected  genes. Statistical significance was set at p &lt; 0.05 after FDR correction.

## 3.2. Two transdiagnostic subtypes manifesting opposite gray matter morphological abnormalities are identified

In our discovery dataset, analysis of subtyping results (illustrated in Fig. 2A) revealed the presence of two distinct transdiagnostic subtypes characterized by individualized gray matter morphological abnormalities. Specifically, subtype 1 encompassed 70 patients (schizophrenia, n = 14; BD, n = 35; ADHD, n = 31), while subtype 2 included 44 patients (schizophrenia, n = 23; BD, n = 15; ADHD, n = 6). Disorder composition for each subtype was depicted in Fig. 2B.

These  identified  subtypes  exhibited  contrasting  patterns  of  gray matter morphological abnormalities compared to HCs, as illustrated in Fig. 2C. Subtype 2 demonstrated widespread reductions  in  GMV  across  various  brain  regions,  whereas  subtype  1 displayed predominantly increased GMV.

## 3.3. Reproducibility and generalizability analysis results

A series of analyses were conducted to validate the reproducibility and generalizability of our subtyping results. This  finding  mitigated  concerns  regarding  potential  biases introduced by a few patients. Secondly, the optimal number of subtypes as determined by HYDRA remained consistent at 2 when ARI values peaked (Fig. S2). Moreover, the ARI between subtyping results obtained using HYDRA and those reported by us was 0.42. Thirdly, within the validation dataset 1, individualized gray matter morphological abnormalities also revealed two distinct subtypes of schizophrenia exhibiting differential  patterns  relative  to  healthy  controls  (uncorrected  results displayed in Fig. 3, and FDR-corrected results in Fig. S3). Fourthly, we investigated whether these identified subtypes could extend to other psychiatric disorders such as depression. The  ARI  between  true subtyping  results  and  those  inferred  by  the  cluster  centroids  of  the identified transdiagnostic subtypes obtained using the discovery dataset was 0.30. These results underscored the generalizability of the identified transdiagnostic subtypes to unseen psychiatric disorders.

3.5. Gray matter morphological abnormalities of transdiagnostic subtypes exhibit distinct associations with neurotransmitter receptors/transporters

Next,  we  delved  into  exploring  the  connections  between  neurotransmitter receptors/transporters and gray matter morphological abnormalities  within  transdiagnostic  subtypes.  This  was  achieved  by constructing two multilinear models that accounted for the spatial distributions of receptors/transporters and the differential patterns of gray matter morphological abnormalities in each subtype. The goodness-offit  of  these  models  (adjusted  R 2 )  was  0.16  (F-statistic  (246,226) = 3.41) for subtype 1 and 0.59 (F-statistic (246,226) = 19.70) for subtype 2, indicating strong model performance (see Fig. 5A). Notably, all FDRcorrected permutation p -values were &lt; 0.001. Furthermore, our analysis utilizing  dominance analysis revealed key neurotransmitter receptors associated with each subtype. Specifically, 5HT1A emerged as significant for subtype 1, whereas mGluR5 was identified as crucial for subtype 2 (see Fig. 5B).

Moreover, we categorized the receptors into excitatory and inhibitory types and computed their cumulative contributions by summing the total  dominance  values  to  understand  their  impact  on  gray  matter morphological abnormalities within each subtype. Our findings indicated  that  excitatory  receptors  predominantly  influenced  the  gray matter morphological abnormalities of subtype 1, whereas inhibitory receptors played a more prominent role in shaping the abnormalities observed in subtype 2 (see Fig. 5C).

## 4. Discussion

Using subject-level gray matter volume deviations as features, we successfully identified two robust transdiagnostic subtypes. These subtypes  demonstrated  resilience  across  different  subtyping  algorithms, were replicated  in  independent  datasets,  and  extended  to  previously unseen psychiatric disorders, thereby affirming their reproducibility and generalizability. Notably, they exhibited opposite differential patterns relative  to  healthy  controls  (HCs)  and  distinct  structural  covariance network-informed disease epicenters. Moreover, we observed that gray matter morphological abnormalities in these subtypes were distinctly associated  with  neurotransmitter  receptors/transporters.  Specifically, the abnormal pattern in subtype 1 was predominantly linked to excitatory  receptors,  while  that  in  subtype  2  was  mainly  associated  with inhibitory  receptors,  thereby  providing  insights  into  the  underlying molecular mechanisms of these subtypes. Additionally, we found that the gray matter differential pattern in subtype 2 correlated with transcriptional profiles of inflammation-related genes, whereas such correlation  was  not  evident  in  subtype  1.  This  highlights  the  complex interplay between neurobiological processes and molecular pathways in psychiatric  disorders.  Overall,  our  findings  unveil  two  reproducible transdiagnostic subtypes characterized by distinct gray matter morphological differential patterns and underlying transcriptional and molecular bases. These insights offer valuable contributions to psychiatric nosology, furthering our understanding of the heterogeneous nature of psychiatric disorders.

Psychiatric disorders exhibit significant heterogeneity, manifesting in  diverse  symptoms,  disease  trajectories,  treatment  responses,  and neuroimaging profiles among patients (Bondar et al., 2020; Drysdale et  al.,  2017;  Krishnan  and  Nestler,  2008;  Nguyen  et  al.,  2022).  This heterogeneity poses a substantial challenge and often leads to conflicting findings in research (Chen et al., 2016). For instance, Chand et al. identified  two  subtypes  of  schizophrenia:  subtype  1  displayed  widespread decreased gray matter volume across the brain, whereas subtype 2 exhibited increased gray matter volume in the basal ganglia and internal capsule (Chand et al., 2020). Similar subtypes have been reported in  other  psychiatric disorders  as  well  (Han et  al.,  2022b;  Han  et  al., 2023e). In our study, we addressed this heterogeneity by identifying two replicated transdiagnostic subtypes with opposing abnormal gray matter morphological patterns. This reconciliation helps to clarify contradictory findings from previous neuroimaging studies (Bondar et al., 2020; Drysdale et al., 2017; Krishnan and Nestler, 2008; L ´ azaro et al., 2011; Okasha et al., 2000). Although structural abnormalities in subtype 1 were  not  significant  in  the  discovery  dataset,  likely  due  to  its  small sample size, the validation datasets consistently replicated the opposite differential patterns. investigated  their  associations  with  neurotransmitter  receptor/transporter  profiles  and  transcriptional  profiles  of  inflammation-related genes.  Neurotransmitter  dysfunction  is  widely  recognized  as  a  key aspect  of  the  pathology  of  psychiatric  disorders,  and  the  efficacy  of modern  antidepressants  and  antipsychotics  hinges  on  the  selective modulation of neurotransmitter function (Hansen and Shafiei, 2022a). Indeed, neurotransmitter receptor profiles have been implicated in both structural and functional dysfunctions observed in patients with psychiatric disorders (Hansen and Shafiei, 2022a, 2022b). Consistent with these findings, our study revealed significant associations between the distribution of neurotransmitter receptors/transporters and the structural  abnormalities  observed  in  the  identified  subtypes.  Moreover, dominance analysis results unveiled distinct contributions of excitatory and  inhibitory  receptors  to  the  structural  abnormalities  observed  in these subtypes. Furthermore, our findings indicated that the structural abnormalities  of  subtype  2  were  linked  to  transcriptional  profiles  of inflammation-related genes. This finding aligns with the inflammation hypothesis of psychosis (Thylur and Goldsmith, 2022). Together, these results provide detailed insights into the distinct molecular and genetic bases underlying the identified subtypes.

This study is not without its limitations. Firstly, the number of psychiatric disorders included in our analysis is limited. However, despite being identified based on three primary psychiatric disorders, the two transdiagnostic subtypes demonstrated generalizability to unseen psychiatric  disorders.  Future  investigations  could  explore  whether  these subtypes hold validity across a broader spectrum of psychiatric conditions. Secondly, due to a lack of sufficient clinical information such as education level, alcohol and especially for medication dosage, we were unable to examine their effects on the clustering outcomes. This represents an important avenue for future research to elucidate the influence of medication on the identified subtypes. Thirdly, we did not account for comorbidities in our analysis. Future studies should investigate whether comorbidities impact our results and whether they contribute to further subtyping within psychiatric disorders.

In  conclusion,  we have identified two robust transdiagnostic subtypes based on individualized gray matter morphological abnormalities. These subtypes exhibit contrasting patterns of gray matter morphological  abnormalities  relative  to  healthy  controls  and  have  divergent structural  covariance  network-informed  disease  epicenters.  Furthermore, the gray matter morphological abnormalities in these subtypes are distinctly associated with neurotransmitter receptors/transporters and transcriptional profiles of inflammation-related genes, indicating their differential  molecular  and  genetic  underpinnings.  Our  findings  offer novel insights into psychiatric nosology and underscore the importance of considering transdiagnostic approaches in psychiatric research.
