## Normative Modeling

Very few comparisons of predictive models for normative modeling have been reported (Ge at al.). We compared 3 different predictive models - ordinary least squares regression (OLS), multiple fractional polynomial regression (MFP), and generalized additive models of location shape and scale (GAMLSS) (Bethlehem et al. 2022; Dima et al. 2022; Dinga et al. 2021; Bozek et al. 2023) which have been previously described for normative modeling. For each method, we tested a univariate and a multivariate model. In the univariate models, age was the predictive variable, and the models were trained on sex stratified data sets following eTIV volume adjustment, site harmonization with Combat, and mean-centering. A 5-fold crossvalidation was used to evaluate each model. All models were implemented in R and trained for each region independently. The model with the best overall performance based on average mean absolute error (MAE) was selected and trained on all control samples for each region.

## Supplemental Analyses

In the primary analyses, z-score distributions of cases were compared to controls using the z-test. Using the same methodology, we carried out supplemental pairwise comparisons between all cases / phenotypes (e.g. AD to EMCI, EMCI to LMCI, and AD to LMCI). We also performed supplemental analyses on laterality. The laterality index (LI) was calculated for all subjects for each region using the formula LI = (VL - VR)/(VL+VR) where VL is the volume of the region on the left side and VR is the volume of the region on the right side. As previously described, the top-performing model was trained on the control subjects, z-scores were calculated for both cases and controls, z-score distributions were compared using the z-test, and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Additionally, we compared z-score distributions of left sided regions to right sided regions within each phenotype.

## Imaging Data Acquisition and Sample Populations

Unprocessed T1w images and clinical measures were accessed from the following resources:

The Lifespan Human Connectome Project in Development (HCP DEV)

All subjects (n=628; 291 male, 337 female) were defined as controls and considered to have a broadly typical development that excluded a history of a serious medical or psychiatric condition (Harms et al., 2018; Somerville et al., 2018).

Human Connectome Project Young Adult Study S900 Release (HCP YA)

All subjects belong to a sibship, were defined as controls (n=726; 328 male, 398 female), and considered to be broadly healthy without a history of neurodevelopmental, psychiatric, or neurological disorders (Van Essen et al., 2012).

The Lifespan Human Connectome Project in Aging (HCP AGE)

All subjects were defined as controls (n=701; 306 male, 395 female) and had typical aging without major psychiatric or neurologic disorders. Subjects were screened for cognitive deficits and while dementia like Alzheimer's disease was excluded, mild to moderate cognitive deficits were allowed depending on the age of the subject (Bookheimer et al., 2019).

The Human Connectome Project for Early Psychosis (HCP EP)

All subjects were assessed with the SCID-5-RV. Control subjects (n=56; 37 male, 19 female) did not have formal psychiatric diagnoses or history of psychiatric hospitalization. Subjects with non-affective psychosis (Early SCZ) (n=83; 59 male, 24 female) met DSM V criteria for schizophrenia, schizophreniform, schizoaffective, psychosis NOS, delusional disorder, or brief psychotic disorder with onset within five years of study enrollment. Subjects from the HCP EP cohort with affective psychosis (i.e. bipolar disorder with psychotic features or depression with psychotic freatures) were not included in the current analyses. (Lewandowski et al., 2020).

UCLA Consortium for Neuropsychiatric Phenomics LA5c Study (UCLA) Control subjects (n=117; 62 male, 55 female) were without prior psychiatric diagnosis including substance abuse. Case subjects (UCLA SCZ) were diagnosed schizophrenia (n=41; 31 male, 10

female) according to criteria in the DSM IV using the SCID-I. OpenNeuro Dataset ds000030 (Bilder et al., 2020; Poldrack et al., 2016).

Brain Correlates of Speech Perception in Schizophrenia Patients With and Without Auditory Hallucinations (SCZ AH)

All subjects were assessed with the SCID-5-RV. Control subjects (n=23; 15 male, 7 female) were age and gender matched to cases without past psychiatric diagnoses. Cases were defined as those meeting DSM V criteria for schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder and either experienced daily auditory verbal hallucinations (SCZ +AH) (n=21; 19 male, 2 female) or were free of hallucinations for at least 6 months (SCZ -AH) (n=23, 16 male, 7 female). OpenNeuro Dataset ds004302 (Soler-Vidal et al., 2022).

Aggregate Schizophrenia Cohort (All SCZ)

To increase the sample size of subjects with schizophrenia, the subjects with schizophrenia from each cohort were aggregated into an additional cohort (n=168; 125 male, 43 female).

The Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)

Controls were cognitively normal subjects (n=124; 56 male, 68 female) without memory concerns, evidence of dementia, MMSE &gt;= 24, and education adjusted Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory II ≥ 9 for 16 or more years of education, ≥ 5 for 8-15 years of education, ≥ 3 for 0-7 years of education. Early Mild Cognitive Impairment (EMCI) (n=211; 111 male, 100 female) was defined as having subjective memory complaint without functional impairment and MMSE &gt;= 24. The status of 'early' MCI was established using education adjusted scores on the

Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory II ≥ 16 years education: 9-11; 8-15 years: 5-9; 0-7 years: 3-6. Late Mild Cognitive Impairment (LMCI) (n=113; 53 male. 60 female) was defined as having subjective memory complaint without functional impairment and MMSE &gt;= 24. The status of 'late' MCI was established using the Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory II ≥ 16 years of education: ≤ 8; 8-15 years: ≤ 4; 0-7 years: ≤ 2. Alzheimer's Disease (AD) met definition of probable Alzheimer's disease (n=88; 47 male, 41 female) based on National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke (NINCDS) / Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association (ADRDA) criteria and MMSE 20-26 (Bernstein et al., 2021). A subset of 540 subjects acquired on Siemens 3T were used as described in Bernstein et al (15).

## Site Harmonization

In univariate models (see main text and below), volumes were harmonized across sites in sex stratified datasets using Combat as implemented in Neuroharmonize python library (16-18). To reduce the possibility of removing variation related to disease state rather than site, site effects were first estimated using control subjects and harmonization was then applied to both cases and controls. Alternatively, site was included as a covariate in multivariate models and modeled as a fixed effect in OLS and MFP models and as a random effect in GAMLSS. With the exception of ADNI, site refers to the physical scanner used to obtain the imaging. Despite using the subset of subjects described in Bernstein et al. with MRI acquisition on a Siemens 3T scanner (15), the number of ADNI sites was 44 making harmonization algorithms suboptimal due to small n per site and some sites without control samples (Supplemental Table 5). Since the acquisition protocol was harmonized across Siemens 3T scanners, we could treat ADNI as a single site. Recent analyses by Vidal et al. using the Frequently Traveling Human Phantom MRI dataset

demonstrated that variation in HIPS-THOMAS segmentation is much higher between scanners than within scanners (Vidal et al. 2024). We therefore were confident in treating this subset of ADNI data as one site rather than 44. A summary of the sites and MRI acquisition parameters and number of individuals per site are available in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7.

## Covariate Analysis

To assess the effect of individual variables on model performance, we compared the performance of models using age only, age plus one additional variable (e.g. sex, TIV, or site), and all variables using the univariate and multivariate GAMLSS models specified above. For example, the effect of TIV on model performance was assessed using a univariate approach where age was the independent variable, and volumes were adjusted for TIV or the multivariate approach where age was the independent variable and TIV was included as a covariate. As above, 5-fold cross validation was used to compare models and MAE averaged over all folds and nuclei was used as the primary metric of model performance.

## Model Training Time

The microbenchmark R package was used to evaluate training times for all models. Each model was trained 20 times on the control samples using volumes for the left whole thalamus. Training times were then averaged. The hardware was an Apple MacBook Pro with Apple M1 Max processor (8 performance cores, 2 efficiency cores) with 64 GB of ram.

## Supplemental Results

## Normative Modeling

Six univariate and multivariate models using ordinary least squares regression (OLS), multiple fractional polynomial regression (MFP), and generalized additive models of location, shape, and scale (GAMLSS) were compared with 5-fold cross-validation across the 22 thalamic regions for a total of 660 trained models. Global mean absolute error (MAE) was comparable amongst all models (Supplemental Table 1). GAMLSS models were the overall top performing models based on MAE. MAE values between test and train datasets were not significantly different as assessed by a two-tailed t-test applied across folds and regions (minimum p=0.93), ruling out overfitting. Z-scores for all three models were highly concordant (Supplmental Figures 8-10). In the case of the left medial geniculate nucleus, it appears z-scores from OLS and MFP are nearly identical suggesting that the optimal model chosen by MFP was linear (Supplemental Figure 10). Although model performance was similar and GAMLSS requires increased training times, there are benefits to using a GAMLSS multivariate model including increasing the public accessibility of our normative models by elimination of steps such as sex-stratification, eTIV adjustment, and combat harmonization and the ability to leverage multiple methods described to adapt a GAMLSS model to new data (1,3).

Model calibration plots for the GAMLSS multivariate model were visually assessed and did not reveal substantial miscalibration (left Pulvinar shown in Supplemental Figure 11 as an example). Visual inspection of z-scores and volumes demonstrated that the model mitigated effects of age, site, sex, and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) as evidenced by near zero slopes for controls and shifting means for cases (Supplemental Figures 6 and 7).

## Supplemental Analyses

Statistically significant differences in the means of the z-score distributions between all pairs of phenotypes for left and right thalamic regions and the laterality index are summarized in Supplemental figure 6. Statistically significant differences in the means of the z-score distributions between left and right regions were observed in the control group where the left AV and VLA had lower z-scores than the right and the right Pul had lower z-scores than the left (Supplemental Table 8).

## Alzheimer's disease vs schizophrenia

Comparing the aggregate schizophrenia cohort to Alzheimer's disease, we observed some thalamic regions affected in both compared to controls such as the bilateral pulvinar and bilateral mediodorsal-parafasciculus, however, a statistically significant difference in the z-score distributions was observed between the two phenotypes indicating that there is a greater effect in Alzheimer's disease (Supplemental Figure 5). The largest difference observed between the two is the left anteroventral nucleus, which displayed the largest difference in means observed between controls and Alzheimer's disease, whereas no statistically significant differences in z-score distributions were observed in the left anteroventral nucleus in any of the schizophrenia cohorts. Additionally, the whole thalamus laterality index, right ventral posteriolateral nucleus and its laterality index, and left mediodorsal-parafascicular nucleus stand out as the largest differences between schizophrenia and Alzheimer's disease. The aggregate schizophrenia cohort appears to lie along the progression from early MCI to late MCI with significantly decreased z-score distributions in the right ventral anterior and right ventrolateral posterior nuclei in schizophrenia compared to early MCI and significantly decreased z-score distributions in the left anteroventral

and right ventral posterior lateral nuclei in schizophrenia compared to late MCI (Supplemental Figure 5).

## Model training time

Training times were 3-4 times longer for multivariate models compared to univariate models and a GAMLSS multivariate models required about 14 seconds in training time whereas an MFP model required about 0.2 seconds to train (Supplemental Table 9).

## Covariate analysis

Adjusting for eTIV had the largest individual effect on model performance followed by sex while site had the smallest individual effect on model performance (Supplemental Figure 2). Model performance was similar regardless of the method of adjusting for TIV or site (Supplemental figure 2, Supplemental Table 1).

## Extreme z-score deviations

Infranormal and supranormal z-scores were observed in 42.4 percent and 48.4 percent of control subjects, respectively (Supplemental Table 9). The percentage of subjects with at least one infranormal z-score increases from early MCI to late MCI to AD while the percentage of individuals with at least one supranormal z-score decreases (Supplemental Table 10). There was a more heterogenous pattern in schizophrenia cohorts (Supplemental Table 2). In the regional overlap analyses, there was an increase in the percentage of subjects with infranormal z-scores and a decrease in the percentage of subjects with supranormal z-scores in most regions that progresses from early MCI to late MCI and AD (Supplemental Figure 4). In contrast,

schizophrenia cohorts demonstrated more heterogeneous patterns of extreme z-score deviations and the regional overlap appears greater for infranormal z-scores than supranormal z-scores for all phenotypes (Supplemental Figure 7). Statistically significant increases in infranormal z-score deviations are seen bilaterally in all cohorts except UCLA SCZ and decreases in supranormal zscore deviations are seen bilaterally in AD and aggregate SCZ and on the left in Early SCZ (Supplemental Figure 12).
