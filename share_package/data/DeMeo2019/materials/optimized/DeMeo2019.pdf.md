## Dynamic gray matter volume changes in pediatric multiple sclerosis

A 3.5 year MRI study

Ermelinda De Meo, MD, Alessandro Meani, MSc, Lucia Moiola, MD, Angelo Ghezzi, MD, Pierangelo Veggiotti, MD, Massimo Filippi, MD, and Maria A. Rocca, MD

Neurology ® 2019;92:e1709-e1723. doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000007267

## Abstract

## Objectives

To assess, using MRI, the spatial patterns of gray matter (GM) atrophy in pediatric patients with multiple sclerosis (MS), their dynamic changes over time, and their clinical relevance.

## Methods

Sixty-eight pediatric patients with MS (30 with a clinical and MRI follow-up after 3.5 years) and 26 healthy controls (HC) underwent clinical and MRI evaluation. To overcome di /uniFB03 culties in obtaining longitudinal scans in pediatric HC, a group of 317 pediatric HC from an NIH-funded MRI Study of Normal Brain Development was used to estimate GM developmental trajectories. In pediatric patients with MS, deviations from normative GM volume values at the voxel level were assessed at baseline and during the follow-up, using linear mixed-e /uniFB00 ects models. Correlations between GM volume deviations and disability, IQ, and white matter (WM) lesion volumes (LV) were estimated.

## Results

Pediatric patients with MS showed failures in GM development in several cortical and subcortical regions, as well as GM atrophy progression in most of these regions, which were only partially related to focal WM LV. Signi /uniFB01 cant correlations were found between regional GM atrophy (particularly of deep GM regions) and disability, whereas higher IQ was associated with reduced deviations from age-expected GM volumes of speci /uniFB01 c GM regions at baseline and during the follow-up.

## Conclusions

Impaired GM maturation occurs in pediatric patients with MS, which is only partially driven by WM in /uniFB02 ammation, suggesting that early neurodegenerative phenomena contribute to disability. High IQ, a measure of reserve, may o /uniFB00 er protection by promoting remodeling of GM pruning in this young age.

Widespread focal demyelinating lesions in the myelin-rich white matter (WM) are one of the pathologic hallmarks of multiple sclerosis (MS). However, it is now well-established that the gray matter (GM) is not spared by the disease, even during the earliest phases. 1 Early GM tissue loss (i.e., atrophy), measured using MRI, has been associated with long-term accumulation of disability and cognitive impairment in patients with MS. 2 Studying the dynamics of GM atrophy development during the /uniFB01 rst stages of the disease may therefore provide important clues about the factors responsible for long-term clinical outcomes. The most suitable patient groups for such studies are those who present with clinically isolated syndromes and pediatric patients.

Given the rarity of pediatric MS, MRI studies of this population are relatively scarce. Cross-sectional investigations have demonstrated reduced volumes of the thalamus 3,4 and whole brain 5 in pediatric patients with MS compared to ageand sex-matched healthy controls (HC). During childhood and adolescence, subtle modi /uniFB01 cations of brain structures related to maturational phenomena occurring in the brain WM and GM (myelination and synaptic pruning) have been demonstrated using volumetric MRI measurements in healthy youths. 6 Thus it is possible that disease onset during such a critical period of life might a /uniFB00 ect the physiologic trajectories of maturation. In line with this hypothesis, a 2-year longitudinal study 7 showed that disease onset during childhood leads to a failure of age-expected primary brain growth and subsequent brain atrophy.

Several studies in both adult and pediatric patients with MS have consistently shown regional variations in atrophy development, with the early involvement of deep GM nuclei, followed by several cortical regions located in the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes. 8 -10 Whether this regional pattern is partially due to a failure of region-speci /uniFB01 c age-related brain growth in pediatric patients has not yet been investigated.

Starting from these considerations, this longitudinal study investigates the complex interplay between brain growth and disease-related pathology on the regional trajectories of GM volume modi /uniFB01 cations in pediatric patients with MS. To explore the in /uniFB02 uence of WM in /uniFB02 ammation on our /uniFB01 ndings, the relationship between GM volume and focal T 2-hyperintense WM lesions was assessed. To determine the clinical relevance of GM volume changes, their correlation with clinical disability and IQ 11 was studied.

## Methods

## Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

Approval was received from the local ethical standards committee on human experimentation, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their parents prior to study enrollment.

## Participants

Sixty-eight right-handed pediatric patients with relapsingremitting MS (RRMS) 12 were enrolled and underwent baseline clinical and MRI evaluation. Thirty of them were re-assessed with clinical and MRI evaluation after a median follow-up of 3.5 years (range 1.2 -6.3 years). Twenty-eight patients or the baseline cohort refused the follow-up evaluation, while the remaining 10 had the baseline evaluation performed only recently (less than 1 year of follow-up).

For all evaluations, patients with MS had to be relapse- and steroid-free for at least 1 month prior to clinical and MRI assessment. In addition, they had to be on stable treatment for MS for at least 6 months.

Patients with acute disseminated encephalomyelitis were excluded according to published operational criteria. 13 None of the patients had a diagnosis of a clinically isolated syndrome at the time of study inclusion and all of them had at least 2 clinical attacks and the formation of new CNS lesions on serial MRI. Whenever needed, appropriate genetic testing was performed to exclude leukodystrophies. Exclusion criteria were concomitant therapy with antidepressants, psychoactive drugs, or a history of other primary neurologic or medical disorders in addition to MS. Participants judged by their neurologist to have primary psychiatric impairment in addition to MS were also excluded.

To overcome the di /uniFB03 culty of obtaining longitudinal MRI scans from pediatric HC, 2 groups of pediatric HC were selected for comparison of MRI volumes: (1) a group of 26 sexand age-matched HC recruited at Hospital San Ra /uniFB00 aele (HSR) with no history of neurologic dysfunction and a normal neurologic examination, studied on the same MRI scanner as patients with MS at baseline; and (2) a group of 317 pediatric HC from the NIH-funded MRI Study of Normal Brain Development (NIH HC) with longitudinal MRI assessments for brain volume quanti /uniFB01 cation (median follow-up 3.6 years, range

0.9 -5.4 years). 14 The data from the NIH-funded MRI Study of Normal Brain Development were downloaded in June 2016. MRI data from all available HC were initially downloaded, then an image quality check for motion and other artifacts was performed. From the /uniFB01 nal 317 NIH HC dataset considered suitable for the analysis, a subset was followed up once (n = 163; median follow-up 2.0 years, range 0.9 -4.3 years) and another twice (n = 154; median follow-up 3.9 years, range 2.0 -5.4 years) aged from 4.2 years at the /uniFB01 rst MRI scan up to 22.3 years at the last MRI scan. All study participants were Caucasian.

## Clinical assessment

All patients underwent a neurologic examination with rating on the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) at baseline and follow-up. At baseline, patients also underwent an assessment of premorbid IQ through the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children. 15

## MRI acquisition

Scans from pediatric patients with MS and pediatric HC acquired at HSR were obtained using a 3.0T scanner (Intera, Philips Medical Systems, Best, the Netherlands) under a program of regular maintenance (no major scanner hardware or software upgrades occurred during the study) at baseline and follow-up. The following brain MRI were acquired: (1) dualecho (DE) turbo spin-echo (TSE) (repetition time [TR]/echo time [TE] = 2,599/16,80 ms, echo train length [ETL] = 6, /uniFB02 ip angle = 90°, matrix size = 256 × 256, /uniFB01 eld of view [FOV] = 240 × 240 mm 2 , 44 axial 3-mm-thick slices) and (2) 3D T1-weighted fast /uniFB01 eld echo (TR/TE = 25/4.6 ms, /uniFB02 ip angle = 30°, matrix size = 256 × 256, FOV = 230 × 230 mm 2 , 220 contiguous, axial slices with voxel size = 0.89 × 0.89 × 0.8 mm 3 ). For all scans, the slices were positioned to run parallel to a line that joins the most inferoanterior and infero-posterior parts of the corpus callosum, with careful repositioning at follow-up.

Scans of the NIH HC were obtained at 6 pediatric centers on GE (Cleveland, OH) and Siemens (Munich, Germany) 1.5T scanners. The standardized MRI protocol included (1) DE TSE (TR/TE = 3,500/5 -119 ms, ETL = 8; /uniFB02 ip angle = 90°, matrix size = 256 × 256, FOV = 256 × 224 mm 2 , 2-mm-thick axial slices); (2) whole brain 3D T1-weighted radiofrequencyspoiled gradient echo sequence (TR = 22 -25 ms, TE = 10 -11 ms, /uniFB02 ip angle = 30°, FOV = 256 × [160 -180] mm 2 , matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1.0 mm). Additional details on acquisition measures and participants are described in Evans et al. 14

## Lesional analysis

A single experienced observer, unaware of to whom the scans belonged, performed MRI analysis. T2-hyperintense and T1-hypointense lesion volumes (LV) were measured on the DE and 3D T1-weighted scans, respectively, using a local thresholding segmentation technique (Jim 6, Xinapse Systems, Colchester, UK). The 3D T1-weighted images were coregistered and resliced to match the T2-weighted scans before identi /uniFB01 cation and segmentation of the T1-hypointense lesions and underwent lesion re /uniFB01 lling before GM volumetric analysis. 16

## GMvolume analysis

Regional GM growth modeling in HC and the evaluation of deviations from normative data in patients with MS required the alignment of participant images to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space. Taking into account the longitudinal consistency of MRI scans belonging to the same participant, within-subject serial longitudinal registration as implemented in SPM 12 was used to align the available 3D T1weighted images (either 2 or 3). 17 For each participant, this step creates an image of the Jacobian determinants of the transformation between each timepoint and the midpoint average template. Then, midpoint average templates and baseline scans (for participants who had only a single scan available) were used for groupwise alignment: /uniFB01 rst, they were segmented into di /uniFB00 erent tissue types via the segmentation routine. 18 Then, GM and WM segmented images of all participants, in the closest possible rigid-body alignment with each other, were used to produce initial GM and WM templates and to drive the deformation to the templates. At each iteration, the deformations, calculated using the di /uniFB00 eomorphic anatomical registration using exponentiated lie algebra (DARTEL) registration method, 19 were applied to GM and WM, with an increasingly good alignment of participant morphology, to produce templates. Finally, an a /uniFB03 ne transformation that maps from the population average template to MNI space was calculated. With the aim of transforming each timepoint of each participant into standard space, the midpoint average templates were intensity modulated using the Jacobian determinant (obtained by applying serial longitudinal registration), and then each group of timepoints, including baseline scans, was spatially normalized (DARTEL registration), modulated, and smoothed with a 4-mm Gaussian kernel using the DARTEL tool ' Normalize to MNI space. ' The result of this preprocessing procedure was a modulated, smoothed, and normalized GM image for each participant at each timepoint; these were subsequently used to estimate regional GM volume developmental trajectories in HC and evaluate potential deviations in patients with MS.

In line with previous imaging studies, 26 we detected a heterochronic process of maturation, 24,35 comprising an initial increase, followed by GM volume reduction, 22 resulting in di /uniFB00 erent ages for peak GM volume. Specifically, brain regions showing a negative growth rate /uniFB01 rst, such as those in the parietal cortex, are thought to mature earlier than frontal regions, re /uniFB02 ecting the temporal achievement of the main milestones in cognitive and functional development.

Once the GM volume developmental trajectories of the HC cohort were established, we estimated the deviations of pediatric patients with MS from the expected values at the voxel level, as well as their change over the follow-up period. In line with previous studies demonstrating a failure of whole-brain age-expected brain growth, 7,20 we found a failure from the sexand age-expected GM developmental trajectories in pediatric patients with MS in several cortical regions belonging to the frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes and cerebellum, as well as subcortical regions comprising the thalamus and caudate nucleus. The analysis of correlations showed that highly interconnected brain regions, such as the basal ganglia, 37 and late developing areas, such as anterior fronto-

temporal regions, 38 were more in /uniFB02 uenced by the presence of focal WM lesions and by prolonged exposure to diseaserelated processes (as re /uniFB02 ected by the correlation with disease duration). Based on this, it is tempting to speculate that MSrelated damage may lead to a failure of brain development by depriving late-developing regions of the a /uniFB00 erent input needed for their proper maturation, possibly as a consequence of Wallerian degeneration phenomena due to focal lesions along WM tracts. The possibility of impaired maturation of WM tracts connecting these regions should also be considered, as suggested by a recent di /uniFB00 usion tensor MRI investigation in very young pediatric patients with MS (younger than 12 years), which detected an age-dependent regional vulnerability to MS pathology in the WM. 39

Of note, we also identi /uniFB01 ed several regions with signi /uniFB01 cant deviations from sex- and age-expected GM volume values where di /uniFB00 erences were not associated with focal WM lesions. This suggests that other processes, not strictly related to the presence of focal in /uniFB02 ammatory demyelinating lesions in the WM, lead to GM alterations in the early stages of the disease. Early GM involvement in patients with MS has also been seen in previous studies of adult patients with early RRMS 40 and radiologically isolated syndromes. 41

To better characterize abnormalities of GM volume, we also explored the trajectories of their evolution over a 3.5-year follow-up in a subgroup of patients with MS who had a longitudinal MRI assessment. This analysis showed that in pediatric patients with MS there were continued deviations from the expected maturation trajectories in the majority of GM areas, including several cortical and subcortical regions. The correlations with the increase in T2 LV con /uniFB01 rmed, once again, that only for some of these regions (the thalamus and a few regions located in the precentral and postcentral gyri) maturational trajectory deviation was in /uniFB02 uenced by the accumulation of focal WM lesions. Combined with the results of baseline assessment, these /uniFB01 ndings suggest the existence of at least 2 mechanisms explaining GM damage: the /uniFB01 rst, more related to the in /uniFB02 ammatory WM lesion dependent component of the disease, mainly occurs at the earliest stages of the disease and involves highly connected regions, while the second is characterized by primary involvement of the GM. Even though this aspect was not investigated in the current study, focal lesions within the GM are unlikely to explain these volumetric abnormalities, since they have been found to be relatively rare in pediatric patients with MS. 42,43

The longitudinal analysis also allowed the identi /uniFB01 cation of a few areas in which deviations from normal maturational trajectories tended to decline over time. In the absence of a pathologic assessment, we can only speculate on the possible reasons for this. Clearly, it may simply re /uniFB02 ect reaching of a plateauing limit of tissue loss, which might occur with different timing in di /uniFB00 erent GM structures. Alternatively, it is tempting to hypothesize that by virtue of their young age, high brain plasticity potential, 44 and remyelination capabilities, 45

pediatric patients may bene /uniFB01 t from some reparative mechanisms that help to preserve the GM. In line with this, a previous 2-year study that analyzed T1-weighted signal intensity recovery in newly formed WM lesions in pediatric and adult patients with MS showed greater lesional recovery in pediatric patients, which was suggestive of a greater reparative capacity.

To determine the clinical relevance of GM changes, we explored their correlations with disability and premorbid IQ. In contrast to a previous cross-sectional study by our group 3 that found no correlation between GM atrophy and clinical disability (likely due to the smaller number of patients and different method of analysis), in the current investigation failure of GM development in key brain regions, such as the thalamus, caudate nucleus, precuneus, and frontal regions, was associated with the severity of clinical disability. This is in agreement with many studies performed in adult patients with MS, 10,46 including a recent one that demonstrated such an association in all clinical phenotypes of the disease, including clinically isolated syndrome patients. 10

General intelligence is in /uniFB02 uenced by genetic factors, with an e /uniFB00 ect of heritability that increases with age (about 30% in early childhood to 80% in adulthood). 11 Higher IQ has been linked to higher brain volume, higher GM volume, greater cortical thickness, neural e /uniFB03 ciency, and WM integrity. 11 Using regional methods of analysis, a network of brain regions mostly located in the frontal and parietal lobes as well as speci /uniFB01 c regions in the temporal and occipital lobes have been related to individual intelligence. 47 In line with this, at baseline we found a relationship between higher premorbid IQ and GM volume preservation in the cingulate cortex, thalamus, and other strategic brain regions belonging to the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes and cerebellum. Consistent with our results, the volume of these regions has been related not only to general intelligence, but also to verbal and nonverbal intelligence. 48 Higher IQ was also found to be protective against tissue loss during the follow-up in the cingulate cortex, precuneus, and several regions located in the frontal and temporal lobes and the cerebellum, which might be due to GM pruning, which selectively eliminates GM that does not e /uniFB00 ectively contribute to cognition, as suggested by a study in healthy children. 49

This study is not without limitations. First, only part of our patients underwent a clinical and MRI follow-up assessment. Although the number of patients assessed at follow-up may seem small (n = 30), it should be considered that the prevalence of pediatric MS is relatively low. Second, we analyzed only IQ as a possible measure of reserve. Other factors, such as socioeconomic characteristics and leisure activities, which have not been collected in our patients, should be included in future analyses. Finally, the clinical stability of our patients during the study period did not allow any analysis of prediction of disability worsening. As a consequence, a longer follow-up is needed to ascertain the clinical relevance of GM volumetric abnormalities in pediatric patients with MS.
