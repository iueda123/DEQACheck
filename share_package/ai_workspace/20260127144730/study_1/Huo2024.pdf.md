Alzheimer ' s disease (AD) is the most common type of dementia and one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide (1,2). Mild cognitive impairment (MCI), which is considered a transitional stage between normal aging and AD (3,4), exhibits variable characteristics. A subset of patients with MCI may progress to AD within 3 years after receiving an MCI diagnosis (5,6). Therefore, identifying people with MCI provides a window for potential early intervention to prevent progression to AD.

There is considerable variability in brain function and clinical symptoms among patients with AD and MCI (7). Relying solely on neuropsychological assessment can lead to misdiagnoses, especially in atypical forms of AD, due to the presence of atypical phenotypes (8,9). In addition, subtypes with homogeneous clinical phenotypes may be driven by distinct neurobiological factors (10,11), and different subtypes may exhibit diverse trajectories of cognitive decline (12,13). This poses signi /uniFB01 cant challenges for diagnosing and predicting disease trajectory and treatment response. Thus, studies of the heterogeneity in AD and MCI are pivotal for improving

Biomarkers allow for improved detection of heterogeneity and subtypes. Recent functional biomarker studies have provided emerging /uniFB01 ndings regarding AD and MCI subtypes. Some studies (14,15) have identi /uniFB01 ed subtypes that show distinct differences in multiple functional networks, such as the default mode network (DMN), dorsal attention network (DAN), and limbic system (LIM). Based on a larger scale of abnormal functional networks, 4 neurophysiological subtypes of AD were de /uniFB01 ned, and the corresponding subtypes of MCI were also identi /uniFB01 ed (16). These studies have also observed differences in clinical presentation and treatment responses among neurophysiological subtypes. However, a limited number of studies have employed dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) patterns to investigate the neurobiological heterogeneity within patients with AD and MCI.

To comprehend the heterogeneity of AD and MCI, it is crucial to examine individual neurobiological variations beyond

The normative model is a statistical approach capable of mapping demographic variables (such as age and sex) or behavioral variables into quantitative neuroimaging features. In the normative model, disease can be de /uniFB01 ned as an extreme of the normal range or a deviation from normal function without prior clinical labeling (17). ## METHODS AND MATERIALS

## Participants

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) scans of 1896 individuals were acquired from 3 datasets. Dataset 1, which included 644 healthy individuals, was used in this work to build a normative model and was obtained from the Cambridge Centre for Aging and Neuroscience repository (Cam-CAN, available at http://www.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/datasets/camcan/) (24,25). Dataset 2 comprised 257 normal control (NC) participants, 257 patients with MCI, and 295patients with AD from the MCAD (Multicenter Alzheimer Disease Imaging Consortium) dataset. Dataset 3 included 259 NC participants and 184 patients with MCI from the ADNI (Alzheimer ' s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative) database (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/), which was used to investigate longitudinal changes in MCI. Information on demographic and clinical characteristics of study participants and ethical information is shown in Table 1 and Table S1. Additional information regarding the Cam-CAN dataset can be found at https://camcanarchive.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/dataaccess/pdfs/CAMCAN700\_ MR\_params.pdf, while details about the ADNI dataset are presented at http://adni.loni.usc.edu/methods/documents/.

## Image Acquisition and Preprocessing

In dataset 1, all resting-state functional images of participants were obtained at a single site (Medical Research Council [UK] Cognition and Brain Sciences Unit) using a 3T Siemens TIM Trio scanner with a 32-channel head coil (25). For dataset 2, individuals were scanned on 1 of 7 different 3T MRI scanners to obtain T1-weighted images and resting-state functional images (26). In dataset 3, individuals were scanned on a variety of 3T scanners using standardized protocols at each site, and resting-state functional images were included in the current study. The corresponding MRI acquisition protocols and detailed scanning parameters are provided in Table S2.

All the fMRI scans from 3 datasets were preprocessed using the Brainnetome Toolkit (http://brant.brainnetome.org) (27). Detailed information is provided in our previous study (28) and can be found in the Supplemental Method S2. After quality control, we obtained a regional fMRI signal for each of the 246 regions in the Brainnetome Atlas (29) by averaging signals across their respective voxels. For structural MRI images, we utilized the standard procedures in the CAT12 toolbox (r1450) (http://dbm.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/), and the regional gray matter volume (GMV) and cortical thickness of each brain region were extracted according to the Brainnetome Atlas. Detailed information on image acquisition and preprocessing has been provided in our previous study (30) and is given in the Supplement.

## Identi /uniFB01 cation of dFC Features

dFC matrices for each participant were identi /uniFB01 ed using Pearson ' s correlation coef /uniFB01 cients combined with the sliding time window method (window length = 60 seconds) between all pairs of 246 regions. Connectivity strength (i.e., Pearson correlation coef /uniFB01 cients) was normalized using Fisher ' s r -toz transformation. Then, each FC matrix was decomposed into 17 networks based on the Yeo-17 network template (31). For each Yeo-17 network, we calculated the average functional connectivity connection strength (a-FCS) and its /uniFB02 uctuation (fFCS, average change of the framewise variation of the strengths) from dFC matrices (Supplemental Method S3). In this way, each participant ' s dFC pattern was represented by 34 features.

## Normative Modeling for a-FCS and f-FCS

We estimated normative models to predict each dFC feature using sex and age as covariates in Gaussian process regression (17,32) in a large healthy reference cohort from dataset 1. Gaussian process regression is grounded in a distribution over functions, facilitating Bayesian nonparametric interpolation. This approach ensures robust /uniFB01 tting for nonlinear relationships among variables and delivers consistent estimation of predictive uncertainty (33). In this study, we utilized Gaussian process regression to evaluate variations in brain characteristics based on demographic variables, speci /uniFB01 cally age and sex, with estimating prediction con /uniFB01 dence. The predicted con /uniFB01 -dence intervals can be interpreted as intervals within the normal range, with con /uniFB01 dence quantifying the degree to which each sample point is consistent with the normative model. To assess the generalizability of the model, we used 10-fold cross-validation before applying the model trained on datasets 1 to dataset 2 and dataset 3. Finally, the normative model was trained based on dataset 1 for subsequent disease deviation analyses. In this study, 34 normative models could be built to predict each feature of the dFC pattern (17 a-FCS and 17 f-FCS features, respectively).

## Statistical Analysis of Brain Intrinsic, Structural, and Clinical Characteristics

To investigate the deviation patterns in subtypes of AD and MCI, we calculated the average deviation of each subgroup and assessed the similarity between AD and MCI subtypes using Spearman correlations. We compared the differences between subtypes in clinical characteristics (using the MiniMental State Examination [MMSE] for all individuals, and Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA] scores available from

5 sites) with age and sex as covariates. Furthermore, differences in deviation patterns, functional connections, and structural features were identi /uniFB01 ed using 2-sample t tests with age, sex, and head motion as covariates. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate method (corrected a = .05).

We replicated the experiment on dataset 3 to investigate the robustness of our /uniFB01 ndings. We used Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to assess the risk of MCI subtypes progressing to AD. The logarithmic rank test (36) evaluated differences in survival rates among MCI subgroups, with survival rates indicating MCI diagnosis maintenance. A linear regression model was used to estimate cognitive decline duration in subtypes over time, with age and sex as covariates.

## Replication Experiments on Different Atlases

To further validate the robustness of the method and the main results of the current study, we replicated the experiments using the same procedures based on the Power-264 Node Atlas (37) and Schaefer-200 Parcel Atlas (38). The confusion matrix of the subgroup results between each paired atlas was used to quantify the robustness of the results.

## DISCUSSION

This study quantitatively addressed neurophysiological heterogeneity by analyzing deviation patterns from the dynamic functional connectome normative model and identi /uniFB01 ed disease subtypes in AD and MCI using a comprehensive resting-state fMRI multicenter dataset. Two consistent MCI subtypes were identi /uniFB01 ed from deviation patterns across 2 independent datasets. These subtypes differed in functional phenotypes, spatial atrophy, AD conversion risks, and rates of cognitive decline.

Our study yielded highly reliable and reproducible /uniFB01 ndings. To enhance the robustness of the results, we estimated the normative models with a large and independent dataset (CamCAN) to minimize potential biases associated with reusing data in multiple analysis steps. We implemented a 10-fold crossvalidation technique to investigate the reliability of our normative models. We also performed a 100-repeated procedure, applied various clustering methods, and used another 2 atlases to evaluate the stability of identi /uniFB01 ed subtypes. We also observed consistent results for MCI in 2 independent datasets, which suggests the presence of genuine biological differences between the subgroups.

## Normative Models of dFC Patterns

Previous studies on brain structure, pathophysiology, and cognition have highlighted the heterogeneity of AD and MCI (15,39 -41). Additionally, many researchers have asserted the inherent neurobiological heterogeneity of these conditions (16,42,43). To quantify heterogeneity, several studies (18 -22) recently applied a normative model of brain functional and structural features to estimate deviations for psychiatric disorders and identify subtypes. In contrast to the case-control analysis, the normative model is a reference model to quantify individual variation against centiles of variation in a reference population without clinical diagnosis (44,45). Here, based on a large sample and independent dataset (Cam-CAN), we built a sex-speci /uniFB01 c normative age model of brain functional features (Figure S4). In contrast to stationary FC as brain features traditionally, the dFC patterns that we used in this study

could provide a new perspective in observing brain activities and the intrinsic organization of the brain (46,47).

## Heterogeneous Deviation Patterns From the Normative Model in Patients With AD and MCI

Given the advantage of the normative model in obtaining individual-speci /uniFB01 c deviations for patients, we investigated dynamic functional features ' deviations for each patient with AD or MCI and explored the heterogeneity of deviations among patients. We identi /uniFB01 ed subtypes with positive deviation patterns, which comprised approximately one-third of patients with AD and one half of individuals with MCI, and the others with negative deviations. The subtypes of AD and MCI shared similar deviation patterns, indicating a potential transformation of MCI-1 into AD-1 and MCI-2 into AD-2.

Positive deviation patterns for AD and MCI were both mainly in the SM and DAN, indicating increased dFC strength and /uniFB02 uctuations. The functional connectivity strength in the DMN of AD-2 and MCI-2 decreased signi /uniFB01 cantly. Similar observations have been reported by others (48,49) for the FC of speci /uniFB01 c regions within the DMN in AD, including the left hippocampus, anterior cingulate cortex, and gyrus rectus. Similar to the DMN, there was a signi /uniFB01 cant decrease in the ventral attention network, DAN, and SM of AD-2 and MCI-2, consistent with previous research (50,51). Notably, AD-1 and MCI-1 showed abnormal increases in the DMN, suggesting that a reduction in DMN connectivity does not always manifest at the individual level of cognitive decline. One plausible explanation for this is that the decrease and increase in DMN connectivity are both functions of increasing amyloid deposition, and the elevation of amyloid deposition is associated with cognitive decline (52). Similarly, excessive excitation was observed in the SM, which is consistent with /uniFB01 ndings on mild AD reported by Ferreri et al. (53). This abnormal increase may represent compensatory activity.

The results indicate that dFC pattern alteration plays an important role in neuropathological heterogeneity analysis. On the whole, subtypes of MCI and AD sharing similar deviation patterns indicates that individuals with MCI may already exhibit heterogeneous patterns akin to those seen in AD. It raises the intriguing possibility that the heterogeneity typically observed in AD could begin to manifest during the MCI stage. Alternatively, it could imply that MCI and AD represent a continuum of similar heterogeneity patterns, suggesting that MCI and AD may share common underlying neuropathological mechanisms. Additionally, these /uniFB01 ndings re /uniFB02 ect that the normative model technique is useful for identifying heterogeneity and subtypes of AD and MCI.

## Heterogeneous Brain and Clinical Characterization of Deviation-Based Subtypes

The subtypes identi /uniFB01 ed through the dynamic functional connectome deviation pattern re /uniFB02 ected signi /uniFB01 cant brain and clinical heterogeneity. The AD-2 individuals with negative deviation patterns showed decreased GMV and cortical thickness, consistent with the severe functional changes and cognitive impairment seen in AD-2 individuals. Most participants with MCI (56%) were classi /uniFB01 ed as a similar negative subtype, which may indicate neuron loss and weakened synaptic connections leading to functional impairment in this subtype. The difference was also observed between MCI-2 and AD-2 in the LIM. The primary function of the LIM is to regulate motivation, emotion, learning, and memory (54). Therefore, subsequent changes in the functional modes of the subnetwork are associated with the progression of MCI-2 into AD. Compared with MCI-1, MCI-2 exhibits a more pronounced degree of structural atrophy, which is indicative of a more severe decline in cognitive abilities. Additionally, the negative deviation patterns of MCI-2 and AD-2 were evident in the DMN, showing a signi /uniFB01 cant reduction in FC. These DMNcentric changes seem to characterize a speci /uniFB01 c AD subgroup. The DMN is preferentially disrupted and shows pronounced structural atrophy, accounting for the decline in various cognitive functions and episodic memory (55).

Interestingly, AD-1 and MCI-1 and AD-2 and MCI-2 seem to share similar FC patterns with a few structural differences. This discrepancy may be attributed to asynchronous changes in structure and function during disease progression.

More importantly, the dynamic deviation patterns also revealed the different types of cognitive decline in MCI. The negative deviation pattern corresponded to a faster decline rate. Meanwhile, decreasing GMV may re /uniFB02 ect neurodegeneration and disease progression. Thus, based on the imaging results, subtypes with a negative pattern exhibit more severe structural atrophy and intrinsic FC disruption than those with a positive pattern. These results highlight that decreased dynamic functional patterns were associated with a high risk of conversion from MCI to AD. Previous studies have found several biomarkers of disease progression (56,57), and converging evidence suggests that individuals with MCI may belong to different subtypes with different risks. In this study, the results of cognition in dataset 3 showed that dynamic functional deviation patterns may be a valuable predictor of progression. There was no signi /uniFB01 cant difference in MMSE scores between the MCI-1 and MCI-2 subtypes, which may be due to dynamic functional activity changes beyond the measurable pathology and followed neurodegeneration (28).

Several limitations of the current study should be considered. First, scanning protocols varied across datasets and sites. To mitigate site effects on the calculation of deviations, we computed the mean deviation of the NC participants as a baseline for each site to adjust the deviations for patients accordingly. Second, longitudinal data were unavailable in the in-house dataset, so we used the participants with MCI from ADNI as an independent site for validation. We also noted that the sample size of the MCI group with longitudinal data is relatively small, which cautions us to interpret some of the results carefully. Finally, various neuropathological patterns identi /uniFB01 ed across different imaging modalities may give rise to distinct subtypes. The extent to which the subtyping /uniFB01 ndings derived from other features/modalities correspond to overlapping patient subgroups remains unclear, presenting a challenging area of research (42,58 -60). Further studies that incorporate more factors would be important for exploring the causes of subtype emergence.

## Conclusions

This study constructed normative models using a large healthy cohort and identi /uniFB01 ed dynamic functional connectome

subtypes associated with AD and MCI. These subtypes exhibited signi /uniFB01 cant differences in their intrinsic brain functional phenotypes and spatial atrophy patterns as well as in disease progression and cognitive decline trajectories. This study provides new insights for developing early prevention and personalized treatment strategies for AD.
