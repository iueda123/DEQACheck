Quantifying individual differences in neuroimaging metrics is attracting interest in clinical studies with mental disorders. Schizophrenia is diagnosed exclusively based on symptoms, and the biological heterogeneity makes it difficult to accurately assess pharmacological treatment effects on the brain state. Using the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience data set, we built normative models of brain states and mapped the deviations of the brain characteristics of each patient, to test whether deviations were related to symptoms, and further investigated the pharmacological treatment effect on deviation distributions. Specifically, we found that the patients can be divided into 2 groups: the normalized group had a normalization trend and milder symptoms at baseline, and the other group showed a more severe deviation trend. The baseline severity of the depression as well as the overall symptoms could predict the deviation of the static characteristics for the dorsal and ventral attention networks after treatment. In contrast, the positive symptoms could predict the deviations of the dynamic fluctuations for the default mode and dorsal attention networks after treatment. This work evaluates the effect of pharmacological treatment on static and dynamic brain states using an individualized approach, which may assist in understanding the heterogeneity of the illness pathology as well as the treatment response.

Key words : normative model; schizophrenia; pharmacological treatment; brain network; dynamic functional connectivity.

## Introduction

Schizophrenia is one of the most complex and heterogeneous mental disorders. The antipsychotic treatment is the first-line treatment for patients with schizophrenia. Many studies have suggested that the treatment significantly affects brain structure and function, yet individual differences in treatment responses are ubiquitous and prominent (Lin et al. 2021; Mehta et al. 2021; Matrone et al. 2022). Up to 30% of schizophrenia patients do not respond or respond poorly to standard treatment with antipsychotics. However, existing techniques usually operate at the group level to get an average profile of those who respond or do not respond to medical treatment, and provide no path to individual-level inference. To move away from group average statistics, neuroscientists and psychiatrists have adopted the normal growth charts approach, trying to quantify individual differences in brain and symptoms in patients suffering from mental disorders. As a promising approach, normative modeling posits that psychopathology characteristics of patients with the same diagnosis are not homogeneous, it highlights the value of understanding individual variation relative to group means (Marquand et al. 2016; Rutherford et al. 2022a, 2022b). Normative modeling has been applied in computational psychiatry, detecting individuals who deviate from the norm, including autism spectrum disorder (Bethlehem et al. 2020; Shan et al. 2022), major depressive disorders (Lin et al. 2023), Alzheimer's disease (Pinaya et al. 2021; Verdi et al. 2023), ADHD (Wolfers et al. 2020), and schizophrenia (Wolfers et al. 2018; Lv et al. 2020; Chien et al. 2022). Based on the normative model, this work aimed to examine how the deviations of the dynamic and static brain states are modulated by antipsychotic treatment, and whether the baseline severity of symptoms could predict the longitudinal changes.

Like the normal grow chart method, we must build a lifespan reference model to quantify individual variation against centiles of variation in a reference population to provide good lifespan coverage (Gur et al. 2014; Wolfers et al. 2020). Placing individuals into this coordinate system creates the opportunity to characterize their profiles of deviation. Recently, several studies tried establishing normative models charting brain structure lifespan trajectories and functional connectivity (FC; Rutherford et al. 2022a). Using 3 large data sets, Andre Marquand et al. (2016, 2019) established the normative model through different techniques and found that normative modeling features have the strongest statistically significant results when conducting group

difference statistics and classification tasks. Inspired by recent developments in mapping human brain charts, a recent study proposed that divergence between intra- and inter-person trajectory estimates varies with age and specific brain measures. They found that age-related brain changes inferred from crosssectionally mapped brain charts can substantially underestimate actual changes measured longitudinally (Di Biase et al. 2023). Motivated by this framework, psychiatrists have adopted the normative model in computational psychiatry (Oliveira-Saraiva and Ferreira 2023). In our previous study, deviations are quantified as having a z score with an absolute value larger than a threshold, and we found that compared with healthy controls (HCs), more patients with major depressive disorders showed deviation in the static and dynamic brain states (Lin et al. 2023), and we also identified a subtype of patients with larger extreme deviations from the normal range (Jing et al. 2023).

A previous study suggested that the normative model is suitable for longitudinal measurements in ascertaining brain development trajectories. Several studies have adopted a normative model to depict the distributions of patients with schizophrenia in populations (Wolfers et al. 2018; Elad et al. 2021). However, to our knowledge, no study has used the normative model to investigate the treatment effect in schizophrenia. Numerous studies have investigated the brain changes following both shortand long-term antipsychotic treatment, yet substantial heterogeneity is observed in the therapeutic effect (Zeng et al. 2021). A recently published metaanalysis examining 1280 individuals systematically synthesized and quantified the relationship between resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) and antipsychotic response in schizophrenia. They found that based on pretreatment rsfMRI data, striatal and default mode network (DMN) functional segregation and integration metrics could predict treatment response with relatively high accuracy (Mehta et al. 2021). However, there was vast heterogeneity in the reviewed studies regarding patient characteristics. Recent studies also emphasize the importance of investigating the dynamic fluctuations of the brain networks. Including 140 drug-naïve schizophrenia patients and 119 HCs, a recent study found that patients exhibited impaired functional topology and increased temporal variability in dynamic FC in DMN, visual network (VN), and the somatomotor network (Tang et al. 2023). Another recent study also found altered static and dynamic connectivity properties after rTMS treatment, and the brain changes were associated with reduced severity of clinical symptoms (Xie et al. 2023). Traditionally, schizophrenia was characterized by positive symptoms and negative symptoms. To look at a broader spectrum of psychotic symptoms, and gain new insight into the predictive value of psychotic symptoms, a 5-factor consensus model of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) distinguishes between excited, disorganized, and depressive symptoms in addition to positive and negative symptoms were proposed (Wallwork et al. 2012). In the present study, to better depict the characteristics of different patients, we adopted this 5-factor model.

Previous studies have confirmed the utility of placing individuals into reference models. Motivated by the recent developments in normative models, this investigation aimed: (i) to use the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN) data set to build brain aging trajectories inferred from normative modelsoffunctional MRI data; (ii) to establish the brain-behaviors relationships, decompose the symptoms into 5 dimensions to test whether deviations from these models were related to psychiatric symptoms in patients with schizophrenia; (iii) to further investigated the pharmacological treatment effect on deviation distributions, and predict the deviation changes using baseline clinical variables.

## Materials and methods

## Data set and scanner parameters

Data used in the preparation of this work were obtained from the Cam-CAN repository (available at http://www.mrc-cbu.cam. ac.uk/datasets/camcan/) (Shafto et al. 2014; Taylor et al. 2017) ( n =652). The Cam-CAN is a large population-based study of the healthy adult life span. The analytical strategy was carried out in accordance with guidelines approved by the Cambridge 2 Research Ethics Committee, which also approved the data use protocols. All participants gave full, informed, written consent. The demographic characteristics of the sample are described in Table S1. The scanner parameters were also reported in the Supplementary Materials.

## Demographic data of the patients with schizophrenia

The Ethics Committee of the Peking University Sixth Hospital approved the study. Forty healthy subjects and 42 patients with schizophrenia were enrolled and gave written informed consent according to the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki. Five patients discontinued participation shortly before the first session. For the following data analysis, 37 subjects with a mean age of 28.41 yr (SD = 8.41) were included (for demographic characteristics at baseline, please see Table 1). The symptom severity of all schizophrenia patients was evaluated using the PANSS before and after the treatment by the same psychiatrist. All the patients received mono- or dual-therapy with antipsychotic medications for 6 wk. The antipsychotics were selected according to the individual patients' symptom characteristics. The antipsychotic medications are listed in the Supplementary Material. All patients with schizophrenia underwent 2 experimental sessions. The first session began before beginning antipsychotic treatment. The second session occurred on the last day of 6 wk of antipsychotic treatment. Each healthy volunteer underwent one experimental session.

## Image data acquisition

The Cam-CAN data set provides detailed imaging data acquisition parameters, we also added this information to the Supplementary Materials. As for the data collected from patients with schizophrenia, the data acquisition parameters are presented below. Magnetic resonance imaging was performed using a Siemens Magnetom Trio 3.0 Tesla imaging system with a standard head coil at Peking University Third Hospital. The data collection details were as follows. For image registration, high-resolution structural T1 MRI data were acquired with the following acquisition parameters: 256 × 256 matrix size, 192 contiguous axial slices, 1 mm slice thickness, 1 × 1 × 1 mm 3 voxel resolution, 7 ◦ flip angle, 3.44 ms echo time, 2530 ms repetition time, and 1100 ms inversion time. Resting-state functional scans were obtained using a gradient-recalled echo-planar imaging sequence sensitive to blood oxygen-level-dependent contrast (2000 ms repetition time, 30 ms echo time, and 90 ◦ flip angle). The slice thickness was 4 mm (no gap) with a matrix size of 64 × 64 and a field of view of 220 × 220 mm 2 , resulting in a voxel size of 3.4 × 3.4 × 4.0 mm 3 . Each brain volume comprised 33 axial slices, and each functional run contained 240 image volumes.

## Image data preprocessing

The Cam-CAN provides us with the preprocessing neuroimaging data and the details are in the Supplementary Materials. For the imaging data of patients with schizophrenia and HCs, both structural and functional neuroimaging data were processed using spm toolbox in MATLAB (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). The first 10 scans of the 240 dynamics were removed during the preprocessing. Spatial preprocessing involved reorienting to the imaginary line joining Anterior Commissure-Posterior Commissure, realignment, and slice-time correction. Brain tissue was segmented into gray matter/white matter/cerebrospinal fluid normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute space, and smoothed for spatial convolution with a 6-mm Gaussian kernel.

## Calculate dynamic FC pattern

For each individual, we computed dynamic FC matrices between all pairs of regions of the Human Brainnetome Atlas (Fan et al. 2016) using a sliding time window method (window length=60 s, step size = 1) (Chang and Glover 2010; Hutchison et al. 2013; Allen et al. 2014). Then, each FC matrix was decomposed into 17 networks based on Yeo-17 network template (Yeo et al. 2011). The average FC connection strength (aFCS, mean of FC strength from all window-wise FC matrices) and fluctuation characteristic of connection strength (fFCS, average change of the windowwise FC strength) for each Yeo-17 network was yielded from time-varying dynamic FC matrices. Finally, each individual's dynamic FC pattern was represented by 34 features.

## Normative modeling for dynamic FC features

Based on the large public data set Cam-CAN, we estimated sexspecific normative age models for each dynamic FC feature. We used quantile linear regression to estimate the normative range (Allen et al. 2014; Jing et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2023). To quantify the normative model, we estimated the 50th (median) percentile regression curves with bootstrapping confidence intervals. To avoid bias in the model, we computed the normative range by estimating the 5th and 95th percentiles in regression models. The gap between the 5th and 95th percentiles for a given age was as a scaling coefficient to normalize the feature deviation (Jing et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2023). To avoid the potential sex effects on model construction, we separately estimated the normative model in female/male groups. Furthermore, to improve the generalizability of the model, we estimated the normative models of healthy individuals from Cam-CAN under 10-fold cross-validation. Specifically, the data were randomly divided into 10 folds. Nine-folds were utilized for building the model, whereas the remaining fold was employed to evaluate the general performance of the model. This process was repeated 10 times, with each fold being excluded once. Finally, 10 models were aggregated into normative models for dynamic FC features.

## Characterizing bSCZ and aSCZ based on individual deviations and clinical response

General linear models and Pearson's correlations of the deviation profiles on bSCZ/aSCZ and clinical PANSS scores were conducted to explore the relationships between clinical symptom improvement and changes in dynamic FC patterns gaged with the deviation Z -scores. We used permutation tests ( n =5,000) to conduct the Pearson correlations to avoid false positive issues with sex, age, and head motion as covariates. The permutation correlation analysis is non-parametric, distribution-free, and particularly beneficial when dealing with a small sample size.

## Results

## Demographic information of participants

Table 1 shows the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. The data in the analysis consist of 37 adults with schizophrenia and 44 HCs. The data from the Cam-CAN data set were used to construct the sex-specific normative age model (as shown in Fig. 1C). And the comparison was made between the healthy comparison group and the patient's group. We also compared the distribution of the patients before and after 6-wk treatment.

## The establishment of the normative model based on the Cam-CAN dataset

We adopted a certainty measure to evaluate the deviation reliability for each subject based on the 10-fold cross-validation normative model. To this end, the proportion of healthy individuals with supra-deviation (residing above 95% percentiles in normative models) was 2.75% for the aFCS features, and 2.91% for fFCS features. And the proportions with infra-deviation (below the 5% percentiles in normative models) for the aFCS and fFCS were 2.82% and 2.92%, respectively. We used a normative certainty index to quantify the performance of normative models. The normative certainty was measured by max ( np n , nn n ) , where np and nn were the number of positive or negative Z -scores, and n is 10. This index assessed the positive and negative distribution of

Z -scores from 10-fold cross-validations, and a higher value indicated higher reliability of normative models. In this study, the average certainty for all participants from Cam-CAN was 0.991 for aFCS and 0.990 for fFCS, indicating that the normative models we built were reliable.

## Modeling the heterogeneity of dynamic network characteristics

To directly compare the deviation changes after treatment, the heatmaps were created to show the deviation scores across 17 brain networks at the individual level (see Fig. 2A for aFCS and Fig. 2B for fFCS). The results suggested that the deviations in the brain networks' fluctuation significantly decreased after treatment. However, we failed to detect the pattern changes after treatment when analyzing the deviations of the averaged FC. Figure 2C shows the coefficients of variation (the ratio of the standard deviation to the absolute mean value) at network-level for bSCZ and aSCZ. Comparing the variability of aFCS after treatment with baseline, the coefficients of variation decreased after treatment for most of the brain networks, indicating that the networks of the patients converged to the mean state (the drug made them develop in one tendency). According to the changes in the deviations of the dynamic brain characteristics, we divided the patients into 2 groups: the normalization group, whose brain deviation networks decreased after treatment, and the deviated group, whose brain deviations became more distinct after treatment.Wefoundthatatbaseline,for fFCS,the normalization group has milder severity of the positive ( P =0.001, effect size = -1.21) and overall symptoms ( P =0.03, effect size = -0.76). These results suggested that the brain dynamic characteristics of those with more severe symptoms are more likely to be deviated from the normal range.

## The correlation between the clinical symptoms and the deviations

First, we pooled the baseline and after-treatment data together to get a correlation between the deviations in brain characteristics with the symptoms. As for the FC strength (Fig. 3), the deviations of the sensorimotor network showed a significant positive correlation with positive and overall symptoms, and deviations of the ventral attention network showed a significant positive correlation with the overall symptoms. Only sub-DMNs showed a positive correlation with the negative symptoms. In contrast, the deviations of the brain network's dynamic fluctuations, including all of the identified brain networks, all positively correlated with the severity of the negative symptoms.

At baseline, we found negative correlations between the deviation of aFCS in VNs with positive symptoms, and the deviation in dorsal attention network, control network, and VN with disorganization symptoms (as shown in Fig. S2). However, after treatment, the correlation disappeared. At baseline, the deviations of the brain network's fFCS, especially the VN, semantic-motor network, dorsal attention network, limbic network, and default network, positively correlated with the severity of the negative symptoms, and the control network and DMN negatively correlated with disorganized symptoms (as shown in Fig. S2). However, after treatment, the negative symptoms were positively correlated with the dynamic fluctuations deviations of the different subnetworks of dorsal attention and semantic-motor and limbic network, as shown in Fig. S3. Furthermore, the fFCS deviations of the control and sub-DMN network were positively correlated with the overall symptoms (Fig. S3).

## Baseline clinical symptoms could predict the deviations after treatment

We further analyzed the predictive effect of baseline clinical symptoms for the change of deviations in patients. For aFCS, the severity of depressive symptoms at baseline could negatively predict the deviation changes as shown in Fig. 4. These results suggested that, the brain networks of those patients with milder depressive symptoms were more likely to be placed back to the normal range in clinical therapy. And the most significant brain networks were the ventral and dorsal attentional and somatomotor networks. In addition, we also found a negative correlation between the overall symptoms with the deviations of the subDMN, which also validated the proposal that with more severe symptoms, the network's deviations were more likely to be more distinct after treatment.

As for the dynamic fluctuations in Fig. 5, the severity of overall symptoms and the positive symptoms at baseline could negatively predict the dynamic fluctuations deviations of dorsal and ventral attention, salience, control, and DMN.

## Discussion

This is the first study to adopt a normative model to assess brain changes affected by pharmacological treatment in patients with schizophrenia. First, we build brain aging trajectories inferred from normative models of functional MRI data using the CamCAN data set. We found that the patients can be divided into 2 groups: the first group showed a significant normalization trend, the other group showed a more severe deviation after treatment, and the normalized group had milder symptoms at baseline. Then, we found that the average FCS and dynamic fluctuations deviations of the sub-DMN positively correlated with positive symptoms.Furthermore,the baseline severity of symptoms, especially the depressive and the overall symptoms, could predict the deviation of the brain networks after treatment.

We detected that patient with schizophrenia exceeded the normative ranges by a great number for brain networks' dynamic characteristics than the healthy comparison individuals (see Fig. S1). The average deviations of the brain networks of the patients are higher than HCs, which is consistent with the hypothesis that schizophrenia is a brain disease, there are significant brain changes relative to HCs. A previous study also found that patients with schizophrenia showed more infranormal deviations for at least one brain region than comparable controls when analyzing brain structures (Lv et al. 2020). In the present study, the patients could be divided into 2 groups: the normalized group, whose brain characteristics returned to the normal range after treatment, and the deviation group, whose brain characteristics became more distinct after treatment. We also found that the normalized group had milder symptoms than the deviation group, especially the positive and overall symptoms. Unlike previous studies, which proposed that the negative symptoms could better predict the treatment effect (Glenthøj et al. 2020), the present study tried to predict the changes in brain characteristics after treatment. It is worth noting that we also found antipsychotic treatment decreased the deviations of the average FC strength but not so much the dynamic fluctuation characteristics. Previous findings suggest that the static FC was normalized after treatment, along with the improvement of clinical symptoms in schizophrenia (Mehta et al. 2021). However, the effect of treatment on dynamic FC varies across different studies. A previous study revealed that symptom reduction was correlated with increased FC variability

|

(Zhang et al. 2021). Another study reported that the decreased FC variability between ventral posterior-lateral and right medial orbital superior frontal gyrus correlated with the reduction of PANSS scores after treatment (Yang et al. 2023). In the present study, we could not reach the conclusion that the treatment normalized the static FC, since the decreased coefficient of variation for the deviations only means that the population is close to the group-level mean value. The results of the present study and those inconsistent findings regarding the dynamic FC changes with antipsychotic treatment call for future studies to focus on the dynamic FC in patients with schizophrenia.

When pooling the data from baseline and after treatment, we found that the deviations of aFCS and fFCS of a sub-DMN showed a positive correlation with the negative symptoms. Previous studies also emphasized the relationship between DMN and negative symptoms (Hare et al. 2019). DMN has been reported with heterogeneous alterations in patients with schizophrenia (He et al. 2013; Kraguljac et al. 2016; Conio et al. 2020). For the dynamic fluctuations, we found negative correlations between the negative symptoms with the deviations of the dynamic fluctuations in all of the identified brain networks. Most of the studies focused on positive symptoms, the correlation between the dynamic FC of brain networks and the negative symptoms brings new perspectives for future studies. At baseline, we found a negative correlation between the somato-motor and dorsal attention networks with depressed symptoms, and this correlation disappeared after treatment. Although only a few studies focused on the depressive symptoms in schizophrenia, it is a commonsymptom,with up to 80% of patients with schizophrenia experiencing depressive episodes (Bosanac and Castle 2013; Zhuo et al. 2020). Previous studies focused on the brain network changes in depression and schizophrenia also have reported common large-scale brain network changes, including the essential node in attention and DMNs (Chang et al. 2018). However, the characterization of the brain imaging signatures of schizophrenia patients with depressive symptoms still remains to be investigated.

In addition, the baseline clinical symptoms could predict the deviation changes of the averaged FC and the dynamic fluctuations after treatment. Especially, with milder depressive symptoms,the static brain characteristics were more likely to be placed back to the normal range, such as the VN, sensorimotor network, and attentional network. In contrast, with milder positive symptoms,the deviation changes of the FCs' dynamic fluctuation in the dorsal attention network, limbic network, control network, and sub-DMN were more likely to be placed in the normal range.While controversial,previous studies found that depression scores positively correlated to favorable treatment outcomes in patients with schizophrenia (Jäger et al. 2008; Kopelowicz et al. 2008; Colasanti et al. 2010). In the present study, the milder depressive symptoms positively correlated with brain normalization. Studies over the past 5 yr have shown that depressive symptoms play a destructive role in the prognosis of patients with schizophrenia, decreasing treatment efficacy (Fusar-Poli et al. 2013). In addition, the overall symptom severity is also negatively correlated with the fFCS deviations after treatment, including the dorsal and ventral attention network, salience network, control network, and DMN. These results suggested that with milder symptom severity, the brain networks were more likely to return to the normal range after treatment, this is consistent with previous studies, suggesting that those with more severe symptoms were more likely to be treatment resistant (Ortiz et al. 2020).

Our findings should be interpreted cautiously since there are a few limitations to consider. First, the sample size was small, which is a limitation. Second, we had to use 10-fold cross-validation in healthy individuals and out-of-sample predictions in individuals with schizophrenia, as our healthy sample was too small to split into 2 independent samples. This potentially introduces a small bias. Third, although all the patients underwent a 2-wk drug eluting period, the potential confounding effect also influenced our results.

## Conclusion

In the present study, we adopt a normative model based on the Cam-CAN database, to assess brain changes affected by pharmacological treatment in patients with schizophrenia. We found that the dynamin fluctuation deviations in patients with milder positive and overall symptoms were more likely to return to normal ranges after treatment. Then, we found that the symptoms significantly correlated with large-scale brain networks' average FCS and dynamic fluctuations deviations. Furthermore, the baseline severity of symptoms, especially the depressive and the overall symptoms,could predict the deviation of the brain networks after treatment. Our study suggested that normative modeling will be broadly applied to clinical conditions, which could deepen our understanding of the heterogeneity in mental illnesses.
