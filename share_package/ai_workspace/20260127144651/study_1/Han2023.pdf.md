Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a spectrum disorder with high interindividual heterogeneity. We propose a comprehensible framework integrating normative model and non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to quantitatively estimate the neuroanatomical heterogeneity of OCD from a dimensional perspective. T1-weighted magnetic resonance images of 98 first-episode untreated patients with OCDandmatchedhealthycontrols(HCs, n = 130) were acquired.We derived individualized differences in gray matter morphometry using normative model and parsed them into latent disease factors using NMF. Four robust disease factors were identified. Each patient expressed multiple factors and exhibited a unique factor composition.Factor compositions of patients were significantly correlated with severity of symptom,age of onset,illness duration,and exhibited sex differences,capturing sources of clinical heterogeneity.In addition, the group-level morphological differences obtained with two-sample t test could be quantitatively derived from the identified disease factors, reconciling the group-level and subject-level findings in neuroimaging studies. Finally, we uncovered two distinct subtypes with opposite morphological differences compared with HCs from factor compositions. Our findings suggest that morphological differences of individuals with OCD are the unique combination of distinct neuroanatomical patterns. The proposed framework quantitatively estimating neuroanatomical heterogeneity paves the way for precision medicine in OCD.

Key words : gray matter volume; individualized analysis; subtypes of OCD; OCD.

## Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is characterized by obsession and compulsion (Mataix-Cols et al. 2004). Typically, individuals with OCD exhibit tremendous heterogeneity to the extent that two individuals with this diagnosis can exhibit totally different non-overlapping symptom profiles, bringing out a wellaccepted notion that OCD is a heterogeneous disorder (MataixCols et al. 2005). The heterogeneity is reflected in multifarious genetic architectures, symptom profiles, neuroimaging representations, and treatment responses (Han et al.; Alexander et al. 1986; McKay et al. 2004; Mahjani et al. 2021; Han, Xu, Guo, Fang, Wei, et al. 2022a, 2022b, 2022e). In neuroimaging studies adopting case-control approaches,the notable individualized heterogeneity turns out to be one of the leading obstacles toward reproducible biomarkers indicative of clinical decision-making (Saxena and Rauch 2000; Boedhoe et al. 2018). The main reason is that case-control approaches are designed to probe group-level differences while ignore the individual variations (Wolfers et al. 2018). The derived group-level differences are only representative of a fraction of patients, essentially representing an 'average patient' (Wolfers et al. 2018). A growing body of study recognizes the high interindividual heterogeneity and attempts to resolve it in mental disorders including OCD (Brugger and Howes 2017; Han et al. 2021; Han, Xu, Guo, Fang, Wei, et al. 2022a, 2022b, 2022e).

OCD is a spectrum disorder of potentially overlapping syndromes co-occurring in one patient (Mataix-Cols et al. 2005). To resolve the heterogeneity of OCD, clinical psychiatrists parse OCD symptoms into multiple potential symptom dimensions (Mataix-Cols et al. 2005; Bloch et al. 2008). For example, symptoms of OCD are assumed to consist of four symptom dimensions: contamination/washing, aggressive/checking, symmetry/ordering, and hoarding (Mataix-Cols et al. 2005; Bloch et al. 2008). studies reveal that OCD symptom dimensions are linked to distinct patterns of comorbidities (Hasler et al. 2005), genetic polymorphisms(Hasler et al.2006),impairments in distinct neural circuits (Mataix-Cols et al. 2004), and differential responses to pharmacological and nonpharmacological treatments (MataixCols et al. 1999; Mataix-Cols et al. 2002). Although the dimensional approaches based on symptomatology show potential benefits in identifying neural markers relate to illness course and treatment response, several controversies to their implementation remain (Gilbert et al. 2009). (i) The correct number of factors and the appropriate factor structure for OCD are still controversial (Summerfeldt et al. 1999; Bloch et al. 2008). (ii) Similar clinical features or symptom patterns can be caused by different underlying mechanisms, limiting these features' usefulness to differentiate etiologically distinct patients (Hasler 2010). (iii) The dimensional approach based on symptomatology has a vague diagnostic threshold in terms of how to handle sub-threshold symptoms, and some patients may not clearly belong to any subtype (Okada et al. 2015). (iv) More seriously, subtypes based on symptomatology may not be stable over time (Lahey et al. 2005). Recently, researches turn to performing dimensional analyses on objective neuroanatomical data such as structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data to resolve the heterogeneity in neuropsychiatric disorders including depression, autism, epilepsy, and Alzheimer's disease (Zhang et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2022; Lee et al. 2022; Shan et al. 2022).

A number of approaches are put forward to resolve the morphological heterogeneity of gray matter in neuropsychiatric disorders using objective MRI data. Normative model can infer individualized neuroimaging differences of a patient by measuring its deviates from the healthy distribution (Marquand et al. 2016). Using this method, researchers successfully quantify structural brain heterogeneity at subject level in mental disorders (Wolfers et al. 2018; Zabihi et al. 2019; Wolfers and Beckmann 2020). For example, using the normative model, Wolfers et al. find that the individualized gray matter morphological differences in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder would be concealed by traditional case-control designs, providing a step toward a systematic mapping of the heterogeneity of mental disorders (Wolfers et al. 2018). The similar conclusions are found in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (Wolfers and Beckmann 2020) and autism spectrum disorder (Zabihi et al. 2019). Individualized neuroimaging differences further help to identify more homogeneous subtypes with distinct (even opposite) neuroanatomical differences profiles (Liu et al. 2021; Shan et al. 2022) and clinical manifestations, reconciling the conflicting findings of neuroimaging studies and facilitating personalized clinical decision-making in mental disorders (Han, Xu, Guo, Fang, Wei, et al. 2022a, 2022b, 2022e; Han, Zheng, et al. 2022; Shan et al. 2022). However, these pioneer studies do not provide effective methods to deal with the high-dimensional results derived from the normative model (Wolfers et al. 2018; Wolfers and Beckmann 2020). Additionally, the quantitative association between subjectlevel differences derived from the normative model and grouplevel differences are not well elucidated. In other words, previous studies cannot explain how subject-level differences contribute to the observed group-level differences, leading to a lack of interpretability of subject-level differences.

To quantitatively estimate the heterogeneity of OCD, we proposed a novel framework integrating the normative model and non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) from a dimensional perspective. This framework obtained subject-level differences in gray matter morphometry measured with voxel-based morphometry analysis (VBM) using the normative model

(Ashburner and Friston 2000) and parsed them into latent disease factors using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF). As a promising method widely used in MRI studies, NMF decomposed high-dimensional data into a factor matrix (latent disease factors) and a weight matrix (factor compositions) (Anderson et al. 2014; Shan et al. 2022). Thus, individualized morphological differences were represented as a linear combination of the identified disease factors. The framework assigned each patient a unique factor composition, thus retaining interindividual variability. We posited that each patient with OCD expressed multiple latent disease factors rather than one single factor. In this study, we would show that factor compositions were associated with clinical characteristics capturing sources of clinical heterogeneity. We further showed that our framework could significantly infer factor compositions of unseen patients. What' more, we demonstrated that group-level morphological differences could be quantitatively derived from the identified disease factors, reconciling the group-level and individualized findings in neuroimaging studies. Finally, we uncovered two putative OCD subtypes with opposite morphological differences compared with HCs from factor compositions.

## Methods

## Sample

This study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the research ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Every participant signed an informed consent before the experiment.

Ninety-eight first-episode and drug-naive patients with OCD were recruited from outpatient services of the Department of Psychiatry, the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. The diagnosis was made by two experienced psychiatrists independently according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-V) for OCD. The symptom severity was evaluated by the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) (Goodman et al. 1989). None of the patients had nervous system disease, brain trauma, comorbid, other mental/psychotic disorders, first-degree relatives with a history of mental disorders, or neurological diseases. One hundred and thirty healthy controls (HCs) were recruited from the community through poster advertisement. In addition, all participants were Han Chinese, righthanded,andhadtomeetthefollowing exclusion criteria: (i) taking drugs such as anesthetics and analgesics in the past 1 month; (ii) substance abuse; (iii) a history of a brain tumor, trauma, surgery, or other organic body diseases; (iv) suffering from cardiovascular diseases, diabetes or hypertension; (v) contraindications for MRI scanning; and (vi) other structural brain abnormalities.

## Data acquisition

T1-weights MRI scans were acquired using 3-Tsela GE Discovery MR750 scanner (General Electric, Fairfield Connecticut, USA) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. The 3D T1-weights scans were acquired as follows: repetition time (TR) = 8164 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.18 ms, flip angle = 7 ◦ , resolution matrix = 256 × 256, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm 3 , flip angle = 7 ◦ , slices = 188, thickness = 1.0 mm.

## Voxel-based morphometry analysis

We obtained GMV for each subject using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12, http://dbm.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat12/). Main steps included: (i) segmenting images into gray matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid; (ii) normalizing segmented images into Montreal Neurological Institute space and resampled them to 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm; (iii) nonlinear modulation; and (iv)

the gray matter images were smoothed with an 6-mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel (Ashburner 2009; Han et al. 2021). We also calculated the total intracranial volume (TIV) for the following analysis. The Image Quality Rating (IQR) combing image noise contrast ratio, inhomogeneity contrast ratio and root mean square was recorded to assess images quality (Brown et al. 2019; Han, Xu, Guo, Fang, Wei, et al. 2022a, 2022b, 2022e).

## Modeling latent disease factors

Combing the normative model and NMF, we proposed a framework that allowed one patient to express multiple latent factors rather than assigned it to a single category. The main assumption of our framework was that each patient with OCD was a combination of multiple latent disease factors. There were two main steps in modeling disease factors.

First, we obtained personalized morphological differences for each patient with OCD using the normative model by evaluating

its deviates from the normal distribution. Following the previous studies (Marquand et al. 2016; Wolfers et al. 2018), we trained a Gaussian process regression model to predict voxel-wise GMV from age and sex in HCs and then applied the trained model to infer GMV of patients with OCD. For each voxel, the Z -score was obtained by quantifying the deviation between the predicted GMV andthetrueonethennormalizedbytheuncertaintyof prediction. The positive Z -scores suggested increased GMVs than expected in patients than HCs and vice versa. We divided voxel-wise morphological differences into increased and decreased parts according to Z -scores. For decreased parts, the Z -scores were multiplied by -1. The following step was performed in increased and decreased parts separately.

Then, we identified the potential disease factors from individualized morphological differences using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF). As a data-driven multivariate method, NMF produced a sparse, parts-based representation of the data under non-negativity constraints (Lee and Seung 1999). Compared with independent component analysis (ICA) and principal component analysis (PCA), the results of NMF were more explanatory (Sotiras et al. 2017). In addition, NMF yielded a soft (probabilistic) parcellation of voxel-wise aberrance and was proved to accurately identify subject-specific functional networks (Li et al. 2017) and reveal individualized coordinated patterns of cortical development during adolescence (Sotiras et al. 2017 be expressed as the following formula:

D = W × H + /epsilon1 W , H &gt; 0.

In this study, D represented Z -scores matrix (subject × voxel), H represented the disease factors (the number of latent factors K × voxel), W represented the corresponding weights (factor compositions) of subjects (subject number × K ), and /epsilon1 was the residuals. The number of latent disease factors ( K ) needed to be pre-defined.

Inspired by the previous study (Lee et al. 2022), we proposed a strategy to automatically determine the optimal K from 2 to 6. To be more specific, for each k , we ran NMF 100 times as the algorithm depended on random initialization and the final estimate was obtained by selecting the solution closet to the remaining 99 estimates across runs (Berry et al. 2007). The corresponding factors were matched through the Hungarian matching algorithm (Zhang et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2022). The optimal K was determined when stability value (the averaged pairwise correlation across all latent factors) reached the maximum across runs. The corresponding factors and weights with the optimal K were recorded.

## Association between factor compositions and clinical characteristics

To associate the identified disease factors and clinical characteristics, the Pearson's correlation coefficients between factor compositions and clinical characteristics including illness duration, age of onset and symptom severity were calculated. We also investigate whether there were significant differences between female and male patients in terms of factor compositions using two sample t -test.

## Robust analysis

To exclude the chance that the identified disease factors were driven by a few patients, we identified disease factors on randomly selected 80% of patients with the optimal K (100 times). The Pearson's correlations between factors using all patients and those using a subset of patients were obtained where the corresponding factors were matched through the Hungarian matching algorithm (Zhang et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2022).

## Examining the extendibility of our framework on unseen patients

To examine the extendibility of our framework, we investigated whether the identified disease factors could be used to infer factor compositions of unseen patients using leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV). For each validation, disease factors were obtained from training dataset and then were used to infer factor compositions of the patient in test dataset using a linear regression model. The coefficients of the regression model were treated as its factor compositions. The Pearson's correlations between the predicted factor compositions and the true ones (obtained using all patients) were calculated. The significance was evaluated using permutation testing (1,000 times).

## Association with the results of group-level analysis

Then, we investigated how the individualized differences contributed to the observed group-level differences. The group-level differences were obtained with two sample t test where age, sex, and TIV were included as covariates. This procedure was done using SPM12 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). We constructed a linear regression model to predict the group-level differences (unthresholded T map) from the identified 4 disease factors. Here, we supposed that the group-level differences were representative of an 'average' patient and the coefficients of the regression model were assumed as its factor compositions.

## Clustering analysis

Finally, to further verify whether the identified disease factors could capture clinical heterogeneity in OCD,we performed affinity propagation clustering to uncover putative subtypes of OCD from factor compositions (Frey and Dueck 2007).

Using affinity propagation clustering, patients with OCD were clustered into 2 subtypes from factor compositions ( n =35 for subtype 1, n =63 for subtype 2). Then, we performed two-sample t test to investigate GMV differences of these 2 subtypes compared with HCs controlling age, sex, and TIV using SPM 12. As a result, subtype 1 exhibited significantly increased GMVs while subtype 2 exhibited significantly decreased GMVs in distributed brain regions than HCs (FDR corrected P &lt; 0.05, cluster size &gt; 100 voxels). In accordance with these findings, we found that sex differences were reflected in differences weight in positive factor 2 and increased weights of negative factor 2. Age of onset was another confounder hampering unified findings in OCD. Patients with OCD were usually further divided into early-onset and late-onset subtypes according to the illness onset, and these two subtypes exhibited differences in genetic load, symptom profiles, comorbidities, treatment responses, and neuroimaging differences (Taylor 2011; Anholt et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2020). In neuroimaging studies, researchers found that early-onset patients tended to exhibited larger volumes in distributed brain regions than lateonset patients, possibly reflecting an increased environmental sensitivity, delayed dendritic pruning, or compensatory changes (Nickson et al. 2016; Blank and Meyer 2022). Correspondingly, in our results, the weights of positive factor 2 showed significant negative correlation with age of onset, suggesting the earlier illness, the larger gray matter volume in OCD than healthy population. In addition, weights of the identified disease factors were significantly correlated with the severity of symptom and illness duration, pronouncing the identified disease factors were relevant to neuronal substrates and potential neuroplastic changes as the disease prolonged.

Using the same dataset but different approaches, our previous works identified two putative OCD subtypes with opposite morphological differences compared with normal population (Han, Xu, Guo, Fang and Wei 2022c, 2022d). Consistent with these results, we uncovered two neuroanatomical OCD subtypes exhibiting opposite morphological differences in distributed brain regions. When mixed together, patients with OCD did not demonstrate significant differences compared with HCs. These results accounted for the conflicting findings in neuroimaging studies. Previous studies reported increased, decreased, and no differential gray matter volume in brain regions within and beyond the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit in OCD (Okasha et al. 2000; Lázaro et al. 2011). The inconsistent results were mainly attributed to confounders such as medical exposure and comorbidity; our results argued that the inconsistent results were relevant to the high heterogeneity in OCD. After these confounders were well controlled, patients with OCD were still divided into two subtypes with opposite morphological differences. All these results argued that group-level differences were not representative of most patients (Wolfers et al. 2018; Lv et al. 2020; Wolfers and Beckmann 2020; Liu et al. 2021) and the identified disease factors could capture sources of clinical heterogeneity in OCD. Although these two subtypes demonstrated remarkably opposite gray matter volume differences, they did not exhibit any significant differences in clinical characteristics. This result hinted that there might be potential subtypes with similar clinical characterization but different neuroanatomical substrates, providing a new

|

insight into taxonomy and facilitate potential clues to precision diagnosis and treatment of OCD.

Although a number of methods inferring personalized differences were proposed to resolve the heterogeneity, the derived subject-level differences were hard to understand (Marquand et al. 2016; Han, Xu, Guo, Fang, Wei, et al. 2022a, 2022b, 2022e; Han, Zheng , et al. 2022). In this study, we showed that group-level differences were only representative of the 'average' patient (red circle in Fig. 4C). What's more, our framework derived more explanatory individualized differences by showing that group-level differences could be quantitatively derived from the identified disease factors, reconciling the group-level and subjectlevel findings in neuroimaging studies.

Finally, we summarized the advantages of our framework. First, our framework did not need a pre-defined number of disease factors. Second, instead of assigning patients to nonoverlapping subtypes, our framework quantitatively estimated the heterogeneity by identifying latent disease factors from a dimensional perspective. We assumed that patients with OCD expressed multiple factors other than predominantly expressed a single disease factor and the heterogeneity across patients was reflected in the various factor compositions. On the assumption,our framework assigned each patient a unique factor composition, thus retaining interindividual variability (Tang et al. 2020).Third,our framework yielded more comprehensible individualized differences, reconciling the group-level and subject-level differences. Forth, our framework showed potential translational foreground as it could infer factor compositions of unseen patients.

There were several limitations should be considered. First, the sample size was modest. However, we carefully excluded the chance that our results were driven by a few patients. Another independent dataset was needed to validate our results. Second, we only included first-episode treatment-naive patients. Further studies should take into account more heterogeneous samples, such as patients with recurrent episodes/relapses or with common psychiatric comorbidities. Third, alcohol and tobacco were not controlled, and future studies should examine their effects on the latent disease factors. Fourth, we did not record the obsessive dimensions. Thus, we could not investigate the association between the identified disease factors and symptom dimensions. Future studies should examine this relationship.

## Conclusion

To quantitatively estimate the heterogeneity in OCD, we proposed a framework integrating the normative model and NMF. Our framework obtained structural brain differences at subject level then parsed them into latent disease factors from a dimensional perspective. Using the framework, we identified 4 robust latent disease factors. Each patient with OCD expressed multiple factors and exhibited a unique factor composition, capturing sources of clinical heterogeneity. We also found that the group-level differences using traditional case-control approaches could be derived from the identified disease factors, reconciling the group-level and subject-level findings in neuroimaging studies. In addition, our framework could infer factor compositions of unseen patients in a cross-validation manner. Finally, the weights of disease factors identified two putative subtypes with opposite neuroanatomical differences compared with healthy controls. Our framework provides a new insight into resolving the neuroanatomical heterogeneity and paves the way for precision medicine in OCD.
