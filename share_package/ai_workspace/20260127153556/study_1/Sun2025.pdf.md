## 1. Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD), a widespread and intricate mental health  condition  affecting  nearly  300  million  people  worldwide  (Cui et al., 2024), is characterized by a wide range of complex symptoms. These include persistent sadness, anhedonia, as well as severe physical and  cognitive  impairments,  which  can  present  in  various  symptom combinations among different individuals (Marx et al., 2023; Milaneschi et al., 2021). A significant challenge in understanding MDD lies in its heterogeneity  that  manifests  as  variations  in  clinical  presentations, treatment responses, and underlying brain mechanisms (Kennis et al., 2020;  Toenders  et  al.,  2020). ## A B S T R A C T

Background: Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent and intricate mental health condition characterized by a wide range of symptoms. A fundamental challenge in understanding MDD lies in elucidating the brain mechanisms underlying the complexity and diversity of these symptoms, particularly the heterogeneity reflected in individual differences and subtype variations within brain networks.

Methods: To address this problem, we explored the brain network topology using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data from a cohort of 797 MDD patients and 822 matched healthy controls (HC). Utilizing normative modeling of HC, we quantified individual deviations in brain network degree centrality among  MDD  patients.  Through  k-means  clustering  of  these  deviation  profiles,  we  identified  two  clinically meaningful MDD subtypes. Moreover, we employed Neurosynth to analyze the cognitive correlates of these subtypes.

Results: Subtype 1 exhibited positive deviations of degree centrality in the limbic (LIM), frontoparietal (FPN), and default mode networks (DMN), but negative deviations in the visual (VIS) and sensorimotor networks (SMN), positively correlating with higher cognitive functions and negatively with basic perceptual processes. In contrast, subtype 2 demonstrated opposing patterns, characterized by negative deviations in degree centrality of the LIM, FPN, and DMN and positive deviations of the VIS and SMN, along with inverse cognitive associations.

Conclusions: Our findings underscore the heterogeneity within MDD, revealing two distinct patterns of network topology between unimodal and transmodal networks, offering a valuable reference for personalized diagnosis and treatment strategies.

Moreover, this approach of averaging individual differences may also obscure or hide distinct subtypes with unique neurobiological characteristics (Jing et al., 2023).

Normative modeling has emerged as a promising tool to overcome these limitations by quantifying abnormalities at the individual rather than group level (Marquand et al., 2019; Rutherford et al., 2022). This approach constructs normative standards based on large-scale neuroimaging datasets of healthy populations and leverages statistical models to map the expected range of brain function or structure at a particular age, allowing for the identification of deviations in individual patients relative to their corresponding normative models (Remiszewski et al., 2022; Rutherford et al., 2022). Normative modeling has been successfully applied to explore the heterogeneity of various neuropsychiatric disorders.  For  example,  using  normative  modeling,  researchers  have successfully revealed high heterogeneity in anatomical loci of schizophrenia-related changes (Lv et al., 2021). Another study has also found that the brain functional phenotypes and cognitive decline trajectories were greatly heterogeneous in Alzheimer's disease and identified  two  subtypes  based  on  their  individual  deviation  patterns  (Huo et  al.,  2024).  These  studies  have  demonstrated  that  deviations  from normative models can effectively characterize differences between individual  patients  and  healthy  controls,  as  well  as  among  patients themselves,  and  can  be  used  to  identify  potential  neurobiological subtypes.

In this study, we aimed to dissect the heterogeneity in MDD through normative model-driven subtyping of functional brain networks. Utilizing rs-fMRI data from a cohort of 797 MDD patients and 822 matched healthy  controls,  we  investigated  individual  deviations  in  functional brain network topology. By applying k-means clustering to deviation profiles derived from normative models of healthy brains, we sought to identify distinct MDD subtypes characterized by their unique functional brain network patterns. Moreover, we employed Neurosynth, a platform for large-scale automated synthesis of fMRI data (Yarkoni et al., 2011), to explore the cognitive correlates of these subtypes, gaining insights into how brain alterations may affect cognition. We hypothesized that MDD patients exhibited heterogeneity in the topological structure of functional brain networks, and there were potential MDD subtypes, each with  distinct  neurobiological  mechanisms,  clinical  symptoms,  and cognitive functions.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Participants

The sample data were derived from 1619 participants, encompassing 797  individuals  diagnosed  with  MDD  (mean  age:  35.167 ± 11.950 years, 289 males and 508 females) and 822 healthy controls (HC, mean age: 35.045 ± 13.336 years, 331 males and 491 females), see Fig. S1. These data were obtained from a publicly accessible dataset from the REST-meta-MDD project  (Yan et  al.,  2019)  provided  by  the  DIRECT consortium (Chen et al., 2022). Site 4 and Site 25 were excluded since Site 4's participants were duplicated with those from Site 14, and Site 25 contained an excessive number of late-onset depression cases (age &gt; 60 years) and remitted patients. We applied exclusion criteria for all participants as follows: 1) lack of information on sex, age, or education; 2) age &lt; 18 years or &gt; 65 years; 3) excessive head motion [mean framewise displacement (FD) &gt; 0.2 mm]. MDD patients were further excluded if their 17-item Hamilton Rating Scale for depression (HAMD) scores &lt; 8 points. We also removed participants with imaging distortions by visual inspection or with imaging signal lost in any region of interest (ROI) obtained from the cortical areas of the Harvard-Oxford atlas (Desikan et al., 2006; Kennedy et al., 1998). In addition, we did not include the sites with fewer than 10 MDD patients or 10 HC subjects to ensure that each site had enough MDD and HCs, thus balancing the objectives of optimizing the overall sample size and minimizing extreme biases. MDD diagnoses adhered to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders,  Fourth  Edition  (DSM-IV)  or  International  Classification  of Diseases  10  (ICD-10)  criteria.  The  research  protocol  followed  the Declaration of Helsinki Ethical Principles and received approval from the local Institutional Review Boards (IRB) at each site. All participants provided the IRB-approved, written informed consent before engaging in the study procedures at their local institutions.

## 2.2. Brain network analysis

Brain image processing was performed according to a standardized protocol using the DPARSF software (http://www.rfmri.org/). The first ten functional volumes were discarded to allow for signal equilibration and environmental adaptation. The remaining data were corrected for the  time shifts  among different  acquisitions  within each  volume and then realigned to the first volume for head motion correction. The corrected  images  were  normalized  into  standard  Montreal  Neurological Institute space (resampling voxel size = 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm). The nuisance  regression  was  conducted  to  remove  Friston-24  motion  parameters, white matter signals, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) signals. Temporal bandpass filtering was applied at a frequency range of 0.01 to 0.10 Hz.

In this study, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted on the average time courses between pairs of 96 brain regions defined by the Harvard-Oxford  atlas,  with  coefficients  normalized  using  Fisher's-z transformation.  This  procedure  generated  a  96 × 96 functional  connectivity  (FC)  matrix  for  each  participant  to  facilitate  subsequent network topological analysis. To assess the robustness of our findings across different brain atlases, we replicated the analysis using the AAL90 atlas (details in Supplementary Materials). Degree centrality measures direct connections between network nodes without prior assumptions, which prevents deviations from actual data caused by subjective selection  of  brain  regions  (Bullmore  and  Sporns,  2009;  Jia  et  al.,  2023). Following previous research, a network sparsity threshold ranging from 0.05 to 0.50, at 0.01 intervals, was adopted (Larivi ` ere  et  al.,  2022). Degree centrality for each brain region was calculated at every sparsity threshold  using  the  Graph  Theoretical  Network  Analysis  Toolbox (GRETNA, http://www.nitrc.org/projects/gretna), with the area under the curve (AUC) across all thresholds defined as a summarized scalar of network  indicators.  AUC  was  chosen  for  further  statistical  analysis because  it  avoided  potential  biases  introduced  by  selecting  a  single threshold (Tan et al., 2023) and had demonstrated considerable sensitivity in detecting topological variations in brain networks (Maas et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2021). Fig. 1 presented the flowchart of our methodology, with Fig. 1A illustrating the process of calculating network degree centrality for each participant.

## 2.4. Subtyping MDD based on individual deviations

To  discern  MDD  subtypes  characterized  by  significant  deviation patterns  in  degree  centrality,  we  employed  a  data-driven  k-means clustering algorithm (Fig. 1E). Specifically, we leveraged the deviation maps of all MDD patients as the clustering features, with the distance between any two patients defined as the Euclidean distance calculated from  their  respective  deviation  maps.  This  approach  enabled  us  to identify  patient  clusters  sharing  similar  degree  centrality  deviation patterns. To refine the clustering performance, we adjusted the number of  clusters  (k = 2,  3, … ,  10)  and  implemented  the  Silhouette  Index (Rousseeuw, 1987) alongside the Calinski-Harabasz Index (Cali ´ nski and Harabasz,  1974)  as  evaluation  metrics  to  assess  the  clustering  effect across varying cluster counts. During the k-means clustering process, we enhanced its robustness and ensured the reliability of clustering outcomes  by  setting  the  maximum  number  of  iterations  to  10,000  and repeating  the  calculations  1000  times.  This  method  allowed  us  to ascertain the optimal number of clusters that best represented significant degree centrality deviation patterns among MDD patients.

After  distinguishing  subtypes,  we  conducted  further  analyses  to compare  their  demographic  profiles  and  clinical  variables  (Fig.  1E). Using a similar approach, we generated spatial overlap maps specific to each  subtype,  portraying  the  percentages  of  individuals  exhibiting positive or negative outliers in various regions. We then compared these subtype-specific maps with the overall spatial overlap maps to investigate  whether  the  distribution  of  outliers  in  brain  regions  was  more consistent  within  MDD  subtypes  than  across  the  entire  MDD  patient population.

To  delve  deeper  into  the  disparities  in  deviation  patterns  among MDD  subtypes,  we  performed  a  comparative  analysis  at  both  the regional  and  network  levels  (Fig.  1E).  At  the  regional  level,  we computed and compared the mean deviation maps for each subtype. At the network level, we adopted the seven brain networks defined by the Yeo  atlas,  including  the  visual  (VIS),  sensorimotor  (SMN),  dorsal attention (DAN), ventral attention (VAN), limbic (LIM), frontoparietal (FPN), and default mode (DMN) networks (Thomas Yeo et al., 2011). Each of the 96 cortical regions outlined in the Harvard-Oxford atlas was assigned to the network containing the highest number of voxel attributions within that region. Following this, we calculated the mean deviation values across all regions within each network for every patient and compared these values among different MDD subtypes.

## 2.5. Correlation analysis between subtype-specific alterations and cognitive functions

We  investigated  the  relationship  between  the  spatial  patterns  of subtype-specific  deviations  and  cognitive  processes  (Fig.  1E).  Neurosynth (https://neurosynth.org/) is a platform that synthesizes results from thousands of fMRI studies, providing a quantitative representation of how specific brain regions/voxels correlate with neuropsychological processes in multitasking fMRI (Yarkoni et al., 2011). Given our focus on cognitive functions reported by Neurosynth, we selected 117 cognitive terms  from  the  Cognitive  Atlas  (https://www.cognitiveatlas.org/),  a publicly accessible ontology in cognitive science that standardizes and integrates  cognitive concepts (Poldrack et al., 2011). All these terms were also represented in Neurosynth, ensuring a comprehensive cognitive analysis.

To construct the region-by-cognitive function matrix, we adopted the same brain atlas as that used for the deviation maps to guarantee consistency  across  analyses.  We  then  segmented  the  Neurosynth  metaanalytic task activation maps for all selected cognitive terms into 96 distinct regions, where the value assigned to each region corresponded to the mean activation across all voxels within that specific brain area. After excluding cognitive terms that exhibited zero activation across all regions, we obtained a 96 × 114 matrix, which captured the associations between various cognitive functions and the segmented brain regions.

Subsequently, for each subtype, we conducted a partial least squares (PLS)  regression  analysis  to  explore  the  spatial  correlation  between cognitive  functions  and  the  observed  deviations.  Here,  the  cognitive function matrix served as the predictor variable, while the deviation map acted as the response variable. For each statistically significant PLS component, we employed a bootstrapping approach (with 1000 iterations) to assess the variability of the weights assigned to each cognitive term. To refine these weights, we adjusted the original weights through division by their corresponding estimated bootstrapping errors, resulting in corrected weights for all cognitive terms. Finally, we ranked these cognitive terms according to their corrected weights, which indicated their respective contributions to the significant PLS component.

## 2.6. Statistical analysis

One-sample t -tests  were  performed  on  the  deviation  values  of  all regions for each subtype to assess the statistical significance of the mean deviations. Based on the direction of the t-values (positive or negative), regions were categorized as either positively or negatively deviating. Afterwards, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted between the deviation values of these regions and HAMD scores to evaluate their associations  with  depression  severity.  The  significance  levels  for  the above  multiple  comparisons  were  corrected  using  the  Bonferroni method.  For  comparing  brain  network  deviations  between  subtypes, two-sample t-tests were utilized. To investigate the relationship between network abnormalities and depression severity, a similar approach was conducted  at  the  network  level:  one-sample  t-tests  on  network  deviations to identify networks belonging to the positive network or the negative one, followed by Pearson correlations between these networks' mean deviations and HAMD scores. The significance levels for these multiple comparisons were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. A p -value &lt; 0.05  was  deemed  statistically  significant  for  all analyses.  By  employing  permutation  tests,  combined  with  spatialautocorrelation  correction  (permutations = 10,000),  we  determined whether the explained variance of each PLS component, which quantified the explanatory power of the cognitive function matrix over the deviation map, significantly exceeded random expectations. Additionally,  the  significance  levels  of  the  spatial  correlations  between  PLS scores and deviation maps were ascertained using permutation tests.

## 3.2. Subtype-specific characteristics in network deviation patterns

To accurately evaluate the clustering tendency within our sample data, we utilized the Silhouette index and the Calinski-Harabasz index, both renowned for their robustness in assessing the quality of internal clustering. A higher value of these indices indicates improved clustering effectiveness. As shown in Fig. 3A, both indices reached their maximum values at two clusters, conclusively indicating that the optimal number of clusters for k-means clustering was k = 2. Accordingly, we categorized two distinct MDD subtypes, with subtype 1 accounting for 55 % of cases and  subtype  2  comprising  the  remaining  45  %,  based  on  individual variations in degree centrality measures.

The spatial overlap map showed that in subtype 1, positive outliers were  primarily  concentrated  in  the  left  posterior  parahippocampal gyrus, right frontal operculum cortex, left inferior frontal gyrus (pars triangularis), and left anterior temporal fusiform cortex. Negative outliers were primarily localized in the left precentral gyrus, left postcentral gyrus, right postcentral gyrus, and the inferior lateral occipital cortex. Regarding subtype 2, positive outliers were predominantly noted in the right inferior frontal gyrus, left anterior supramarginal gyrus, and the right frontal operculum cortex, while negative outliers were observed in the left posterior superior temporal gyrus, left parietal operculum cortex, bilateral frontal pole, and right temporal pole.

Compared to the spatial overlap maps of outliers for the entire MDD cohort, the outlier distributions across brain regions for both subtypes demonstrated a higher level of consistency (Fig. 3B). Specifically, for regions with positive outliers, subtype 1 exhibited a spatial overlap rate from 0.2283 % to 1.8265 %, whereas subtype 2 covered from 0.2786 % to 1.9499 %. For regions with negative outliers, subtype 1 displayed a spatial overlap rate from 0.2283 % to 2.0548 %, while subtype 2 ranged from 0.2786 % to 1.9499 %. The brain maps presented in Fig. 3C not only visualized the mean deviation of each region for both subtypes but also facilitated a comparative analysis of deviation differences across all regions  between  the  two  subtypes.  Notably,  these  maps  underscored that despite sharing similar spatial distributions at the regional level, the deviation patterns exhibited by distinct subtypes manifested opposing trends of change.

Upon closer examination of the mean deviations at the network level for the two subtypes, as presented in Table 1, it became evident that the two subtypes displayed distinct and discernible patterns of deviations. Specifically,  subtype  1  exhibited  a  distinct  deviation  pattern  at  the network  level,  with  negative  deviations  observed  within  the  visual system  (VIS; t = GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 10.287,  corrected p &lt; 0.001)  and  sensorimotor network (SMN; t = GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 16.959, corrected p &lt; 0.001). Conversely, positive deviations were noted in the ventral attention network (VAN; t = 2.433, corrected p = 0.018), limbic network (LIM; t = 9.304, corrected p &lt; 0.001), frontoparietal network (FPN; t = 11.967, corrected p &lt; 0.001), and default mode network (DMN; t = 14.590, corrected p &lt; 0.001). In contrast, subtype 2 displayed a reversed pattern of deviations, characterized by positive deviations in the VIS ( t = 5.772, corrected p &lt; 0.001), SMN ( t = 16.955, corrected p &lt; 0.001), and VAN ( t = 5.792, corrected p &lt; 0.001). However, negative deviations were observed in the LIM ( t = GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 15.746, corrected p &lt; 0.001), FPN ( t = GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 2.422, corrected p = 0.019), and DMN ( t =GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 11.550, corrected p &lt; 0.001), highlighting the opposing trends between the two subtypes across these functional networks.

The statistical results of two-sample t -tests conducted on the network mean  deviations  indicated  significant  differences  between  the  two subtypes. Specifically, deviations in subtype 2 were significantly higher than those in subtype 1 within the VIS, SMN, and VAN (VIS: t = 11.178, corrected p &lt; 0.001; SMN: t = 23.733, corrected p &lt; 0.001; VAN: t = 3.056, corrected p = 0.003), but significantly lower in LIM, FPN, and DMN (LIM: t = GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 17.666, corrected p &lt; 0.001; FPN: t = GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 9.351, corrected p &lt; 0.001;  DMN: t = GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 18.353,  corrected p &lt; 0.001). ## 3.3. Clinical variability and symptom correlation in MDD subtypes

As illustrated in Fig. 4A and Table 2, subtype 2 exhibited a significantly higher onset age compared to subtype 1 ( t = 2.286, p = 0.023). No significant differences were found in terms of age, sex, disease duration, medication status, or onset period between the two subtypes. Further subgroup analyses indicated that medication use and episode status had no significant impact on subtype deviation patterns (detailed analyses in Supplementary Materials). Notably, subtype 2 demonstrated more severe symptoms on the general somatic symptoms and hypochondriasis subscales  of  HAMD  than  subtype  1  (general  somatic  symptoms: t = 2.169, p = 0.031; hypochondriasis: t = 2.290, p = 0.023). Furthermore, Fig. 4B illustrated that in subtype 2, the deviation values of the right posterior  inferior  temporal  gyrus  (ITGpost)  and  LIM  were  negatively correlated with the HAMD scores (ITGpost.R: r = GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 0.184, corrected p = 0.013; LIM: r = GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 0.139, corrected p = 0.025). Conversely, in subtype 1, no  significant  correlations  were  observed  between  these  regional  or network deviation values and the HAMD scores.

## 3.4. Associations between subtype deviations and cognitive function maps

Fig. 5A showed that the first component of the partial least squares (PLS) regression explained 66.79 % and 67.84 % of the variance (both with  corrected p &lt; 0.001),  indicating  that  the  spatial  patterns  of cognitive  functions  in  PLS1  and  network  degree  centrality  deviation maps jointly captured 66.79 % and 67.84 % of the covariance between the two variables. The PLS1 scores for both subtypes exhibited significant spatial correlations with the deviation maps (subtype 1: r = 0.817, corrected p &lt; 0.001;  subtype  2: r = 0.8236,  corrected p &lt; 0.001; Fig.  5B).  This  suggested  that  brain  regions  exhibiting  increased  or decreased  deviations  were  correspondingly  associated  with  active  or diminished  cognitive  functions  in  two  subtypes.  After  ranking  the cognitive terms based on the corrected weights, we discovered distinctive  patterns  in  the  cognitive  processes  between  the  two  subtypes (Fig.  5C).  In  subtype  1,  the  cognitive  tasks  with  the  highest  positive loadings were primarily advanced cognitive functions such as impulsivity, social cognition, memory retrieval, and emotion regulation. The most negatively weighted tasks for this subtype were those related to motor functions and bodily perception like speech production, multisensory  integration,  movement,  and  visual  perception.  Conversely, subtype 2's highest positive loadings involved primarily motor functions and bodily perception, including movement, imagery, and pain. Meanwhile,  its  most  negatively  loaded  processes  centered  on  advanced cognitive functions such as memory retrieval, emotion regulation, and impulsivity.

Our analysis revealed intersubject  differences  in  the  spatial  distribution  and  directional  characteristics of individual deviations, underscoring the intricate complexity of MDD neuropathology. Moreover, we identified two distinct neurobiological subtypes of MDD, each distinguished by its unique topological abnormalities, clinical presentations, and cognitive profiles. Importantly, we noticed a low overlap rate across  regions  presenting  positive  and  negative  outliers  among  MDD patients,  where  this  regional  heterogeneity  has  been  confirmed  in several past normative modeling researches (Shao et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023). Our results not only emphasized the significant neuropathological feature of altered brain functions in MDD, but also highlighted the considerable variability in these alterations across individuals, which could lead to phenotypic heterogeneity (Segal et al., 2023) and provide potential clues for the diverse manifestations of MDD.

Through the comprehensive analysis of deviations across MDD patients,  we  successfully  identified  two  distinct  subtypes.  Notably,  deviations within each subtype were more concentrated than those in the pre-subtype  analysis,  and  the  distribution  of  these  deviations  varied significantly between the two subtypes. This finding re-emphasized the heterogeneity among MDD patients and highlighted the significance of subtype classification. In subtype 2, the right inferior temporal gyrus exhibited  reduced  degree centrality  relative  to  normal  levels.  Earlier research has indicated that during facial emotion recognition, adolescents with MDD exhibited deactivation in the inferior temporal gyrus, a region crucial for social cognition and the handling of perception and affect  (Tang  et  al.,  2018).  Furthermore,  research  has  reported  that regional functional activity coherence in the inferior temporal gyrus was significantly lower in anxious MDD patients compared to non-anxious MDD patients (Zhou et al., 2024), indicating functional differences in this  region  across  different  MDD  subtypes.  The  significant  negative correlation between negative deviations and HAMD scores in our study further stressed that the heightened severity of MDD symptoms in subtype 2 was linked to this region's decreased connectivity within the brain network. These findings implied that the negative deviation in degree centrality might impact specific cognitive functions and potentially help to  uncover  the  underlying  clinical  relevance  of  MDD  brain  changes. Additionally, the mean deviation patterns across regions in the two MDD subtypes were spatially similar but directionally opposite, implying that a region-level analysis alone might not fully capture the intricate functional alterations in MDD. This underlined the necessity of exploring abnormalities in network degree centrality between different subtypes from a more comprehensive and holistic perspective. Such an approach could provide a whole-brain hierarchical characterization of functional abnormalities  in  MDD,  thereby  informing  the  development  of  more targeted and effective treatment strategies.

Upon further mapping the deviations of regional activity patterns onto functional networks, we discovered remarkable functional disparities  between  subtype  1  and  subtype  2.  Remarkably,  these  subtypes presented significant differences in the direction of deviations within the LIM, FPN, DMN, VIS, and SMN, reflecting divergent alterations in brain connectivity. In healthy individuals, the brain displays an equilibrium between  segregation  and  integration  of  brain  networks  during  the resting state, a balance vital for facilitating a diverse array of cognitive functions (Wang et al., 2021b). The deviations observed in two subtypes of patients, when compared to healthy controls, underscored a novel insight from our study: the hierarchical organization of brain function in individuals  with  MDD  may  experience  distinct  alterations  in  both segregation  and  integration  processes.  This  revelation  suggested  a unique pattern of functional brain reorganization in MDD. Referencing prior findings that identified a correlation between gradient dispersion and network degree centrality (Pasquini et al., 2023), we proposed that the observed discrepancies could be rationalized by the expansion and compression of the brain functional gradient. Typically, the gradient demonstrates an orderly extension from unimodal networks at its base to transmodal networks at its apex (Haueis, 2021). Subtype 1 exhibited increased degree centrality in transmodal networks such as the LIM, FPN, and DMN, while decreased degree centrality in unimodal networks like the VIS and SMN. This pattern aligned with previous reports (Sun et al., 2023) and may suggest an overly broad span from basic sensory inputs to abstract cognitive operations in brain processing -an abnormal segregation of the functional gradients from lower to higher levels  of  processing.  Conversely,  subtype  2  displayed  higher  degree centrality in unimodal networks such as VIS and SMN, and lower degree centrality in transmodal networks like LIM, FPN, and DMN. Building upon our prior work demonstrating a compressed primary-totransmodal gradient in MDD patients (Xiao et al., 2023), our findings complemented this discovery, suggesting an overly integrated hierarchical structure in information processing for subtype 2. In conclusion, our  results  provided  new  insights  into  the  abnormal  integration  and differentiation of brain functional networks in MDD patients through the lens of inter-network connectivity patterns.

The subtype-specific abnormalities within brain functional networks were also reflected in the respective cognitive features of each subtype. Specifically, the abnormal hyperactivity within the VIS and SMN in subtype 2 suggested their influences on somatosensory processes,  such  as  multisensory  and  pain.  Considering  the  effect  of enhanced activity in the VIS (e.g. lingual gyrus) and SMN on pain experiences (Nickel et al., 2020; Russo et al., 2019), this abnormality could potentially  exacerbate  general  somatic  symptoms  in  this  subtype. Several limitations of this study should be taken into account and addressed  in  future  research.  Firstly,  while  normative  models  have provided valuable insights into the heterogeneity of MDD and its distinct subtypes by elucidating the age-related alterations of brain function, the reliance on cross-sectional data has limited our capacity to observe longterm associations and establish causal relationships between deviations and  clinical  manifestations.  Therefore,  longitudinal  studies  tracking these deviations over time are essential to validate the stability of the identified subtypes and expand our knowledge of the neurobiological courses  underlying  MDD.  Secondly,  although  the  sample  size  of  this study was commendable, our findings have not yet been validated with external data from different populations. To enhance the reliability and broaden  the  applicability  of  our  subtype  classification,  upcoming research ought to incorporate a more diverse participant pool. Thirdly, the incomplete clinical information for certain MDD patients, such as their first-episode status and medication usage, constrained our thorough assessment of these critical clinical factors. To understand their influence on MDD subtypes more deeply, future studies should strive to collect more comprehensive clinical data. Lastly, this study has significantly advanced our understanding by analyzing cognitive functions at the group level. However, future research could examine the relationship between brain function abnormalities and cognitive functions at the individual level to better reflect the heterogeneity of MDD.

## 5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study has formulated a normative model framework based on a healthy control cohort, thereby dissecting the significant inter-individual variability in the spatial distribution and direction of degree centrality deviations among MDD patients at both regional and network levels. Notably, we have identified two distinct MDD subtypes, each characterized by their unique degree centrality alterations, clinical presentations,  and  cognitive  features.  These  findings  have  not  only enriched  our  comprehension  of  the  intricate  neurobiological  mechanisms underlying MDD's clinical heterogeneity but also provided the possibility  for  subtyping  MDD  patients  and  developing  personalized therapeutic interventions.
