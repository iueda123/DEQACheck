## Supplemental Methods

## Voxel-Based Morphometry (VBM)

Structural MRI data of participants were analyzed with the Computational Anatomy Toolbox 12 (CAT12 http://www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/), a VBM protocol carried out within SPM. The steps used to calculate gray matter volume (GMV) were as follows: (1) Each T1-weighted image was manually set at the anterior commissure as the origin of the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) coordinate system; (2) co-registered images were segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid; (3) the GM maps were normalized into MNI space; (4) the voxel values of density were modulated to volume; (5) the GM maps were smoothed with an isotropic Gaussian kernel ( full width at half maximum = 10 mm) (Fig.1(a)).

## Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF)

NMF is an unsupervised machine learning method used to decompose matrices; it can be used for dimension reduction and extraction of features (1) and has been widely utilized across multiple fields in MRI data analysis (2), image analysis (3), and signal processing (4). Compared with principle component analysis, independent component analysis, and singular value decomposition, NMF  can  capture  robust  and  reliable  non-negative  underlying  structural  differences  in  high dimension data, and is well-suited for structural MRI to detect differences among individuals.

## Determining the optimal number of factors

The decomposition algorithm we used achieved sparse-NMF. Increased sparsity improves the interpretability and specificity, and is important for signal analysis and brain modelling (5, 6). In order to determine the optimal number of factors and the sparsity parameter, we examined the reconstruction error and reproducibility of the obtained solutions in the ABIDE\_I\_TD gray matter (GM) matrix and ABIDE\_II\_TD GM matrix. This strategy is analogous to the model selection in Sotiras's work (7).

Firstly, we examined how the reconstruction error of the data approximation varies with the number of factors (K from 2 to 20) and sparsity parameters lamda (lamda= L/ the number of voxel, L  is  the  maximal  number  of  non-zeros  voxels  in  each  factor,  lamda  from  0.1  to  0.9).  The reconstruction error was defined as equation (2), and it was used to evaluate the accuracy of matrix decomposition. We found that as values of K and lamda were increased, the approximate error was reduced. The reconstruction error tended to be stable when K was larger than 5 and lamda was larger than 0.3 (Fig.s1A, B).

Reproducibility analyses were performed on the ABIDE\_I\_TD GM matrix and the ABIDE\_II\_TD GM matrix by testing K from 5 to 8 and sparsity parameter lamda from 0.3 to 0.5. Reproducibility was quantified by the similarity of factors between the ABIDE\_I\_TD and the ABIDE\_II\_TD cohorts (Fig.s1C),  computed  as  the  mean  correlation  between  corresponding  factor  pairs  (using  the Hungarian matching algorithm). We found that the similarity was the largest when K was 6 and lamda was 0.3 (the similarity was 0.748). Therefore, we selected K = 6 and lamda = 0.3 as the number of factors and sparsity parameter.

Finally, guided by previous work (8), the NMF procedure was repeated 100 times to weaken the influence of the random initializations. The final factors were obtained by selecting the factor closest to the remaining 99 times decomposition.

## Meta-analytic decoding of factors using NeuroSynth

To decode the psychological and physiological functions of the derived factors, we compared the spatial  pattern  of  factors  to  the  functional  anatomy  of  the  human  brain  using  NeuroSynth (http://www.neurosynth.org/) (9), an online platform for meta-analysis of functional neuroimaging literature. Guided by previous work (10), we used factor maps as inputs to decode the associated psychological and physiological functions. Then, to characterize the functional properties of each factor, we sorted all correlation coefficients for each factor in descending order and determined the  top  five  terms  showing  the  maximum  correlation  with  each  factor.  Similar  terms  (e.g., 'execution' and 'executive') were merged into a base form to avoid selecting repetitive terms; this yielded  26  terms.  A  heatmap  was  plotted  for  visual  assessment  of  the  potential  functions associated with each factor.

## Back reconstruction algorithm of non-negative matrix factorization

In our analysis, the back reconstruction algorithm of non-negative matrix factorization (BRNMF) was used to reconstruct the ABIDE\_I\_TD GM matrix and ABIDE\_I\_ASD GM matrix. Firstly, the NMF was performed on the ABIDE\_II\_TD GM matrix, in this step we obtained the factor matrix and subject loading matrix (weight matrix). Then the factor matrix of ABIDE\_II\_TD was used to estimate the subject loading matrix of ABIDE\_I\_TD and ABIDE\_I\_ASD based on Eq. (3).

<!-- formula-not-decoded -->

The  multiplicative  update  method  (https://github.com/smatmo/l0-sparse-NMF)  was  used  to solve the NMF problem and estimate the subject loading matrix (11). The difference between NMF and BR-NMF is that BR-NMF iteratively updates the H matrix, while keeping the W matrix obtained from the ABIDE\_II\_TD GM matrix decomposition fixed, and does not stop until the reconstruction error is almost unchanged compared with the previous iteration.

## Selection of covariates

Normative modelling is similar to the use of growth charts in pediatric medicine, except the conventional response variable (e.g. height and weight) is substituted for a quantitative biological readout  (e.g.  regional  brain  activity).  The  classical  covariates  (age  and  gender)  can  also  be substituted for clinically relevant variables (12). Using age and gender as covariates is a common configuration  in  previous  studies  (13-15).  In  our  study,  we  used  the  weights  as  the  response variable which represent the individual differences in GM volume. Previous studies using MRI have demonstrated  the  correlation  between  age  and  global  or  regional  GM  volume,  and  observed gender  differences  in  GM  volume  (16,  17).  Thus,  age  and  gender  play  an  important  role  in characterizing GM volume. In our study, we found significant negative correlations between the weight  of  factor  and  the  significance  differences  between  the  weights  of  male  and  female participants  (Fig.  s2,  s3).  Therefore,  we  chose  age  and  gender  as  the  covariates  to  define  the mapping.

## Cross-validation

To assess the generalizability of the model, we used 10-fold cross-validation before applying the model trained on ABIDE\_II\_TD to the discovery cohort. We partitioned the data into 10 'folds' and  repeatedly  trained  the  model  on  90%  of  the  data,  withholding  the  remaining  10%  for estimating generalization performance. This procedure was repeated 10 times so that each 'fold' was excluded once.

## Predicting age using the weights

In our analysis, we found that age is significantly correlated with the weights. Therefore, we treated age as one of the covariates to develop the normative model. Although our claim is not to predict age using the weights, to further prove the relationship between the age and weights, we used the weights to predict the age of each subject. Firstly, we trained a predictive model based on ABIDE\_II\_TD and ABIDE\_I\_TD weights, respectively, which predicts the age of ABIDE\_II\_TD and ABIDE\_I\_TD  using  a  random  forest  regression  model  (5-fold  cross-validation)  (Fig.s8  A  and  C). Second,  to  test  the  generality  of  the  predictive  model,  we  used  the  model  developed  in ABIDE\_II\_TD to predict the age of ABIDE\_I\_TD subjects (Fig.s8 B) and we used the model developed in ABIDE\_I\_TD to predict the age of ABIDE\_II\_TD subjects (Fig.s8 D). These models did well when predicting age in an independent sample.

## Reproducibility analysis

We added four steps to examine the robustness and generalizability of our work. First, to estimate the generalizability of the NMF algorithm, we implemented NMF on the GM matrix of ABIDE\_I\_TD and obtained six latent factors and corresponding weights. The similarity between ABIDE\_II\_TD latent factors (Fig.s9) and ABIDE\_I\_TD latent factors was 0.9067, which indicates that NMF leads to a highly stable and reproducible representation of factors in the TD cohort. We obtained the weights of ABIDE\_II\_ASD and ABIDE\_II\_TD participants by using the non-negative reconstruction algorithm to reconstruct the GM matrix. To exclude the effects of covariates on weights,  we  used  the  GLM  method  to  regress  out  age,  gender,  handedness,  FIQ,  and  sites. Significant  differences  in  weights  were  observed  between  the  ABIDE\_II\_ASD  and  ABDIE\_II\_TD cohorts (Fig.s9), which were similar to the results in the main text. Compared with TD, participants

with ASD exhibited significantly increased weights in factors 1, 3, and 6 ( p &lt;0.05, uncorrected; twosample t -test).

To establish the robustness and generalizability of the normative model, weights of the six latent factors derived from ABIDE\_I\_TD were used to generate normative models (Fig.s10), and deviation of the six weights relative to the norm range were computed and compared between ABIDE\_II\_TD  and  ABIDE\_II\_ASD.  Significant between-group  differences  in deviations  were observed between  ABIDE\_II\_ASD and ABIDE\_II\_TD cohorts (Fig.s10), and ASD exhibited significantly increased deviation in factors 1, 3, and 6 ( p &lt;0.05, un-corrected; two-sample t -test) compared with TD. These results are similar to the results reported in the main text.

Thirdly, to establish the robustness and generalizability of the clustering solution, we clustered ABIDE\_II\_ASD  participants  into  three  subtypes  based  on  the  deviation  matrix  using  Gaussian mixture analysis. Fig.s11 shows the clustering center of the three subtypes. The clustering center obtained in the reproducibility analysis was in line with that reported in the main text. Subtype 1 and subtype 3 showed all positive deviations, but the deviations of participants in subtype 3 were larger than those in subtype 1. Participants in subtype 2 showed all negative deviations. Table s4 shows the demographic characteristics for participants across the three ASD subtypes. However, we found that demographic characteristics of ABIDE\_II\_ASD subjects used for clustering analysis did not match the ABIDE\_I\_ASD subjects (in the main text). For this reason, in order to make the results of the two groups as comparable as possible, we matched the demographics characteristics of the two groups, including age, gender, and full-scale IQ. Thus, 37 participants of subtype 1, 151 participants of subtype 2, and 33 participants of subtype 3 were included in subsequent analysis (Fig.s11  D-F).  Significant  differences  were  observed  between  subtypes  1  and  2  (p=0.021)  and between subtypes 2 and 3 (p=0.020) in ADOS social scores (Fig.s11 E). A marginally significant difference was found between subtypes 2 and 3 (p=0.051) in ADOS communication scores (Fig.s11 D). We did not find significant differences across the three subtypes on ADOS RRB scores.

We also computed a Dice coefficient of ABIDE\_II\_ASD clustering labels obtained from the ABIDE\_I\_TD normative model (developed in the last step) and the ABIDE\_II\_TD normative model (developed in the main text), and the Dice coefficient was 0.530, which suggests the stability of clustering.

When we performed the clustering analysis, we found 3 was the optimal number of subtypes according  to  the  Calinski  Harabasz  score  and  Silhouette  score,  for  both  discovery  analysis  and reproducibility analysis, which also suggests the stability of the clustering solution.
