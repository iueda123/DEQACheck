## Abstract

Background: People with bipolar disorder (BD) tend to show widespread cognitive impairment compared to healthy controls. Impairments in processing speed (PS), attention and executive function (EF) may represent 'core' impairments that have a role in wider cognitive dysfunction. Cognitive impairments appear to relate to structural brain abnormalities in BD, but whether core deficits are related to particular brain regions is unclear and much of the research on brain-  cognition associations is limited by univariate analysis and small samples.

Methods: Euthymic BD patients ( n = 56)  and  matched  healthy  controls  ( n = 26)  underwent T1-  weighted MRI scans and completed neuropsychological tests of PS, attention and EF. We utilised public datasets to develop normative models of cortical thickness ( n = 5977) to generate robust estimations of cortical abnormalities in patients. Canonical correlation analysis was used to assess multivariate brain-  cognition associations in BD, controlling for age, sex and premorbid IQ.

Results: BD showed impairments on tests of PS, attention and EF, and abnormal cortical thickness in several brain regions compared to healthy controls. Impairments in tests of PS and EF were most strongly associated with cortical thickness in the left inferior temporal, right entorhinal and right temporal pole areas.

Conclusion: Impairments in PS, attention and EF can be observed in euthymic BD and may be related to abnormal cortical thickness in temporal regions. Future research should continue to leverage normative modelling and multivariate methods to examine  complex  brain-  cognition  associations  in  BD.  Future  research  may  benefit  from exploring covariance between traditional brain structural morphological metrics such as cortical thickness, cortical volume and surface area.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

People with bipolar disorder (BD) tend to show widespread cognitive impairment compared to healthy controls. 1,2 BD is also associated with structural brain abnormalities, including abnormal cortical thickness, compared to healthy controls, across widespread areas of the brain. 3 Both cognitive impairments and brain abnormalities can be observed in euthymia, suggesting they represent core features or consequences of BD. 2,4 Some studies suggest a relationship between brain structure and cognitive impairment in BD; 4-7 however, there is currently a lack of multivariate studies.

Processing speed (PS), attention and executive function (EF) are often considered primary or 'core' cognitive impairments in BD and may explain wider cognitive dysfunction, such as poor memory. 8-10 From this perspective, their potential relationship to abnormal brain morphology may be particularly important. Some research suggests that performance on tests of PS, attention and EF may indeed relate to cortical thickness in BD, even in euthymia. 5,11 Neuropsychological tests  that  capture  these  processes,  such  as  the  Digit  Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) and Trail-  Making Test (TMT), often detect a large magnitude of impairment 12,13 and have been specifically related to cortical thickness in BD. 11 However, findings are inconsistent  with  not  all  studies  detecting  associations. 13-15 Few  previous studies have focussed on the aforementioned cognitive processes, and studies often omit tests of PS and attention or focus instead on general cognitive functioning. Thus, the complex nature of the relationship between core cognitive dysfunction and cortical thickness is not well understood and there is no consensus on the specific brain regions involved.

Several challenges currently limit brain-  cognition studies: recent research  suggests  that  brain-  behaviour  associations  are  not  replicable, 16 and results appear inconsistent with some studies failing to  detect  significant  structural  brain-  cognition  associations. 15,17,18 This may be driven by small samples; here, normative modelling approaches offer a powerful tool to mitigate this limitation and provide more robust estimates of brain abnormalities. 19 Previous research generally  relies  on  univariate  analysis,  where  the  relationship  between a single cognitive function and the structure of several brain regions is tested. Studying cognitive functions independently may not accurately model cognitive impairment in BD, since processes likely  interact  and  overlap,  especially  in  the  presence  of  cognitive impairment. 8,9,20,21 The  univariate  approach is also constrained by the problem of multiple comparisons, where there is a risk of missing real associations after correcting for multiple comparisons (i.e., increased type-  II errors). Multivariate methods that can simultaneously accommodate the multivariate cognitive and brain data may better  reflect  the  complex,  multimodal  nature  of  behaviour  and physiology. Such methods are beginning to be leveraged to assess brain-  cognition  relationships 5,22,23 ;  however,  few  studies  have  utilised these methods, especially in BD.

We examined multivariate associations between cortical thickness and core cognitive functions in euthymic BD. We used large public neuroimaging datasets to build normative models of cortical thickness to produce robust estimations of structural abnormalities in BD. 19 We hypothesised that abnormalities in cortical thickness in groups of regions would be associated with poor core cognitive performance, particularly in PS and attention.

## 2 | METHODS

Data  were  collected  as  part  of  the  Bipolar  Lithium  Imaging  and Spectroscopy Study (BLISS). 24 T1-  weighted imaging, diffusionweighted  imaging  and  lithium  imaging  data  have  been  reported previously. 24,25 BLISS was granted a favourable ethical opinion by a United Kingdom National Research Ethics Committee (14/NE/1135). All participants provided written informed consent.

## 2.1 | Participants

In total, 59 patients with BD were enrolled in the study, recruited via two routes. First, the local Clinical Research Network (CRN) approached  psychiatrists  in  secondary  care  services  who  screened their caseloads against eligibility criteria and approved a list of patients  who the CRN contacted to offer participation in the study. Second, patients were recruited from primary care centres via the North East Commissioning Service: GP practices screened their databases using eligibility criteria and mailed information packs to eligible patients, who then contacted the CRN to take part.

Inclusion criteria for patients comprised a diagnosis of BD (type I  or  II)  according  to  DSM-  5  criteria.  The  diagnosis  was  confirmed during clinical interviews using the NetSCID diagnostic tool, a validated  computerised  version  of  the  Structured  Clinical  Interview for DSM-  5 criteria (Telesage, Inc., Chapel Hill, NC, USA), which facilitates data storage. Comorbid psychiatric diagnoses identified by NetSCID were permissible (excluding neurodevelopmental, neurocognitive, neurodegenerative and substance abuse disorders) if the primary diagnosis was BD. Where further information was required to  confirm  the  primary  diagnosis,  case  notes  were  reviewed  and discussed with a senior psychiatrist (DAC) as required. All patients were euthymic for 1 month prior to the scan, defined as a score of &lt; 7 on both the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) 26 and the 21-  item Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM-  D). 27

BLISS  was  originally  designed  to  investigate  lithium  treatment effects  and  therefore  aimed  to  recruit  a  1:1:1  split  across  three groups: BD taking lithium, BD naïve to lithium and healthy controls. This study is a secondary analysis of the BLISS data, where lithium effects were not the primary focus, therefore, we merged the two BD  groups  to  increase  statistical  power.  Where  appropriate,  we checked for differences between the two BD groups to check for confounding effects of lithium. A total of 32 BD patients were taking lithium; the other patients were taking maintenance treatments but were naïve to lithium.

A total of 27 matched healthy controls (HC) were recruited via the  Institute  of  Neuroscience  volunteer  database  at  Newcastle

University.  Healthy  controls  had  no  history  of  psychiatric  illness and were not taking any psychotropic medications. All participants were 18-65 years of age. Exclusion criteria for all participants were: contraindications to MR examination (e.g., cardiac pacemaker claustrophobia, exceeding 150 kg in weight); current or past medical condition  likely  to  affect  brain  structure;  current  or  recent  substance abuse  (NetSCID  Module  E);  alcohol  intake  exceeding  21 units  per week  (self-  reported);  and  a  learning  disability  or  impairment  of capacity.

## 2.2 | Materials

Premorbid IQ was measured with the National Adult Reading Test (NART), a commonly used test in which participants are asked to read a list of words aloud, with no time limit imposed. The examiner records whether each word had been pronounced correctly, totalling the number of errors, which can be converted into an IQ score.

## 2.2.1  |  Neuropsychological tests

A battery of pen-  and-  paper neuropsychological tests 28 was used to measure PS, attention and EF as potential primary cognitive impairments in BD that appear to be related to brain structural abnormalities. 9-11 To  allow  comparisons of our results to previous research, we chose standardised and validated tests that are commonly used in the literature and recommended by the International Society for Bipolar Disorders-Battery for Assessment of Neurocognition. 13,29

The DSST measured PS and attention, as it is sensitive to cognitive impairment in euthymic BD, detecting large effect sizes compared to controls. 13,30 Participants transcribed unique geometric symbols with corresponding Arabic numbers using a key on the page. Participants also completed a sub-  test of the DSST, Symbol Copy, which involves copying symbols from one row of boxes to another, retaining elements of motor speed but not requiring other cognitive processes involved in transcribing. We subtracted the time taken to complete the Symbol Copy version from the time taken to complete the DSST original version to reflect cognitive speed without the motor component.

The TMT was used as a validated, standardised test that is sensitive to cognitive impairment in BD. 30 Part A (TMT-  A) was used to measure PS and attention. Participants connected a series of numbered circles spread on a page in ascending order. Part B (TMT-  B) measured EF: participants connected a series of circles that either contained a number or letter, in alternate ascending and alphabetical order (e.g.,  1-  A-  2-  B-  3-  C,  etc.).  In  both  parts,  the  time  to  complete the task was measured. TMT-  B tests working memory and switching (elements of EF), as well as motor speed, cognitive speed and attention. The latter three functions were fractioned out of the TMT-  B score by subtracting the time to complete TMT-  A from the time to complete TMT-  B, leaving a measure of EF.

Category fluency was also used to measure EF as it appears to be a strong predictor in discriminating euthymic BD patients from

.

controls. 31 Participants named as many animals as possible in 60 sec. The number of correctly named animals was recorded.

The d2 test was used to measure effortful attention and concentration, since effortful (but not automatic) attention appears to be impaired in BD. 32 Participants crossed out as many target stimuli as possible (the letter 'd' with two dashes above or below it) amongst a series of distractor stimuli (letters 'd' or 'p' with any other number of dashes around it) within 20 seconds. Three outcome measures were recorded: the percentage of correct responses; the total number of correctly cancelled targets minus the total number incorrectly cancelled (concentration performance; CP); and the maximum minus the minimum of total items processed in a trial (fluctuation rate; FR). CP captures effortful attention and the ability to coordinate speed and accuracy and correlates with reaction times in BD. 32 FR captures inconsistency in performance.

## 2.3 | Procedure

All  participants  attended  a  screening  visit  to  obtain  informed  consent  and  assess  eligibility  during  a  clinical  interview.  The  interview included: NetSCID to confirm diagnosis and comorbidities in patients and to screen for psychiatric illness in healthy controls; observer-  rated mood scales (YMRS and HAM-  D) to confirm euthymic mood; and an MR safety questionnaire. Clinical interviews were conducted by an experienced research assistant (CF), supervised by a senior psychiatrist (DAC) via discussion of the assessment and suitability for inclusion.

Participants who were eligible were then invited to attend a second session, which included collecting blood samples (not reported here), MRI  scanning  and  neuropsychological  assessment.  MRI  scans  were performed at 9 am by an experienced MRI physicist; scans lasted 2 h for patients taking lithium (lithium acquisition reported previously), 24 and 1 h for all other participants. To avoid fatigue, a comfort break was included  before  the  neuropsychological  assessment.  The  neuropsychological battery was administered according to the respective manuals by an experienced research assistant (CF) trained in administering each test. The battery lasted approximately 50 min and was completed in a quiet testing room. The tests were administered in a fixed order: NART, category fluency, TMT (parts A and B), DSST (original followed by symbol copy) and the d2 test of attention.

## 2.3.1  |  Scanner and image acquisition

MR  data  were  acquired  on  a  3T  Philips  Achieva  MRI  scanner  (Philips  Medical  Systems)  with 1 H  structural  and  diffusionweighted  imaging  performed  using  a  Philips  8-  channel  SENSE head coil. For each participant, we acquired 3D T 1 -  weighted images  (T1w)  of  brain  anatomy  with  a 1 H gradient  echo  sequence (TR = 9.6 ms,  TE = 4.6 ms,  FOV = 240 × 240 × 180 mm,  acquisition matrix = 240 × 208 × 180 mm,  voxel  size = 1 × 1.15 × 1 mm,  reconstructed  into  matrix  size = 256 × 256 × 180 mm,  1 mm 3 average) and 2D T 2 -  weighted Fluid-  Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR)

.

images  (50  slices,  TR = 11,000 ms,  TE = 125 ms,  FOV = 240 × 240 mm, matrix size = 256 × 256 mm, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 3 mm average).

## 2.4 | Data analysis

The analysis pipeline is illustrated in Figure 1.

## 2.4.1  |  MRI data pre-  processing

All  BLISS  MR  images  were  exported  in  DICOM  format  and converted to NIFTI format using the dcm2niix program (v1.0.20180622).  Data  pre-  processing  and  analysis  were  performed  using  FreeSurfer  v6.0, 33 using  the  standard recon-  all pipeline,  including  removal  of  non-  brain  tissue,  segmentation of  grey  matter  and  white  matter  surfaces,  and  cortical  parcellation.  FLAIR  images  were  used  in  the recon-  all pipeline  to improve  the  pial  surface  and  segmentation.  Participants  were excluded if the T1w images included MR artefacts or were poor quality  ( n = 1;  HC). For the remaining participants, visual qual ity inspections were performed on a sub-  sample of participants (approximately half the total sample; n = 30 BD and n = 18 HC) using FreeSurfer's FreeView function. Manual corrections of the pial  surface  were  conducted where necessary using FreeView ( n = 24 participants were edited). After manual corrections, the overall  image  quality  and  FreeSurfer  processing  were  deemed adequate and cortical thickness measures were obtained for 68 anatomical regions of interest (ROIs) for each participant using the Desikan-  Killiany atlas.

## 2.4.2  |  Normative model of cortical thickness

To mitigate issues with a small healthy control sample, we developed normative models of cortical thickness using large public normative datasets. 34-37 A  sample  of n = 6344  healthy  individuals  were  included (normative-  HC: mean age 32.64; 50.3% females; see Data S1 for further details). All data were pre-  processed using the standard recon-  all pipeline in FreeSurfer. We harmonised the normative-  HC data, including the 26 HCs recruited to BLISS, for each ROI by regressing age, sex and scanning protocol (i.e., site; as a random effect) on cortical thickness using a generalised additive model. The correction was performed in R using the package mgcv (https://  CRAN. R-  proje  ct.  org/  web/  packa  ges/  mgcv). We removed controls who had outliers in any ROI, defined as residuals greater than 5 median absolute deviations ( n = 393).

BD data were then adjusted for age, sex and scanning site, using the prediction from the normative model, where the site correction was informed by the site offset estimated for BLISS HCs. Data for each  ROI  were  then z -  scored  based  on  the  normative  mean  and standard  deviation.  The  resulting  data  therefore  reflected  abnormalities in cortical thickness for each ROI for each patient, based on

## 2.4.3  |  Neuropsychological data pre-  processing

A total of 3 BD participants did not complete the cognitive battery and were excluded from this analysis. For the remaining participants, missing values for premorbid IQ and neuropsychological variables were imputed using linear regression (5% of data; see Data S1 for details). Variables were reversed where appropriate so that a higher score reflected better performance.

We removed the effects of age and premorbid IQ as they are known  to  confound  cognitive  performance;  however,  we  did  not control for years of education, since educational attainment is disrupted in BD. 38 Age  and  premorbid IQ were regressed out of the neuropsychological data in the HC group using robust regression, then this model was used to remove normative effects of age and premorbid IQ from the BD data. Standardised residuals  following age and premorbid IQ regression were z -  scored  based  on  the  HC mean and standard deviation so that the neuropsychological data reflected an abnormality from the matched controls.

## 2.4.4  |  Statistical analysis

Statistical  analysis  was  performed  using  R  Studio  v4.1.2. 39 Tests were regarded as significant if p &lt; 0.05. Data were inspected to ensure assumptions of each statistical model were met and neuropsychological data were transformed as required to fit assumptions of multivariate normality (see Data S1). The subsequent analysis was run  using  the  untransformed  neuropsychological  data  as  well  to compare results; results are presented in Data S1.

## Group differences

Student's t -  tests  were  used  to  compare  groups  where  data  were normally  distributed,  otherwise  non-  parametric  Mann-Whitney U tests were used. Group differences in dichotomous variables were analysed using χ 2 tests. Cohen's d was used to estimate effect sizes. Group differences in cortical thickness for each ROI were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-  Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate, which was set at 5%. 40

## Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA)

Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) was used to assess multivariate associations  between  neuropsychological  performance  and  cortical thickness of regions in the BD group (Figure 1). CCA reduces the number of variables in each set to latent variables (canonical variates) that maximise the correlation between two sets of data. The output of CCA is  correlated pairs of canonical variates, which are ordered with the first canonical pair (i.e., U 1 and V 1) explaining the largest proportion of covariance between the two sets. Since the number of BD participants was less than the number of ROIs, a principal components analysis (PCA) was applied to the cortical thickness dataset before CCA to reduce dimensionality to limit the feature-  to-  sample ratio and avoid overfitting. 22,41 The number of principal components (PCs) that collectively explained 90% of this dataset were retained and fed into the CCA as the cortical thickness dataset ( V ). Cross-  loadings for each original variable (e.g., ROIs) were estimated by correlating the original data for each ROI with the canonical variate U1 .

The  statistical  significance  of  the  canonical  correlations  was tested using Pillai's trace as a conservative multivariate test of significance. 42 We also ran a permutation test, where participants were randomly shuffled in the cortical thickness dataset ( V ),  then  CCA was performed on the mismatched datasets. This was done 10,000 times  to  yield  a  distribution  of  statistics  produced  with  randomly shuffled data. We would expect a truly significant result of the original  CCA to have a more extreme value than the results from the permuted data, allowing for 5% of extreme values to be produced by chance. The percentage of randomly permuted results that were more extreme than the original CCA can be interpreted as a p -  value, where p &lt; 0.05 suggests statistical significance.

## 3 | RESULTS

## 3.1 | Group differences in demographic, clinical and neuropsychological data

Table 1 shows demographic information and clinical characteristics for BD and HC groups. Groups did not differ in age or sex. HC had significantly more years of education than BD. BD scored significantly higher on HAM-  D and YMRS than HC but were within the range consistent with euthymic mood. Groups did not differ in premorbid IQ or on average cortical thickness in each hemisphere. Table 1 also displays group differences in raw neuropsychological scores: BD showed significantly poorer performance than HC on most tests, with medium effect sizes ( d &gt; 0.5).  Figure  2  illustrates  neuropsychological  group  differences after correcting for age and premorbid IQ and imputing missing values. The results showed a similar pattern of deficits in BD, except TMT-  B (minus TMT-  A) was not significant ( p = 0.063, d = 0.51; see Data S1).

## 3.2 | Cortical thickness in BD patients

Figure 3 shows cortical thickness abnormalities for each ROI for the BD group compared to the normative data. BD showed a thinner cortex than normative-  HC on average in most areas; group means of cortical thickness for each ROI and associated group differences are provided in Data S1. Medium effect sizes were found for left postcentral, precentral, paracentral and caudal middle frontal areas ( d &gt; 0.5).  Small  effect sizes were found for left precuneus and cuneus; and right paracentral, precentral, postcentral, insula and pericalcarine areas ( d &gt; 0.2). As an exploratory step, we tested whether cortical  thickness  differed  between  patients  taking  lithium  and patients not taking lithium in our sample; no significant group differences were found after correcting for multiple comparisons (all p s &gt; 0.05).

## 3.3 | Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA)

PCA was conducted on the cortical thickness data: 23 PCs explained 90% of the data.  CCA  was  performed  using  the  seven  neuropsychological variables as dataset U and the 23 cortical thickness PCs as dataset V ; Figure 1 shows heatmaps of each dataset and a scatterplot of the first canonical correlation, which had a strong linear correlation of 0.860. Pillai's Trace suggested that this was not statistically significant ( p = 0.112). Results for the remaining canonical correlations are provided in Data S1. A permutation test with n = 10,000 permutations also suggested the correlation was not significant but was trending towards significance ( p = 0.093).

Figure  4  illustrates the  association  between  cortical  thickness  of  each  region  and the cognitive dataset U .  Three regions were most strongly associated  with  the  first  cognitive  canonical  variate U1 :  left  inferior temporal  ( rho = 0.406, p = 0.002),  right  entorhinal  ( rho = 0.380, p = 0.004)  and  right  temporal  pole  areas  ( rho = 0.334, p = 0.012). Three other regions did not reach the threshold of 0.3 but had significant p -  values:  right  inferior  temporal  ( rho = 0.286, p = 0.032), right pars orbitalis ( rho = 0.268, p = 0.046) and right superior temporal ( rho = 0.297, p = 0.026) areas. The full list of cross-  loadings is provided in Data S1.

## 4 | DISCUSSION

We examined multivariate associations between cognitive impairments  and  abnormal  cortical  thickness  in  euthymic  BD.  On average, the BD group showed poorer performance than matched HCs on some tests of PS, EF and attention, even after accounting for age and premorbid IQ. Multivariate CCA suggested that impairments in  processing  speed  (DSST)  and  executive  function  (TMT-  B)  were associated  with  abnormal  cortical  thickness  in  the  left  inferior temporal, right entorhinal and right temporal pole areas in our BD sample. However, the canonical correlation did not reach statistical significance, likely due to a limited patient sample size in our study.

Our results are in line with previous research showing cortical thinning in BD in bilateral frontal and parietal regions. 3,43 Our normative  modelling  approach  mitigated  limitations  associated  with small a control group. Here, matched controls were only needed to estimate the statistical offset required to correct for the scanning site, to harmonise the BD data with the normative model. The normative model was then used to remove normative effects of age and sex from the BD data (without removing information associated with disease) and estimate abnormalities in patients. Despite the modest BD sample, our normative modelling approach yielded results similar to previous large-  scale studies of cortical thickness in BD. 3

Our results are also consistent with studies showing impairments in  core  cognitive  functions  in  BD. 8-10,13,30 We  did  not  find  an  impairment in TMT-  A in our BD sample; previous research suggested that TMT-  A is one of the most robust measures of cognitive impairment in euthymic BD 30 ; however, a recent meta-  analysis found only a  medium effect size when comparing BD to HC, with significant

## 4.2 | Heterogeneity and clinical confounds

An  ongoing  problem  in  researching  the  cognitive  profile  of  BD  is heterogeneity within the disorder: BD groups tend to vary in the degree of cognitive impairment, with some patients performing as well as healthy controls. 1 Patients with BD also vary in brain structural abnormalities. 50 Some evidence points towards the existence of subgroups with distinct cognitive profiles in BD, where the most impaired subgroup shows unique brain morphology. 51 However, a recent review did not find strong evidence to support the idea that cognitive  subgroups  map  onto  distinct  structural  brain  abnormality  profiles  in  BD, 52,53 thus,  the  extent  of  heterogeneity  in  braincognition associations is not yet clear. Our BD sample appeared to vary in cognitive performance, but we accounted for the limitation of heterogeneity by considering cognitive functions and brain morphology as continuous variables that were quantified as a deviation from the healthy norm. However, the potential influence of cognitive heterogeneity should be considered in our results and in future studies.

Clinical confounds such as illness duration, illness severity, BD type (I or II) and psychosis may be related to cognitive impairment and brain abnormalities in BD and were not controlled for here due to  low  statistical  power. 1,3,43,54 Brain-  cognition  associations have been observed even after controlling for clinical confounds 4 ; it may therefore be possible that the associations we found are independent of clinical characteristics, but further research should confirm this. Our sample was euthymic at the time of testing, so we could not assess the effect of mood state on brain-  cognition associations.  Other  studies  found  that  euthymic  and  depressed BD  patients  may  differ  in  cortical  thickness  in  some  areas, 6 so future research should investigate the effect of mood on braincognition associations.

## 4.3 | Brain morphology

We  used  cortical  thickness  to  measure  structural  abnormalities in  BD.  Previous  research  suggested  that  this  may  be  a  more  sensitive  measure of structural alterations, and more strongly related to cognitive impairment, in BD than other structural metrics. 3-5,52 However, cortical volume and surface area have also been associated  with  core  cognitive  functioning  in  BD,  so  should  not  be  discounted in future studies. 11,55 We also acknowledge that we did not analyse white matter integrity or subcortical regions that are known to be involved in cognition in BD, such as the hippocampus, amygdala and cerebellum. 56

A  limitation  with  measuring  cortical  thickness  concerns  mixed findings of whether poorer cognitive functioning is related to thinner  or  thicker  cortex.  Thinner  cortex  is  generally  assumed  to  be related  to  poorer  cognitive  functioning  in  BD 6,54 ;  however,  some studies found that increased cortical thickness was related to cognitive impairment. 4,53 The direction of the relationship between cortical thickness and cognitive functioning depends on other features of cortical morphology: cortical thickness, volume and surface area have been shown to covary in the healthy human brain according to a universal scaling law. 57 This may limit the interpretability of each metric alone. Wang et al. developed a sophisticated model of brain morphology that accounts for covariance between individual features and provides independent components that appear to be sensitive to structural differences in clinical groups. 57 Future research should utilise these independent components of cortical morphology to more precisely assess neural correlates of cognitive impairment in BD.

## 4.4 | Conclusion

This study is one of the few thus far to utilise normative modelling approaches and multivariate methods to measure brain-  cognition associations in BD and we have demonstrated the utility of CCA for this purpose. The results hint at associations between cortical thickness in  temporal regions and impaired PS and EF in euthymic BD. These results  should  be  interpreted  with  some  caution,  given  the  modest sample size and that our results did not reach statistical significance; however, our study provides an example to which future research can be compared. Given the paucity of multivariate brain-  cognition studies in BD, and difficulties with replicating brain-  cognition associations, future research should continue to leverage large datasets, normative modelling and multivariate approaches to further investigate the neural correlates of cognitive impairment in BD.
