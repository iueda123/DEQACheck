## Analysis of the impact of medication use and episode status on deviation patterns

To further explore the potential impact of medication use and episode incidence on subtype deviation patterns, we conducted detailed subgroup analyses based on patients' medication use and episode status. Specifically, for each subtype, we calculated the mean deviations in brain regions for first- and recurrent-episode patients and compared them using two-sample t-tests; similarly, we compared the mean deviations in brain regions between medicated and non-medicated patients. As revealed in Table S1, the results showed that in both subtype 1 and subtype 2, there were no significant differences in mean deviations across brain regions either between initial and recurrent patients or between medicated and non-medicated patients. These findings further supported that medication use and episode incidence did not significantly affect deviation patterns in this study.

## Validation analysis

To verify the robustness of our findings, we further employed the AAL90 atlas to follow the same analysis process and compared whether the patterns of degree centrality deviations and clustering results were consistent across the two atlases. Initially, following the same procedure as with the Harvard-Oxford atlas, we obtained the mean deviations of all participants within the AAL90 atlas across the seven brain networks defined by the Yeo atlas and calculated the correlation coefficients with the mean deviations of all participants under the same brain networks in the Harvard-Oxford atlas. The correlation analysis revealed high consistency in the deviation patterns across all seven functional brain networks between the two atlases (Table S2).

Moreover, we performed k-means clustering analysis on the deviations of all participants under the AAL90 atlas. The results indicated that the optimal number of clusters remained at k = 2, consistent with the clustering outcomes under the Harvard-Oxford atlas. To further examine the similarity of clustering results across different atlases, we calculated the overlap rate of clustering outcomes between the AAL90 and Harvard-Oxford atlases. We found an overall overlap rate of 0.726, with an overlap rate of 0.701 for subtype 1 and 0.758 for subtype 2, suggesting that despite some anatomical parcellation differences between brain atlases, the classification of MDD subtypes showed high stability across different atlases.

Additionally, we calculated the correlations of the mean deviations within the same subtype across the seven networks between the Harvard-Oxford and AAL90 atlases. The results demonstrated high correlation coefficients for both subtype 1 and subtype 2 across these networks (Table S3). These findings indicated that the clustering results across different atlases maintained high overlap rates both at the overall and subtype levels, further validating the consistency of clustering outcomes across different atlases.
