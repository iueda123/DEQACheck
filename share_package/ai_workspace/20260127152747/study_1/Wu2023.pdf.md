## 1. Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is among the most common psychiatric  conditions  worldwide  and  this  severe  mental  condition  is characterized  by  a  high  risk  of  recurrence  and  a  high  probability  of chronicity (Villalobos et al., 2021). MDD is characterized by high variability  in  clinical  symptoms and has been documented to result in a series  of  neurobiological  changes  in  the  brain,  including  structural changes as in the early phases of the depressive state (e.g. Schmaal et al., 2017), on the other hand, adolescent first episode MDD patients do not seem  to  show  changes  in  brain  volume  compared  to  age-matched healthy controls (Schmaal et al., 2016), suggesting that structural (and functional)  changes  in  the  brain  due  to  MDD  may  appear  later  in

## A B S T R A C T

Background: It has been widely accepted that major depressive disorder (MDD) impacts brain structures including the Corpus Callosum (CC). However, this assumption is based on scarce literature data involving small sample sizes. Furthermore, it is still unclear whether such CC volume changes may already be present at a first depressive episode.

Methods: To further investigate this question, we compared 369 first-episode MDD patients (mean age = 35 years (sd = 12), 249 females; 283 early onset, 86 mid-to-late onset) from the open-source REST meta-MDD database closely matched for age and gender to 490 never-depressed individuals (mean age = 37 years (sd = 14); 309 females) using Z -scores obtained from normative neuroanatomical modeling to assess individual variability in CC (sub)volumes.

Results: Relative  to  the  norms  established  by  the  healthy  controls,  first-episode  MDD  patients  displayed  CC volume (z-score) reductions in the entire CC (including the body), as did mid-to-late-onset first-episode MDD patients (age ≥ 45 y). In early-onset first-episode MDD patients (age ≤ 44 y), depression severity symptoms were related to volume increases in the entire CC, as well as the body and splenium.

Limitations: No data on depressive episode duration. Relatively small sample size for mid-to-late first-episode MDD patients.

Conclusions: Our data revealed CC (sub)volume differences in early versus mid-to-late onset first episode MDD. Especially at early onset, depression severity may result in neural white matter activity as potential reaction to stress influences. Our results underline the importance of prompt clinical interventions at early onset MDD.

When  experiencing  first  episode  MDD  in  adulthood,  reductions in cortical thickness and brain volume have been mostly reported in frontolimbic regions, including the (para)hippocampal areas, representing  brain  areas  involved  in  emotion  regulation,  mood,  and memory, neuropsychological functions affected when clinically depressed (Ahern and Semkovska, 2017; Peng et al., 2016; Varghese et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2021).

Because  of  these  widespread  functional  and  structural  changes throughout the brain, one may assume that one particular large brain structure,  the  Corpus  Callosum  (CC),  located  on  the  brain's  midline, might be crucial to the psychopathology of MDD, including the first episode (Lacerda et al., 2005). As such, the CC is believed to be involved in the hemispheric communication of higher cognitive functions, among which are attention, arousal, language, emotion, and memory to name just a few (Hahn et al., 2015). Whereas in neurological literature, the CC has been put  forward  as  a  keynote  player  involved  in  the  pathophysiology  of several major neurological conditions, including multiple sclerosis and Alzheimer's disease (Chakraborty and Pal, 2019), in adult MDD however, the CC has rarely been put central in structural brain research in psychiatry. The few available studies focusing on CC volumes, mostly including  small  clinical  samples,  have  produced  rather  unequivocal findings (Cheng et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2020; Ozalay et al., 2013; Ran et al., 2020), yielding no clear direction (see Supplemental Table 1 and meta-analytical approach Supplemental Fig. 1). Here it must be added that these studies were not uniform in the used atlases/CC parcelations. In spite of the fact that the evidence is not overwhelming, it is broadly assumed that MDD results in CC volume decreases, predominantly in the prefrontal (genu) parts (Wise et al., 2016). However, it remains to be clarified  whether  such  CC  volumetric  changes  already  occur  when experiencing  a  first  depressive  episode.  Furthermore,  given  the  high variability in depressive symptomatology, more individualized analytical approaches may be more suitable (Rutherford et al., 2022).

Therefore, we investigated CC (sub)volume differences (comprising genu, body, splenium) between first episode MDD patients and age and gender  matched  never-depressed  healthy  controls.  Because  heterogeneity is an important aspect of MDD in symptoms and CC volumes, we examined how CC (sub)volumes in MDD can be parameterized as an individualized metric of matched never-depressed normal controls. We applied  normative  modeling  resulting  in  individualized  CC  (sub)volumes in MDD. We hypothesized that besides the entire CC especially the genu  would  show  volume  decreases.  We  further  examined  whether experiencing an 'early ' or 'mid-to-late ' first depressive episode would affect  CC  (sub)volume differently.  Finally,  we  also  explored  whether depression severity symptoms could affect CC (sub)volumes.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Participants

The structural  brain  imaging  data  were  acquired  from  the  RESTmeta-MDD Consortium (Chen et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2019), which is a  collaborative  effort  involving  twenty-five  research  groups  from  17 hospitals in China. Together, the consortium consisted of 2428 collected datasets  (1300  currently  depressed  individuals  and  1128  matched healthy controls, see Yan et al. (2019) for more details about the inclusion and exclusion criteria). For the current VBM study, based on several  quality  measurements,  participants  were  excluded  for  the following reasons: (1) poor imaging quality (via visual inspection, rating scale score from 1 (bad quality) to 5 (excellent quality), with rating scale score &lt; 4; (2) from a hospital site with patient sample sizes (first episode MDD) smaller  than  10;  (3)  no  information  on  medication  status.  In addition,  all  patients  met  the  Diagnostic  and  Statistical  Manual  of Mental  Disorders-IV  criteria  for  MDD  (First,  1997)  and  were  only included if they had a total score of 14 or more on the 17-item Hamilton Depression  Rating  Scale  (HAMD)  (Hamilton,  1967)  at  the  time  of scanning, indicating moderate to severe depression. According to Zisook et  al.  (2007),  we  further  divided  first-episode  depression  into  'early ' (age ≤ 44y) and 'mid to late ' onset (age ≥ 45y). See also the flow diagram in Fig. 1 and for the age and education frequency distribution in Supplemental Fig. 2.

## 2.2. Brain imaging

Structural T1-weighted MRI brain scans were acquired at each site (see Yan et al. (2019) for MRI acquisition parameters). Besides the entire CC,  based  on  the  JHU  DTI-based  white-matter  atlases,  the  CC  was further  anatomically  divided  in  the  genu,  body,  and  splenium  (see Fig. 2).

## 2.3. Data analysis

## 2.3.1. Behavioral data

All  behavioral  analyses  were  carried  out  in  the  R  language.  The significance level was set at p &lt; 0.05, two-tailed, for all analyses.

## 2.3.2. Imaging data

The  anatomical  images  were  preprocessed  using  the  SPM12  (htt p://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) toolbox. The pre-processing steps included  segmentation,  bias-correction,  and  normalization  using  the DARTEL  algorithm  (Ashburner,  2007).  The  segmented  white  matter images were modulated and resampled into 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm. Finally, quality controls of all images were conducted before further statistical analysis.

## 2.3.3. Statistical analysis

We applied the neuroanatomical normative  modeling  to  examine individual-level  variability  in  CC  (sub)volume  using  the  PCNToolkit (v.0.18) package (https://pcntoolkit.readthedocs.io). Normative modeling essentially aims to predict centiles of variance in a response variable  (CC  (sub)volume)  on  the  basis  of  a  set  of  covariates.  To accommodate inter-site variation, hierarchical Bayesian regression was employed  to  train  the  normative  models  on  healthy  participants (Rutherford et al., 2022). Given that gender and age related differences in  the  morphology  of  the  Corpus  Callosum  have  been  reported  (e.g. Suganthy et al., 2003), and that intelligence may be an influential factor (e.g. Luders et al., 2007), we fitted normative models using gender, age, education, and total intracranial volume as fixed effects covariates to predict CC (sub)volumes. This model was further used to estimate zscores that indicate to what extent an individual's CC volume differs from the norms calculated by the reference dataset (healthy controls). A one-sample t -test on the resulting z-scores of first episode MDD patients was then performed to determine the statistical significance of the mean deviation  (z-scores).  Given  the  hypothesis  of  CC  (sub)volume  reductions, significance was set at p &lt; 0.05, one-tailed.

To evaluate the effects of depression severity, we computed three separate Spearman correlations between the HAMD scores and CC (sub) volume z-scores, 1) for the whole first episode MDD group; 2) at early onset (age ≤ 44y), and 3) at mid-to-late onset (age ≥ 45y). Medication

## 3. Results

When considering the whole sample, the two groups (healthy controls,  first  episode  MDD) did not differ in age and gender. However, education was significantly different between groups, with higher education levels in the control group ( p &lt; 0.01, see Table 1). As shown in Supplemental Fig. 2, the education distribution in the HC group covers all levels of education observed in the MDD group. The normative model can  effectively  capture  the  contribution  of  education  to  CC  volume prediction (z-score) in individuals with MDD. Concerning the VBM results,  compared  to  healthy  controls'  norms,  we  found  significant  CC (sub)volume(s) z-scores reductions in the first episode MDD group in the entire CC ( p = 0.04) and in the body of the CC ( p = 0.02, FDR corrected). Depression severity did not influence the CC (sub)volume(s) z-scores ( p 's &gt; 0.05, see Table 2).

When  looking  at  early  onset  (age ≤ 44  y)  and  mid-to-late  first episode onset (age ≥ 45 y) depressed patients separately, both groups did not significantly differ in depression severity scores (HAMD) ( p = 0.08). By definition the two groups significantly differed in age, however, they did not significantly differ in gender ( p = 0.24). Also, education  was  significantly  different  between  both  groups  ( p = 0.02), showing a lower level of education in the late onset first episode MDD patients. We found no significant CC (sub)volume z-scores differences between  early  and  mid-to-late  onset  first  episode  depression  (see Table 2).

On the other hand, relative to the norms computed by the healthy controls,  we  found  that  mid-to-late  onset  first  episode  MDD  showed significant CC volume z-scores decreases for the entire CC ( p = 0.02) as well as for the body of the CC ( p = 0.01, FDR corrected). Furthermore, although we did not find significant CC (sub)volume differences for the early onset first episode MDD patients, depression severity scores were significantly associated with increased entire CC volume z-scores ( r = 0.15, p = 0.01), including body ( r = 0.15, p = 0.03, FDR corrected) and splenium ( r = 0.14, p = 0.03, FDR corrected). ## 4. Discussion

This large structural brain study examined the impact of the first episode ('early ' and 'mid-to-late ' onset) MDD in comparison to neverdepressed  healthy  individuals,  exclusively  focusing  on  Corpus  Callosum (sub)volumes, using an innovative normative modeling approach.

Looking at the whole sample, our findings show significant volume zscore decreases in the entire CC (and body) in MDD patients in the first episode, supporting the hypothesis that when one first becomes clinically depressed, this is accompanied by significant CC volume decreases (see Supplemental Table 1), however not as expected in the genu. This is of  interest  because  neural  activity  within  the  medioprefrontal  brain structures, structurally connected to the ventromedial parts of the brain via  the  anterior  forceps  with  the  genu  (Sarikcioglu  et  al.,  2007),  is mostly found to be increased during the depressive state (Rive et al., 2013; Wu and Baeken, 2023), reflecting its involvement in (dysfunctional) emotional regulation processes. Moreover, our mid-to-late first episode onset MDD patients (age ≥ 45y) showed similar CC volume zscore decreases (entire CC, and body). No such (sub)volumic changes were observed for early onset first episode MDD patients. Although our results do not completely support previous observations where in adult MDD (and bipolar depression) no differences in entire CC volumes were reported  (Piras  et  al.,  2021),  it  has  been  suggested  that  the  neural integrity  of  the  CC  is  compromised  only  late  life  depression  (Emsell et al., 2017), regardless of early adverse life events, such as childhood maltreatment (Ritchie et al., 2012). In line with the current observations, it has been reported that in late life depression (mean age = 72.4 y, sd = 4.8), CC (sub)volumes decreased in genu and splenium (Hahn et al., 2015), suggesting that the effects of depressive symptoms at an older age may affect these deep white matter structures more profoundly. Also in our depressed cohort the CC volume decreases were more apparent in the mid-to-late first episode MDD patients (age ≥ 45y), however, the mean age here was clearly younger (mean age = 53.15 y, sd = 6.30).

Although  no  relationships  with  the  HAMD  scores  were  observed when including the whole sample or looking only at 'mid-to-late first episode MDD, in early onset MDD patients (age ≤ 44y), we found that the more severe the depressive symptoms the larger the entire CC volume, including body and splenium. This seems to somewhat contradict the  assumption  that  depression  severity  more  profoundly  affects  the frontolimbic circuits (genu). It is interesting to note that thicker and more strongly myelinated axons are found most frequently in truncus and splenium, whereas thinner and slightly myelinated axons are found predominantly in the anterior parts of the CC (Danielsen et al., 2020).

White matter increase can be interpreted as a compensatory strategy via early inflammatory  responses, leading to the activation of proinflammatory cytokines, followed by astrocyte proliferation, which results in white matter volume increases; possibly in an attempt to restore earlier efficient brain function (Carceller-Sindreu et al., 2019). Also Li et al. (2020) suggested that increases in cortical thickness may result from the dual activation of the immune-inflammatory response system and  the  compensatory  immune-regulatory  reflex  system,  resulting  in pre-apoptotic  osmotic  changes,  cellular  hypertrophy,  and  cytokineactivated astrocyte proliferation (Li et al., 2020). As such, the observed corpus callosum 'hypertrophy ' in more severely first episode depressed patients may represent a compensatory, neuroplastic response in an effort to attenuate depressive symptomatology (Bansal et al., 2018).

It has been reported that early onset MDD patients showed increases in the white matter of the corpus callosum, the corticospinal midbrain, and  the  inferior  fronto-occipital  fasciculus,  thus  distinguishing  early from late onset MDD (Cheng et al., 2014). It has been suggested that CC volume enlargements may occur in depressed patients with a family history of MDD (Lacerda et al., 2005), or greater CC fractional anisotropy  in  unaffected  first-degree  healthy  relatives  who  experienced adverse events in early life (Frodl et al., 2012). Although we had no information on potential stressful early life events, it has been documented that this could be related to depression severity and mediated by white  matter  alterations  (Wang  et  al.,  2022).  Interestingly,  stressexposed mice exhibit reduced oligodendrocyte volumes in the cortex, but  increased  oligodendrocyte  volumes  in  the  corpus  callosum  was found to be accompanied by an excess of arborisation of oligodendrocyte processes (Poletti et al., 2018). Although speculative, in assuming that severity of depression is related to higher stress levels and stress hormones at the early stages of depression may result in white matter increases, our results imply that the increased CC (sub)volumes represent a compensatory neurobiological defense mechanism due to a disturbed stress system, especially for first-episode early onset MDD patients. This also appears to be independent of whether these patients were taking antidepressants, which is in line with recent reviews that were unable to conclude  whether  antidepressant  treatment  significantly  alters  gray matter volumes (Enneking et al., 2020).

Several limitations of the study need to be discussed. First, although not considered a major limitation, we should note that all recruitment sites were in Asia (China). Second, we could not take into account the duration of the depressive episode, due to the unavailability of these data (Zhao et al., 2021). Third, although based on current literature, the split  between  first  and  late  onset  first  depressive  episode  should  be considered as an arbitrary choice, and other cut-off scores have been used. Furthermore, the sample size of the mid-to-late-onset first-episode MDD patients was relatively small, so here we cannot exclude potential type I and type II errors. Fourth, although not the scope of our study, due to  the  lack  of  this  information,  other  factors such  as  cognitive information (Yamada et al., 2015), specific depressive characteristics (e.g., melancholic vs. non-melancholic, anxiety characteristics), or history or current suicide attempts were not included in the analyses that could influence  the  CC  (sub)volume  integrity  and  structural  connections (Coloigner et al., 2019). Lastly, regarding CC subvolume parcellation, we used only three CC subvolumes, but other parcellation atlases of the CC are available.

In conclusion, in line with previous structural findings of the CC, we found (sub)volume reductions of the CC, especially in the mid- posterior part of the CC, and especially in MDD patients with a 'mid-to-late ' onset. On  the  other  hand,  depression  severity  in  early  first  episode  MDD correlated  positively  with  CC  volume  (including  the  mid-posterior parts),  indicating  (re)activity  of  the  white  matter  against  stress  influences inherent in the depressive state. Adequate antidepressant interventions  in  especially  in  early  onset  first  episode  MDD  seem warranted.
