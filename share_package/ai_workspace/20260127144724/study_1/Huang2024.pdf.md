## Lifespan development of thalamic nuclei and characterizing thalamic nuclei abnormalities in schizophrenia using normative modeling

Anna S. Huang 1 ✉ , Kaidi Kang 2 , Simon Vandekar 2 , Baxter P. Rogers 3 , Stephan Heckers 1 and Neil D. Woodward 1

Thalamic abnormalities have been repeatedly implicated in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia and other neurodevelopmental disorders. Uncovering the etiology of thalamic abnormalities and how they may contribute to illness phenotypes faces at least two obstacles. First, the typical developmental trajectories of thalamic nuclei and their association with cognition across the lifespan are largely unknown. Second, modest effect sizes indicate marked individual differences and pose a signi /uniFB01 cant challenge to personalized medicine. To address these knowledge gaps, we characterized the development of thalamic nuclei volumes using normative models generated from the Human Connectome Project Lifespan datasets (5 -100 + years), then applied them to an independent clinical cohort to determine the frequency of thalamic volume deviations in people with schizophrenia (17 -61 years). Normative models revealed diverse non-linear age effects across the lifespan. Association nuclei exhibited negative age effects during youth but stabilized in adulthood until turning negative again with older age. Sensorimotor nuclei volumes remained relatively stable through youth and adulthood until also turning negative with older age. Up to 18% of individuals with schizophrenia exhibited abnormally small (i.e., below the 5th centile) mediodorsal and pulvinar volumes, and the degree of deviation, but not raw volumes, correlated with the severity of cognitive impairment. While case -control differences are robust, only a minority of patients demonstrate unusually small thalamic nuclei volumes. Normative modeling enables the identi /uniFB01 cation of these individuals, which is a necessary step toward precision medicine.

## INTRODUCTION

Comprising the apex of the diencephalon, the thalamus consists of a collection of nuclei that play essential roles in relaying sensory information to the cortex, regulating cortical activity and arousal, motor function, and cognition [1 -5]. Thalamic nuclei can be assigned to two broad categories, /uniFB01 rst-order and higher-order, based largely on differences in the source of their inputs. Firstorder nuclei encompass nuclei that relay sensory information from the periphery to the cortex and are essential for processing sensory information. The lateral geniculate nucleus, which relays sensory inputs from the retina to the primary visual cortex, is a prototypical /uniFB01 rst-order nucleus. In contrast, higher-order nuclei receive their primary inputs directly from the cortex and are critical to cognitive functions such as attention and cognitive control [2, 6 -8].

Likely by virtue of its heterogeneous structure and functional diversity, the thalamus has been implicated in both developmental disorders and disorders of aging, including schizophrenia [9 -15], attention de /uniFB01 cit hyperactivity disorder [16 -19], autism [20 -25], and Alzheimer ' s disease [26 -31]. In schizophrenia, structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have consistently identi /uniFB01 ed smaller mediodorsal and pulvinar volumes, but largely normal somatomotor nuclei volumes [10, 11, 15].

Structural /uniFB01 ndings are complemented by connectivity studies showing a pattern of hypo-connectivity in higher-order association networks and hyper-connectivity in /uniFB01 rst-order sensorimotor networks [32 -34].

Thalamic involvement in the pathology and mechanisms of these disorders and its role in age-related changes in functions supported by thalamocortical circuits are constrained by our relatively limited understanding of thalamus development across the lifespan. Anatomically, the volume of the whole thalamus exhibits a nonlinear pattern of development characterized by a rapid increase in volume from infancy, peaking in mid-adolescence, followed by a gradual decline into old age [35, 36]. The functions that /uniFB01 rst-order and higher-order nuclei sub-serve and the cortical regions that they are connected to mature at different rates [37, 38]. For example, visual processes typically mature by mid-adolescence [39], while functions such as cognitive control do not reach maturation until young adulthood [40]. Functional connectivity between the sensorimotor thalamus and sensorimotor cortical networks showed lower connectivity with age in youth, while higher-order thalamic nuclei typically show little change in functional connectivity in developmental samples [41, 42]. This divergence of structure, function, and connectivity indicates that thalamic nuclei volumes likely show differential patterns of development across the lifespan. For example, nuclei close to the ventricles (e.g., medial geniculate or mediodorsal nuclei) exhibit faster rates of atrophy than more laterally located nuclei (e.g., ventral anterior or ventral posterolateral nuclei) [44].

The advent of large-scale neuroimaging datasets has created novel opportunities to characterize age effects in the brain across much of the lifespan. Aggregating large-scale neuroimaging data has led to the development and application of normative modeling to derive precise measures of deviation at the individual level [38, 45 -47]. In the case of schizophrenia, thalamic abnormalities have been reliably identi /uniFB01 ed in group comparisons. Yet these differences typically show modest effect sizes and considerable overlap with healthy individuals, indicating thalamus abnormality in schizophrenia is highly heterogeneous. Identifying which individuals show abnormality is essential to unraveling the complex etiology of schizophrenia and precision medicine. With that in mind, the goals of this study are to: 1) characterize age effects on thalamic nuclei using normative models; 2) clarify the association between cognition and thalamic nuclei volumes, and determine how they change over time; and 3) apply our normative models of thalamic nuclei development to determine the frequency of thalamic structure abnormalities in an independent sample of individuals with schizophrenia and examine if deviation scores were associated with cognitive impairment.

## METHODS

## Study participants and procedures

Human Connectome Project Lifespan cohort . Normative modeling of thalamic nuclei development was conducted using data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) Lifespan Studies (combined n = 2490). The HCP Lifespan studies include three independent large-scale, publicly available population-based samples of typically developing individuals covering the human lifespan from ages 5 -100 + : the original HCP S1200 sample (ages 21 -35, n = 1113); HCP Development Lifespan (2.0 release, ages 5 -21, n = 652); and HCP Aging Lifespan (2.0 release, ages 36-100 + , n = 725).

The original HCP S1200 sample [48] was obtained from ConnectomeDB (https://db.humanconnectome.org/), and the HCP Development [49] and Aging [50] Lifespan datasets were obtained from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Data Archive. After randomly removing twins to avoid biasing the normative lifespan model and excluding individuals with poor quality scans or segmentation failures, the /uniFB01 nal sample was 1952 (HCP S1200 n = 825, Development HCP n = 525, Aging HCP n = 725). Detailed demographics and exclusion information from all studies are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

Schizophrenia cohort . Individual deviations in thalamus volumes were drawn from a repository study comprising 643 individuals who participated in one of three neuroimaging projects (CT00762866, R01MH070560, R01MH102266). Diagnoses were con /uniFB01 rmed, or ruled out in the case of healthy individuals, using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV. Clinical symptoms in individuals with a psychotic disorder were further assessed with the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), and premorbid IQ was estimated using the Wechsler Test for Adult Reading (WTAR). Exclusion criteria included the following: signi /uniFB01 cant medical or neurological illness, head injury, pregnancy, age under 15 or above 65, premorbid IQ less than 65, substance abuse or dependence (at least 1 month in patients, lifetime in healthy controls), and any MRI contraindications. Healthy individuals were excluded if they had any current or past psychiatric illnesses or a /uniFB01 rst-degree relative with a psychotic illness. See Supplementary Materials for the CONSORT diagram detailing people we excluded for diagnosis or image quality. Our /uniFB01 nal sample included 211 healthy controls (ranging in age from 15 to 58) and 234 individuals with a schizophrenia spectrum diagnosis (ranging in age from 17 to 61). Basic participant demographics for this sample are included in Table 1. Expanded demographics can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

## Neuropsychological assessment

Human Connectome Project Lifespan cohort . Neuropsychological functioning in this cohort was collected with instruments from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) toolbox, an age-standardized set of task-based measures [51]. Previous con /uniFB01 rmatory factor analyses identi /uniFB01 ed a 5-factor model as best describing cognitive domains in the NIH toolbox, including an executive function domain [52]. As we were interested in the association between executive function scores and higher-order thalamic nuclei volumes, we averaged the index scores from the three NIH toolbox instruments that loaded on the executive function domain in previous studies, and that was consistently collected across the three HCP projects: the Flanker task, the dimensional card sort test, and the pattern completion processing speed test.

Schizophrenia cohort . Neuropsychological functioning in the Schizophrenia Cohort was assessed with the Screen for Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry (SCIP: [53]). Brie /uniFB02 y, the SCIP consists of /uniFB01 ve sub-tests: verbal memory (immediate and delayed), working memory, verbal /uniFB02 uency, and processing speed. The SCIP is a reliable and valid measure of cognitive ability in psychotic disorders [54]. SCIP subtest raw scores were converted to z-scores using normative data and averaged to create a composite

z-score, which served as the primary dependent variable in the analyses described below.

## Neuroimaging data acquisition and preprocessing

The HCP Lifespan Cohort was collected at four locations across multiple scanners. The Schizophrenia Cohort was collected at a single location with three sets of parameters (see Supplemental Materials). Imaging acquisition parameters for each project are presented in the Supplementary Materials. Preprocessing of the raw T1 images from the HCP Lifespan Cohort was done using the PreFreeSurfer HCP pipeline [55], which outputs bias /uniFB01 eld corrected structural T1 images in ' native ' subject space. The Schizophrenia Cohort did not undergo preprocessing as our standard T1 images were suf /uniFB01 cient for thalamus segmentation. All T1 images underwent visual inspection for visible artifacts, including motion artifacts, before thalamic segmentation.

## Thalamus segmentation

Thalamic nuclei were segmented using the Thalamus Optimized MultiAtlas Segmentation (THOMAS) [56]. THOMAS uses a multi-atlas approach to segment the thalamus into 12 subdivisions per hemisphere. The multiatlas was created from thalamic nuclei manually segmented by expert neuroradiologists from 20 white-matter nulled MPRAGE datasets acquired at 7T resolution, which provides the contrast necessary to visualize thalamic nuclei in MRI images. THOMAS outputs have greater reliability than thalamic nuclei segmented using Freesurfer and also performed well when compared against manually segmented thalamic nuclei [56].

Thalamus segmentations derived from THOMAS were visually inspected for quality by overlaying representative slices of each individual ' s thalamus segmentation on their standard T1 image. Individuals were excluded from further analysis if their thalamus segmentation included voxels outside the thalamus or missed voxels within the thalamus. Of the 12 subdivisions output from THOMAS, we excluded the habenula and mammillothalamic tract segmentations from further analysis, then combined the anterior and posterior ventrolateral nuclei subdivisions into a single ventrolateral nucleus segmentation. This resulted in nine thalamic nuclei volumes used for further analysis, consisting of the /uniFB01 rst-order lateral geniculate (LGN), medial geniculate (MGN), ventral posterolateral (VPL), ventral anterior (VA), and ventrolateral (VL) nuclei, and the higher-order anteroventral (AV), pulvinar (PU), mediodorsal (MD), and the centromedian (CM) nuclei.

## Data harmonization

To account for site effects, we used ComBat to adjust for site and scanner effects on both the HCP Lifespan Cohort and our Schizophrenia Cohort using the neuroCombat R package (https://github.com/Jfortin1/ neuroCombat\_Rpackage) prior to analysis. A key feature of ComBat is its ability to correct for the batch/site effects while preserving the inter-batch biological signals of interest by explicitly modeling and preserving the covariates of interest (including age, sex, race, and diagnosis). ComBat models the batch effects as additive and multiplicative components in the data and uses an empirical Bayesian framework to allow for robust estimation of batch effect parameters. Raw thalamic nuclei volumes extracted from THOMAS were harmonized for 12 sites (HCP Lifespan Cohort included 9 sites; Schizophrenia Cohort included 3 sites), with age, sex, race, and diagnoses included as covariates to adjust for during harmonization. Further details for the sites are provided in the Supplemental Materials.

## Modeling normative change over the lifespan

Normative modeling of thalamic nuclei change over the lifespan was conducted using generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS [57]); a robust and /uniFB02 exible framework for modeling nonlinear growth trajectories. GAMLSS models were /uniFB01 tted to thalamic nuclei volumes separately for each hemisphere to model nonlinear age effects (on the outcome mean and variance) over the lifespan (ranging 5 -100), strati /uniFB01 ed by sex, race, intracranial volumes and included random intercepts for families to account for the correlation between participants from the same families in the HCP Lifespan Cohort. See Supplementary Materials for further details regarding GAMLSS model speci /uniFB01 cation and estimation.

## Centile scoring: infra- and supra-normal volumes

We computed individual centile scores for all individuals in our Schizophrenia Cohort to place each individual based on their distance from the median of the age-normed, sex, and race-strati /uniFB01 ed distributions provided by the reference sample (HCP Lifespan Cohort) [38]. This approach allows us to benchmark each individual ' s thalamic nuclei volumes by their typicality, with more atypical individuals having more extreme centile scores.

Once centile scores have been calculated for each individual ' s set of thalamic nuclei volumes, we then categorized their volumes as either: 1) normal if their centile scores fell within the 5th and 95th centiles, 2) supranormal if their centile scores were above the 95th centile, and 3) infranormal if their centiles fell below the 5th centile. This classi /uniFB01 cation provided individualized thalamus deviation maps in which each thalamus subdivision was numerically coded as 1 (supra-normal), 0 (normal), or -1 (infra-normal).

## Statistical analyses

Association between executive function and volumes for different developmental stages . We used generalized estimating equation models (GEE) to quantify the association between executive function and thalamic nuclei volumes, including sex, intracranial volumes, and family membership as covariates for the anteroventral, pulvinar and mediodorsal nuclei. We used an exchangeable correlation structure to control for the correlation between subjects from the same families. By using GEE models, we were able to compare how associations between executive function and nuclei volumes differ between the developmental, young adult, and aging datasets in the HCP lifespan project, as all three age groups were included in one model. Analyses were implemented using the geepack R package Version 1.3.9. To correct for multiple comparisons in 18 tests, we present the False Discovery Rate (FDR) adjusted p values.

Differences in proportions of extreme deviations between schizophrenia and healthy control groups . To better understand how well group differences characterize individual patients, we applied the normative models to operationalize the normative range of expected variation in typically developing individuals as between 5% and 95% of the population for a given age, sex, race, and estimated total intracranial volume (ICV).

As centile scores follow a non-normal distribution, we opted for nonparametric tests for signi /uniFB01 cance. To test group differences in centile scores between healthy control and schizophrenia groups for each thalamic region, we used the Wilcoxon rank sum test for independent samples as implemented in R Version 4.1.3. To test differences in proportions of individuals with infra- and supra-normal volumes between healthy control and schizophrenia groups, we used Pearson ' s Chi-squared test as implemented in R Version 4.1.3. To correct for multiple comparisons in 18 tests, we present the FDR-adjusted p values.

Association between deviation scores and cognition . We tested associations between deviation (centile) scores and cognitive function using Spearman ' s Rho, a nonparametric test for correlation strength. Cognitive function was measured with the SCIP composite z-score. We then qualitatively compared the associations between centile scores and raw volumes with cognitive function. To increase comparability, we used standardized raw scores after regressing for age, sex, race, and ICV, the variables that were included in our normative models. To correct for multiple comparisons, p values were adjusted across 18 nuclei with FDR correction.

## DISCUSSION

Using normative models to characterize the development of thalamic nuclei volumes over the lifespan in a cross-sectional dataset, we found that while all thalamic nuclei volumes were smallest in old age, there were differential trajectories through development and aging. The mediodorsal, centromedian, and pulvinar nuclei showed prominently smaller volumes with age in youth, leveling out in young adulthood in the mediodorsal and centromedian nuclei. In comparison, the lateral geniculate and ventral posterolateral nuclei showed stable volumes in youth through young adulthood. The remaining nuclei we examined demonstrated less clear effects of age in youth.

Compared with our /uniFB01 ndings of decreasing volumes with age across the lifespan, previous lifespan studies showed a Poissonshaped relationship from birth to old age, with a rapid increase in thalamic volumes in development followed by a more gradual decline in aging [35, 36]. It is important to note that our normative model starts at age 5, compared to other studies, which start from birth. In these previous studies, the de /uniFB02 ection point at which increases in volume shift to decreases typically occurred in midadolescence, between 10 and 17 years. This difference in the age at which we start our lifespan model is likely to affect the shape of the developmental curves we observe. brain changes shows this more declining pattern. The decline in development observed in several nuclei is consistent with what might be expected based on non-human animal and post-mortem literature. In rodents, the mediodorsal nucleus shows a pattern of development that precedes and mirrors that of the prefrontal cortex, its primary cortical counterpart, with increased connections that peak in juvenile mice, followed by a profound decrease in innervations into adulthood [58]. In humans, a post-mortem study found that the mediodorsal nucleus had an average of 41% fewer neurons in the adult brain than in neonates [59]. The imaging literature on whole thalamus development in youth is more mixed. Some studies found no change in thalamic volumes with age in youth [60, 61], while others found largely curvilinear change, showing greater volumes with age until late adolescence, followed by a plateau in young adulthood [62, 63]. Narvacan et al. found increasing volumes in youth when examining a crosssectional dataset. However, they found no change in thalamus volumes in a separate longitudinal developmental cohort. Our results may shed some light on these discrepant /uniFB01 ndings, as we showed heterogeneous patterns of development across different nuclei in youth. Some nuclei showed change with age in youth, while others did not. This suggests that the nuclei of the thalamus do not develop in concert and highlights the importance of examining individual nuclei.

Our /uniFB01 ndings of declining volumes with aging across all nuclei are consistent with imaging literature in aging adults in crosssectional [64 -69] and longitudinal datasets [36, 66]. While the /uniFB01 nding of decline with aging is consistent, these rates may differ across nuclei. A recent study showed that nuclei involved in cognitive (MD, AV) and visual functions (LGN, PU) showed faster annual declines with age than those involved in motor (VA, VL) and somatosensory (VPL) functions. Alternatively, nuclei that lie adjacent to the ventricles (AV, MD, LGN, pulvinar, and MGN) showed faster rates of decline (0.59% per year) than those that were more laterally located (CM, VA, VLA, VLP, and VPL), (0.36% per year) [44]. This may be related to the prominent enlargement of ventricles observed in aging.

We have previously shown a modest positive association between cognition and mediodorsal and pulvinar volumes in youth [10]. Both cognition and thalamic nuclei volumes showed a non-linear effect of age across the lifespan. Therefore, we tested whether this association differed as a function of the developmental stage. Our results suggest a difference in the association between executive function and mediodorsal and pulvinar volumes in youth compared to adults. There was a notable negative association between executive function and volumes in youth, which shifted to a modest positive association in young adults that was strengthened in older adults. This /uniFB01 nding of a negative association in youth is not unexpected, based on our /uniFB01 nding of declining mediodorsal and pulvinar nuclei volumes in youth. This decline in volumes may be due to maturation processes, such as dendritic pruning, though we cannot determine which processes using MRI. Previous literature has found similar relationships between cortical thinning and executive function in developmental samples [70]. In older adults, both declines in executive function and thalamic nuclei volumes are more likely due to aging processes. In young adults, there is a weaker association between executive function and volumes, indicating more individual variance during this more stable stage of development. As we were interested in the effects of age, the current analyses differ from previous investigations as we used the age-unadjusted index scores for cognition, thus differing from previous investigations, which typically use age-standardized cognitive measures and include age as a covariate.

In addition to informing typical brain development, normative models can be used to characterize brain abnormalities in individuals within speci /uniFB01 c diagnostic groups. Application of the normative references generated from healthy HCP samples to an independent group of healthy individuals and patients with schizophrenia revealed a signi /uniFB01 cantly higher proportion of patients with infra-normal bilateral mediodorsal, pulvinar, and medial geniculate nuclei volumes. Other nuclei also showed higher frequencies of individuals with infra-normal volumes, ranging between 9 -18% across nuclei. This /uniFB01 nding is consistent with case-control reports of smaller mediodorsal and pulvinar volumes in schizophrenia groups [10 -12, 15], but helps to explain the generally modest effect sizes observed in case-control studies. The frequency of infra-normal thalamic volumes was on par with studies that examined deviation in cortical thickness, where no more than 18% of individuals showed infra-normal deviations [47]. These /uniFB01 ndings in people with a schizophrenia diagnosis can be contrasted with a recent study using normative modeling in a clinical high-risk (CHR) sample, which found largely overlapping deviance between the clinical high-risk and typically developing groups [71]. Though, they did /uniFB01 nd there were differences between typically developing and clinical high-risk individuals who later converted to a psychotic disorder, indicating those who later converted to a psychosis disorder may differ from typically developing individuals. Interestingly, we did not observe an increased frequency of individuals with supra-normal volumes in our schizophrenia group, suggesting that infra-normal volumes are not the result of an overall shift in the schizophrenia sample towards smaller volumes. It is possible that infra-normal volumes represent a subgroup within schizophrenia that presents with smaller thalamic nuclei volumes, though further investigation extensively characterizing the behavioral and clinical phenomenology of this group is needed. The observed association between thalamus volumes and cognition warrants further investigation into whether infra-normal thalamic volumes may be related to cognitive pro /uniFB01 les within schizophrenia [72].

We explored the idea that deviations might be a better signal for examining associations with behavioral measures in clinical samples, as they account for variation observed in the general population. Association between thalamic nuclei volumes and cognition have been observed in large samples of youth and adults [10] and is present in our healthy developmental sample, recognizing that measures of executive function and general neuropsychological functioning are not equivalent. However, effect sizes are typically modest, requiring large samples. In our schizophrenia sample, we showed a qualitatively stronger association with cognition when using deviation scores compared with raw volumes. It is possible that in smaller patient samples, deviation scores may serve as a better metric against which to study associations with behavior in clinical samples.

Several limitations of this study merit consideration. The dataset used to model typical development comes from cross-sectional, not longitudinal, studies. A previous study found different patterns of age effects on the thalamus between cross-sectional and longitudinal data [36]. However, a larger study using normative modeling to examine developmental change across the brain regions found that the cross-sectional datasets provided a good approximation of developmental patterns observed in longitudinal datasets [38]. Another limitation of our data was that we started modeling development at 5 years of age. While extensive development is expected after age 5, we are missing early development, especially in the nuclei where earlier development would be expected, such as the lateral geniculate nucleus [73]. In addition, the data used for both developing models of typical development and identifying deviation in schizophrenia were from multiple different projects with different scanning parameters, and data harmonization was necessary prior to analyses. As such, the models developed are not readily applicable to new datasets, and further work will be required to develop more /uniFB02 exible models that can be applied to novel datasets, such as has been implemented in many cortical and subcortical regions [74, 75]. Finally, alternative factors that co-segregate with schizophrenia,

including antipsychotic medication and adverse lifestyle factors (e.g., increased substance use), may adversely impact the thalamus and partially account for the increased proportion of patients demonstrating deviant volumes [76, 77].

In conclusion, across nine thalamic nuclei, we found heterogeneous patterns in development and largely homogenous decline in aging. In our typically developing sample, there was a shift from a negative association between executive function and volumes in youth to a positive association in young and older adults. Using normative models, we observed a higher frequency of infra-normal volumes in the bilateral mediodorsal, pulvinar, and left medial geniculate nuclei in an independent cohort of individuals with schizophrenia compared with healthy individuals, whereas the frequency of individuals with supra-normal volumes did not differ in our schizophrenia sample compared with healthy controls. Whether the proportion and pattern of deviations are speci /uniFB01 c to schizophrenia or transcend diagnostic categories, is unknown. Finally, we found that deviation scores showed a stronger association with cognition in our schizophrenia sample and propose that using brain deviation scores might be a promising avenue for investigating brain -behavior associations in clinical populations.
