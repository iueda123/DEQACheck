## INTRODUCTION

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a globally prevalent psychiatric condition, characterized by persistent low mood, anhedonia and decreased energy, which can lead to severe outcomes such as suicide. Patients with MDD exhibit considerable variability in symptoms, disease progression, and treatment responses, potentially driven by distinct neurobiological abnormalities [1 -4]. This clinical heterogeneity presents a signi /uniFB01 cant challenge for understanding and treating MDD. Identifying more biologically homogeneous subtypes within the broad clinical diagnosis of MDD may help to unravel this complexity, ultimately improving both diagnosis and treatment. In clinical practice, psychiatrists often categorize MDD patients into subtypes based on symptom pro /uniFB01 les [5]. Despite the great success, clinical phenotypes may result from different underlying biological mechanisms and interact with neurological processes in complex ways [6, 7]. An alternative approach is to identify neurophysiological subtypes using objective neuroimaging biomarkers [3, 8].

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) is a powerful tool for examining altered functional organization in clinical populations [9]. Functional connectivity, measured by correlating spontaneous /uniFB02 uctuations in MRI signals, provides a framework for understanding various aspects of human behavior [10]. Previous rsfMRI studies have shown that MDD is associated with dysfunction and abnormal functional connectivity in frontostriatal and limbic networks [11], consistent with /uniFB01 ndings from chronic stress models in rodents [12 -14]. Patients with different MDD subtypes exhibit distinct patterns of functional connectivity abnormalities, and hence these pro /uniFB01 les have been used to identify novel neurobiological subtypes in neuropsychiatric disorders [3, 7, 15 -18]. However, several challenges remain in identifying neuroimaging-based subtypes of MDD. First, it remains dif /uniFB01 cult to extract features that best capture the neurobiological

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Imaging dataset and preprocessing

The study was approved by the research ethical committee of the First Af /uniFB01 liated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (2018-KY-88), all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The imaging datasets for MDD used in this study were sourced from the REST-meta-MDD consortium (http://rfmri.org/RESTmeta-MDD) [31, 32].These data include rsfMRI data from 25 research sites, comprising 1276 MDD patients (mean age: 36.23 ± 21.38 years, 63.71% females) and 1104 HCs (mean age: 36.15 ± 24.55 years, 58.06% females). Ethical approval for data collection was obtained from local research ethics committees at all sites. MDD diagnoses were made by experienced psychiatrists according to DSM-IV criteria, with symptom severity assessed using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) [33]. The MDD cohort, included 538 /uniFB01 rst-episode individuals, 282 recurrent cases, 447 medication-naive patients and 408 patients receiving treatment (Table 1). Further demographic, clinical and imaging details are available in previous publications [31, 32].

Preprocessing of the fMRI data followed standard analysis protocols across study sites to minimize analytical variation. Detailed descriptions of these steps are provided elsewhere [31, 32]. For this study, we utilized the released mean time courses for each brain region, as de /uniFB01 ned in the Craddock 200 brain atlas, provided by the REST-meta-MDD consortium (http://rfmri.org/REST-meta-MDD) [34]. Global signal regression was not applied, given its potential implications for the understanding the pathogenesis of mental disorders [35, 36].

## Identifying extreme functional connections in MDD

To identify extreme functional connections in MDD patients relative to HCs, we followed a series of steps. First, we used the ' tolerance ' package in R (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/tolerance/tolerance.pdf) to estimate nonparametric two-sided tolerance intervals for each functional connection in HCs. Following prior studies, we set a 90% tolerance proportion with a 10% con /uniFB01 dence level [22]. Functional connections in individual subjects (both MDD and HCs) fell outside this range were classi /uniFB01 ed as extreme. Next, enrichment analysis, based on the hypergeometric cumulative distribution function, was then performed to identify connections that were signi /uniFB01 cantly more prevalent in MDD patients compared to HCs [40]. In neuroimaging, enrichment analysis quanti /uniFB01 es the signi /uniFB01 cance of the overlap between connectivity /uniFB01 ndings and previously de /uniFB01 ned functional networks [41, 42]. This process was repeated 100 times, with 90% of the MDD patients randomly subsampled in each iteration. A consistency rate, representing the proportion of repetitions in which a connection was selected, was computed for each connection. Those with a consistency rate greater than 95% were designated as extreme functional connections in MDD patients and categorized into seven canonical resting networks [43]. For comparison, group-level abnormal functional connections were also identi /uniFB01 ed using two-sample t-tests.

## Association between extreme functional connections and cognitive terms and clinical characteristics

To further interpret the extreme functional connections in MDD, we performed a functional enrichment analysis, which associates these connections with cognitive processes/functional terms. Similar to gene enrichment analysis, this method assesses whether two regions connected by a functional link are co-activated during speci /uniFB01 c tasks or processes, using data from Neurosynth (https://neurosynth.org/) [44, 45]. We limited our analysis to 217 terms with clear biological signi /uniFB01 cance [46, 47]. and the signi /uniFB01 cance was evaluated using permutation testing (10000 iterations) [44].

We also constructed linear regression models to explore the relationship between extreme functional connections and clinical characteristics including symptom severity, illness duration and age of onset were constructed. Signi /uniFB01 cance was set at p &lt; 0.05 with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

## Detecting MDD subtypes using extreme functional connections

We next explored whether extreme functional connections could unveil potential MDD subtypes. The signi /uniFB01 cance of clustering was assessed using the SigClust approach, which tests whether the data /uniFB01 t a single Gaussian distribution (i.e., no subtypes) [48]. After con /uniFB01 rming the presence of subtypes, we used the K-means algorithm classify MDD patients based on extreme functional connections, with Euclidean distance as the distance metric. To avoid local minima, K-means was run 100 times with different centroid initializations [49]. The optimal number of subtypes, ranging from 2 -10, was determined using a cluster ensemble voting technique, which considers 26 indices to assess clustering quality [50].

Cluster stability was evaluated using 100 randomly selected subsample (90% of patients) and leave-one-site out validation. Co-occurrence rates between patient pairs across iterations were summarized into a single stability index, as detailed in the supplementary methods.

## Characterizing subtypes

We then characterized the identi /uniFB01 ed MDD subtypes using demographic and clinical variables such as sex, age, illness duration, symptom severity, and the proportion of /uniFB01 rst-episode versus recurrent and untreated versus treated patients. These comparisons were conducted using t-tests or chisquare tests, as appropriate. Functional connectivity abnormalities for each subtype, relative to HCs, were also assessed using two-sample t-tests, controlling for sex, age, study site, and head motion (mean frame-wise displacement, mean FD) [51, 52]. Signi /uniFB01 cance was set at p &lt; 0.05 with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

## Examining subtype utility in case-control discriminability

Then, we examined whether the identi /uniFB01 ed subtypes were useful and led to an improvement of classi /uniFB01 cation accuracy. We trained linear support vector machines (SVMs) on extreme functional connections to distinguish between patients and HCs. Classi /uniFB01 cation performance was evaluated using a 100 × 10-fold cross-validation framework, yielding a 100 × 1 accuracy vector. Accuracy signi /uniFB01 cance was determined using 100 times random label permutations for each subtype separately. Performance differences between models (models of subtypes vs. models of all patients) were compared using two sample t-tests with signi /uniFB01 cance was set at p &lt; 0.05 after Bonferroni correction.

## Reproducibility and disease speci /uniFB01 city analysis

First, we tested the effect of varying the consistency rate threshold (set at 95% in the main analysis) by lowering it to 90%. The Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) was calculated to compare these outcomes with the original /uniFB01 ndings. Second, we validated clustering results in the subset of untreated patients to exclude the in /uniFB02 uence of medication. Finally, to examined whether extreme functional connectivity pro /uniFB01 le we reported is speci /uniFB01 c to MDD, we applied the same analysis pipeline to a dataset of schizophrenia patients from Centre for Biomedical Research Excellence (COBRE, http://fcon\_1000.-projects.nitrc.org/indi/retro/cobre.html) [53]. The COBRE dataset includes 72 schizophrenia patients and matched 74 HCs. Further details on demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as data acquisition, are available elsewhere [53].

## RESULTS

## Consistently selected extreme functional connections

Patients with MDD exhibited considerable variability in the number of extreme functional connections, ranging from 1604 to 18,825 (Figure S1). We identi /uniFB01 ed extreme functional connections that were consistently selected across 100 subsamples, each containing 90% of the MDD patients. The consistency rate of each connection, de /uniFB01 ned as the proportion of subsamples in which the connection was identi /uniFB01 ed as extreme, was recorded. Out of 19,900 functional connections, 556 showed a consistency rate greater than 95% (Fig. 1A). These connections were predominantly between the visual network and the frontoparietal network, the default mode network (DMN) and the ventral attention network. The network-level distribution of these features is shown in Fig. 1B. Notably, the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), bilateral orbitofrontal cortex, and supramarginal gyrus exhibited the highest nodal degrees. The node-level distribution of these features is also illustrated in Fig. 1B.

For comparison, we used standard t-tests ( p &lt; 0.05) to identify abnormal functional connections in MDD patients. We identi /uniFB01 ed 1276 abnormal connections with a consistency rate greater than 95%. To control for the number of features, we considered only the top 556 abnormal connections with the highest absolute t-statistic values for further analysis. These connections were primarily within the visual network and the DMN, and between the visual and sensorimotor networks, with the highest degrees found in the middle temporal gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, and lingual gyrus (Fig. 1C).

## Association between extreme functional connections and cognitive terms and clinical characteristics

To interpret the extreme functional connections in MDD patients, we conducted a functional enrichment analysis associating these connections with cognitive processes and functional terms. We found signi /uniFB01 cant enrichment in reward, motivation, and pain (all permutation p &lt; 0.05), as summarized in Table S1.

Regarding clinical characteristics, extreme functional connections were signi /uniFB01 cantly associated with the age of onset (adjusted R 2 = 0.39, F statistic = 2.13, Bonferroni corrected p &lt; 0.01).

## Extreme functional connections unveil two robust MDD subtypes with contrasting functional connectivity abnormalities relative to healthy controls

Signi /uniFB01 cant cluster test results showed that there exhibited signi /uniFB01 cant MDD subtypes with extreme functional connections as features (Figure S2). The cluster ensemble voting technique identi /uniFB01 ed the 2-cluster solution as optimal for the entire MDD sample (Fig. 2A). Cluster stability was assessed through 100 subsampling iterations, yielding a stability index of 0.39, signi /uniFB01 cantly higher than chance (permutation p &lt; 0.01). Leave-one-site-out validation yielded a stability index of 0.44 (permutation p &lt; 0.01).

The two subtypes exhibited contrasting patterns of functional connectivity abnormalities relative to HCs (Bonferroni corrected p &lt; 0.05). For clarity, we obtained network-level abnormalities by averaging connectivity abnormalities (t statistics) of within- and between-network connections and calculated node-level abnormalities by adding connectivity abnormalities of attached edges (namely weighted nodal degree). Speci /uniFB01 cally, patients with subtype 1 only exhibited overall increased functional connectivity in 19,579 of the total 19,900 functional connections. Network-level abnormalities were most pronounced within the visual network and between the visual network and the DMN, frontoparietal and ventral attention network. Node-level abnormalities were most pronounced in the middle cingulate gyrus, bilateral striatum and middle temporal gyrus (Fig. 2B). On the contrary, patients with subtype 2 only exhibited overall decreased functional connectivity in 15,405 of the total 19,900 functional connections. Network-level abnormalities were most pronounced within the visual network and between the visual network and the DMN, frontoparietal and ventral attention network. Node-level abnormalities were most pronounced in the anterior and middle cingulate gyrus, bilateral striatum, superior and middle temporal gyrus (Fig. 2C). ## DISCUSSION

We identi /uniFB01 ed two robust subtypes of MDD, each characterized by distinct patterns of functional connectivity abnormalities. Despite these differences in brain connectivity, the two subtypes share similar demographic and clinical characteristics. Speci /uniFB01 cally, patients in subtype 1 exhibited increased functional connectivity between the visual network and the frontoparietal, DMN, and ventral attention network, with prominent nodes in the middle cingulate gyrus, bilateral striatum, and middle temporal gyrus. In contrast, patients in subtype 2 demonstrated decreased connectivity between the visual network and the same regions. These subtypes signi /uniFB01 cantly enhance casecontrol differentiation and show high discriminability between subtypes. Additionally, sensitivity and speci /uniFB01 city analyses con /uniFB01 rmed the reproducibility and robustness of these subtypes, suggesting that the extreme connectivity patterns used for subtype identi /uniFB01 cation are speci /uniFB01 c to MDD, as compared to schizophrenia.

A growing body of research aims to identify subject-level neuroimaging abnormalities, acknowledging the notable heterogeneity in neuroimaging features among patients with MDD. Previous studies have shown that group-level neuroimaging /uniFB01 ndings, which essentially re /uniFB02 ect an ' average patient ' , capture only a subset of patients and often overlook important inter- individual heterogeneity [19, 20]. Several methods have been proposed to investigate individualized abnormalities in structural and functional neuroimaging metrics, such as gray matter morphology and structural covariance [26, 47, 54, 55]. However, studies focusing on abnormal functional connectivity at the subject level, especially in MDD, remains scarce [56 -58]. In this study, we employed a novel method, namely the tolerance interval which does not require prede /uniFB01 ned regions of interest, complex models or preset parameters. This method allowed us to identify subject-level functional connectivity abnormalities in MDD patients. We found that extreme functional connections were concentrated between the visual network and the frontoparietal, DMN and ventral attention network. Key nodes included the ACC, orbitofrontal cortex and supramarginal gyrus. These connections were closely related to cognitive processes including reward, motivation and pain and are associated with age of onset in MDD. The age of onset is consistently recognized as a known source of clinical heterogeneity and a factor that has contributed to con /uniFB02 icting /uniFB01 ndings in previous neuroimaging studies [10, 47, 59, 60]. For comparison, we also used a traditional group-level approach to identify abnormal functional connections, which yielded a distinct spatial distribution from the extreme connections identi /uniFB01 ed using the tolerance interval method. What ' s more, Subtyping based on the tolerance interval method revealed more stable MDD subtypes than those based on group-level features.

Using extreme functional connections, we unveiled two robust MDD subtypes with opposing patterns of functional connectivity abnormalities. Subtype 1 demonstrated overall signi /uniFB01 cantly increased functional connectivity, while patients with subtype 2 exhibited overall decreased functional connectivity. These /uniFB01 ndings are consistent with reports of high intersubject variability in MDD ' s functional connectome and may explain discrepancies in previous studies regarding DMN alterations [10, 61 -64]. Furthermore, the identi /uniFB01 ed subtypes challenge prevailing theories of hypoconnectivity in MDD and provide clarity on reports of hyperconnectivity [65 -68]. When all patients were considered together, no signi /uniFB01 cant differences were observed, underscoring the importance of subtyping in uncovering hidden functional connectivity patterns that traditional case-control comparisons might miss. Unlike symptom-based subtypes, which often exhibit overlapping abnormalities, the subtypes we identi /uniFB01 ed demonstrate clear neuroimaging differences despite their similar clinical pro /uniFB01 les [69, 70]. This /uniFB01 nding aligns with previous neuroimaging studies that have also reported distinct structural and functional abnormalities in neuroimaging-based subtypes without corresponding differences in standard clinical measures [26, 71 -73]. Our results suggest that these subtypes may re /uniFB02 ect fundamental brain differences in MDD that are not captured by conventional clinical criteria and have likely yet-to-be-discovered clinical implications. Functional enrichment analysis suggests that reward-related processes might differ between these subtypes, a hypothesis that warrants further exploration. Additionally, the identi /uniFB01 ed subtypes signi /uniFB01 cantly enhance case-control discrimination and provide new insights into intersubject variability in neuroimaging phenotypes.

To our knowledge, this is the /uniFB01 rst study to identify MDD subtypes using subject-level functional connectivity abnormalities based on a large, multi-site neuroimaging dataset. Reproducibility and sensitivity analyses con /uniFB01 rmed that these subtypes are robust across different validation methods, including subsampling, leave-one-site-out validation, varying consistency rates, and medication status. Furthermore, the disease speci /uniFB01 city analysis indicated that the spatial pattern of extreme functional connections is speci /uniFB01 c to MDD, distinguishing it from schizophrenia.
