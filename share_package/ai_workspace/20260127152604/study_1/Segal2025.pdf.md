## ABSTRACT

BACKGROUND: Interindividual variability in the neurobiological and clinical characteristics of mental illnesses are often overlooked by classical group-mean case-control studies. Studies using normative modeling to infer personspeci /uniFB01 c deviations of gray matter volume have indicated that group means are not representative of most individuals. The extent to which this variability is present in white matter morphometry, which is integral to brain function, remains unclear.

METHODS: We applied warped Bayesian linear regression normative models to T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging data and mapped interindividual variability in person-speci /uniFB01 c white matter volume (WMV) deviations in 1294 cases (58% male) diagnosed with one of 6 disorders (attention-de /uniFB01 cit/hyperactivity disorder, autism, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and schizophrenia) and 1465 matched control participants (54% male) recruited across 25 scan sites. We developed a framework to characterize deviation heterogeneity on multiple spatial scales from individual voxels through interregional connections, speci /uniFB01 c brain regions, and spatially extended brain networks.

RESULTS: The speci /uniFB01 c locations of WMV deviations were highly heterogeneous across participants, affecting the same voxel in fewer than 8% of individuals with the same diagnosis. For autism and schizophrenia, negative deviations (i.e., areas where volume is lower than normative expectations) aggregated into common tracts, regions, and large-scale networks in up to 69% of individuals.

CONCLUSIONS: The prevalence of WMV deviations was lower than previously observed in gray matter, and the speci /uniFB01 c location of these deviations was highly heterogeneous when considering voxelwise spatial resolution. Evidence of aggregation within common pathways and networks was apparent in schizophrenia and autism but not in other disorders.

The brain ' s white matter comprises densely packed myelinated axons that interconnect distinct regions of gray matter and play a critical role in healthy brain function (1,2 to white matter can disrupt coordinated neuronal activity, leading to clinical symptoms of mental illness. Accordingly, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have reported diverse white matter abnormalities in different psychiatric diagnoses (3 -10).

These abnormalities have commonly been revealed using traditional statistical approaches that rely on comparisons of group means, which can overlook the tremendous clinical and biological interindividual variability that characterizes psychiatric illness (11 -14). Normative modeling (15,16) offers a framework for characterizing this variability by allowing one to quantify the extent to which individuals deviate on a given phenotype from normative expectations given relevant demographic characteristics such as age and sex (15 -17).

Normative modeling has been applied to a range of MRIderived phenotypes in different neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders (18 -26); however, few studies have used this approach to investigate the heterogeneity of white matter deviations in psychiatric disorders. In these studies, typically no more than 20% of individuals with the same diagnosis show a signi /uniFB01 cant deviation in the same white matter location or tract (20,23,24,26). This neural heterogeneity raises an important question: how can individuals be assigned the same diagnosis if their brain deviations are so heterogeneous?

Such extreme neural heterogeneity represents a candidate biological correlate of the noted clinical variability in psychiatric populations (11), which may stem from a more fundamental

## Voxel-Scale WMV Heterogeneity

We estimated voxelwise WMV using voxel-based morphometry (VBM) of T1-weighted anatomical MRI scans (29,30) as per Segal et al. (22) (see Supplementary Methods). Details of the VBM procedure, image acquisition, and quality assurance procedures for the T1-weighted images are detailed in Segal et al. (22).

We used normative modeling to obtain person-speci /uniFB01 c WMV deviation maps relative to a model, based on Bayesian linear regression with likelihood warping (31), of population expectations for voxelwise WMV variations (see https://github. com/amarquand/PCNtoolkit, version = 0.21) (15,17). Following strati /uniFB01 cation of the sample into training (HCtrain, n = 1196) and test (clinical data, n = 1294; HCtest, n = 269) subsets (Figure 1A) [see Supplementary Methods and (22)], we /uniFB01 tted separate models to each white matter voxel to predict WMV as a function of age, sex, and site (Figure 1B) (see Supplementary Methods). Then we computed deviation zmaps for each individual (Figure 1C), which quantify the extent to which individual voxelwise WMV estimates deviate from the model prediction [see (15,16) for details; see Supplementary Methods]. Extreme deviations were de /uniFB01 ned as clusters of 10 contiguous voxels with deviation scores of | z | . 2.6 (i.e., p , .005) (22,23) (Figure 1D). See Supplementary Methods for details.

To characterize the interindividual heterogeneity of deviation locations, we calculated the proportion of individuals showing an extreme deviation within each white matter voxel, separately for positive and negative deviations (Figure 1E, F) (22). Then we used permutation testing to test for statistically signi /uniFB01 cant differences in the proportions of cases and controls (HCtest) showing an extreme deviation in each voxel (see Supplementary Methods for details). We repeated our analysis using various extreme deviation thresholds ( z , 2 1.96, 2 1.65) and cluster thresholds (cluster size = 50) to ensure that our /uniFB01 ndings were not driven by this speci /uniFB01 c choice.

## Tract-Scale WMV Heterogeneity

The network context of WMV deviations was evaluated with respect to a normative connectome estimated in an independent, healthy, unrelated cohort of 150 diffusion-weighted MRI scans (71 male, aged 21 -35 years) from the S900 release of the Human Connectome Project (HCP150) (32). The connectome mapped 8646 tracts linking each pair of 100 cortical and 32 subcortical regions, as de /uniFB01 ned using established atlases (33,34) (see Supplementary Methods for details).

## Region-Scale WMV Heterogeneity

To determine whether any affected tracts were preferentially linked to speci /uniFB01 c brain regions, we generated a map of gray matter regions attached to at least one affected tract in the dysconnectome matrix of each individual in the clinical and HCtest groups (Figure 3A). The resulting person-speci /uniFB01 c binary regional maps (Figure 3B), obtained separately for positive and negative deviations, were then aggregated across individuals to obtain group-speci /uniFB01 c overlap maps quantifying, at each region, the proportion of people within a given group for whom at least one affected connection was attached to that region (Figure 3E). Next, we evaluated case-control differences in overlap proportions for each region using the same procedures as those used in the voxelwise and tract-scale analyses (see the Supplement for details).

## Network-Scale WMV Heterogeneity

Next, we examined the degree to which WMV deviations aggregated within distinct functional networks. Using extreme region deviation maps (Figure 3B), we assigned each cortical region to one of 7 canonical functional cortical networks using a well-validated network parcellation (35) and assigned each subcortical region to either the medial temporal lobe (amygdala and hippocampus), thalamus, or basal ganglia (nucleus accumbens, globus pallidus, putamen, caudate nucleus), as done previously (34), resulting in a total of 10 distinct functional networks (Figure 3D).

For each network, we then estimated the proportion of individuals in each diagnostic group with at least one extreme deviation in any region of each network, creating group-speci /uniFB01 c dysconnection networks for positive and negative deviations separately (Figure 3E). Case-control differences in overlap proportions for each network were evaluated using the same procedures as those outlined in previous analyses (see the Supplement for details). We repeated the analysis using a 20-network parcellation (17 cortical networks and 3 subcortical groups).

Figures S2 and S5 quantify the spatial overlap across scales for the HCtest cohort for negative and positive extreme deviations, respectively. Because there were fewer signi /uniFB01 cant case-control differences in overlap for positive deviations (Figures S5 -S9), we focused on negative WMV deviations (i.e., areas where volume is lower than normative expectations). We quanti /uniFB01 ed heterogeneity in WMV deviations as the proportion of individuals showing an extreme deviation in each voxel, estimated separately for each diagnostic group and the HCtest cohort. Across all groups, we found very little spatial overlap in the location of voxel-scale negative extreme deviations, with the maximum percentage overlap never exceeding 5% (Table 2 and Figure 1F, G).

## Tract-Scale WMV Heterogeneity

We investigated whether heterogeneous extreme deviations in each clinical group converged on common axonal pathways that link speci /uniFB01 c brain regions. The maximum overlap across the 8646 connections linking 132 gray matter regions ranged from 4.19% (OCD) to 15.40% (SCZ), with 8.18% in the HCtest group (Table S4 and Figure 2G, H).

Connections that showed statistically signi /uniFB01 cant differences in overlap scores are shown for each disorder in Figure 2I. We observed signi /uniFB01 cantly greater overlap in tracts that were predominately connected to visual and amygdala regions for individuals with ASD and connections to somatomotor areas for SCZ ( p , .05 FDR-corrected, 2-tailed) compared with controls (Table S4 and Figure 2I). For ADHD, BP, MDD, and OCD, no connections survived FDR correction.

Controls showed some evidence of greater overlap than individuals with OCD, but very few connections survived FDR correction (Table S4 and Figure 2I). Alternative analysis thresholds yielded comparable /uniFB01 ndings (see Methods and Materials, Table S5, and Figure S3). These results suggest that WMV deviations affect common axonal pathways in some disorders, although the overlap was only marginally higher than in the voxelwise analysis, suggesting considerable heterogeneity on the tract scale.

## DISCUSSION

We characterized the structural network context of anatomically heterogeneous WMV abnormalities in a diverse range of psychiatric disorders. We found that heterogeneity of voxelwise WMV abnormalities is a feature of most psychiatric disorders. For ASD and SCZ, these heterogeneous loci affected connections that are linked to almost all brain regions and are implicated in nearly all brain networks. Thus, our /uniFB01 ndings suggest that WMV deviations are highly heterogeneous on the voxel and tract scales in all disorders and only aggregate within common, yet widespread, brain regions and networks in ASD and SCZ.

On a voxel scale, few areas showed signi /uniFB01 cantly greater overlap in cases than in controls. No voxel in any disorder showed an extreme deviation in more than 5% of cases for negative deviations and in 8% of cases for positive deviations. The high degree of voxelwise heterogeneity in WMV deviations across disorders is consistent with previous normative modeling studies of WMV in SCZ, BP, and ADHD (23,24) that used a subsample of the data in the current study, as well as similar normative modeling studies of other neural phenotypes in a range of psychiatric diagnoses (19 -21,25,26). The high degree of voxelwise heterogeneity supports the contention that group-level maps of case-control differences are likely to mask considerable within-group heterogeneity.

Despite voxel-scale heterogeneity, we found that deviations aggregated within common tracts, regions, and functional networks for some disorders. This aggregation is expected because coarser scales combine data over larger regions. The HCtest sample provided a normative benchmark for expected overlap at each network scale. At the tract scale, ASD showed greater overlap in edges that link visual areas and the amygdala. This is consistent with the social impairments (37) and atypical visual perception (38) that are seen in ASD, with research implicating amygdala dysfunction (39). Amygdala lesions have also been associated with impairments in social judgment (40), which have been likened to acquired autism

SCZ showed greater overlap in edges that link sensory-motor areas and connect the sensory-motor cortex with the thalamus, which is consistent with previous /uniFB01 ndings of altered somatomotor-thalamic connectivity in SCZ (42 -45). However, for both disorders, the maximum overlap for any connection did not exceed 15%, indicating signi /uniFB01 cant individual variability.

At the region-scale, robust differences in negative deviation overlap were found for ASD and SCZ across nearly the entire brain, except for some parietal and temporal areas in the left hemisphere for ASD and medial frontal areas and the basal ganglia for SCZ. For positive deviations in ASD, differences were observed across most of the brain, except for unimodal regions in the right hemisphere. At the network scale, almost all functional networks were implicated in individuals with ASD or SCZ who had extreme negative deviations (32% and 35% of participants, respectively) and in most individuals with ASD who had extreme positive deviations (69% of participants). These /uniFB01 ndings support evidence of widespread connectivity changes in both conditions (46 -48) and are consistent with reports of high comorbidity and overlaps in genetics, neuroimaging, clinical signs, and cognitive features between ASD and SCZ (49).

In OCD, all functional networks were less likely to be implicated than in controls, which may simply re /uniFB02 ect a lower total WMV deviation burden in the disorder. While past work suggests that disruption of frontostriatal systems is linked to OCD (50), our work is consistent with a recent study that reported that altered structural connectivity is unlikely to drive the observed functional dysregulations (51).

We found no signi /uniFB01 cant differences in WMV overlap between controls and people with ADHD, BP, or MDD, suggesting that WMV aberrations may be less pronounced in these conditions than in people with SCZ or ASD. When taken together with our previous work examining GMV (22), the current /uniFB01 ndings suggest that WMV deviations, as assessed by VBM, may be relatively rare in psychiatric disorders. Fewer than 36% of individuals in any group showed at least 1 extreme negative WMV deviation compared to 76% for GMV deviations in our previous study (22). Positive WMV deviations were more common, with over 61% of people showing at least 1 extreme deviation, consistent with previous research (23,24). It remains unclear whether this increase in WMV is a true biological phenomenon or a limitation of current normative models in capturing nonlinearities in the voxelwise WMV estimates. Using more /uniFB02 exible normative models (31,52) may help address this issue.

## Limitations and Conclusions

The quanti /uniFB01 cation of WMV deviations was based on our previous work on GMV deviations using the Jacobian of the nonlinear warp /uniFB01 eld. However, unlike gray matter, white matter ' s /uniFB01 brous bundles have a strong orientation dependence. Changes perpendicular to /uniFB01 ber orientations indicate modi /uniFB01 -cations in axon number or caliber, while changes along /uniFB01 ber orientations mainly affect /uniFB01 ber length, which has a weaker impact on information transfer. Combining registration that is tailored to white matter with tractography may provide greater speci /uniFB01 city (53,54).

Diffusion MRI is the most widely used method for in vivo whole-brain structural connectivity in humans, but it suffers from several well-documented limitations. For example, studies that have compared connections using tract-tracing and diffusion MRI in nonhuman primates have demonstrated that diffusion MRI struggles to accurately reconstruct tracts in voxels with complex /uniFB01 ber architecture, such as crossing, branching, and turning /uniFB01 bers, as well as in voxels with partial volume effects (55 -57). This complex /uniFB01 ber architecture contributes to an in /uniFB02 ated number of both false positives and false negatives in diffusion MRI approaches. Different tractography algorithms show varying sensitivities to such false-positive and false-negative errors (58,59).

Diagnoses that are based on nosological systems such as the DSM (37) are clinical heuristics that do not necessarily de /uniFB01 ne biological entities, with several well-documented limitations including pervasive comorbidity (60) and within-group clinical heterogeneity (11). Consistent with our previous work (22), we used DSM diagnoses to evaluate heterogeneity and explore potential neural correlates of phenotypic similarities. However, as expected, this reliance may limit the degree of observed neural overlap. Longstanding therapeutic evidence indicates that treatment approaches are often agnostic to diagnostic boundaries and are rarely effective for all individuals within a diagnostic category (61 -64). Focusing on syndromes or symptoms that cut across diagnostic boundaries (65) may yield more precise mappings of voxelwise deviations and their network context and may offer important clues into the circuits to target for therapeutics (66). Similarly, determining the best way of leveraging multiscale normative modeling results to cluster individuals into subgroups is an important future extension of our work. Achieving these goals requires harmonized, transdiagnostic clinical protocols that measures diverse symptom dimensions (67 -69).

Group differences in total deviation burden can drive differences in group overlap, thereby complicating inferences about speci /uniFB01 c targeting of connections, regions, or networks. While GMV studies have methods to address this (22), WMV analyses lack such null models. Therefore, we cannot determine whether observed overlaps re /uniFB02 ect differences in deviation burden or selective targeting, although these differences still have phenotypic consequences [see (22) for a detailed discussion].

In summary, our analysis across 6 psychiatric disorders found very little overlap among individuals with the same disorder in the location of MRI-based WMV deviations. For ASD and SCZ, these deviations affect common axonal pathways that link pairs of brain regions and distributed brain networks. However, the prevalence of WMV deviations is lower than previous observations for GMV using the same sample (22), suggesting that WMV may be relatively spared in psychiatric illness.
