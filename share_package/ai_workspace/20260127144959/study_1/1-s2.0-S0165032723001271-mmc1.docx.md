**Method**

**Data acquisition and preprocess**

Image acquisition parameters are presented in Table S1. And the preprocessing procedure was conducted as below. First, the initial 10 volumes were discarded, and then the slice-timing correction was performed. Second, the images were realigned using a six-parameter (rigid body) linear transformation. After realignment, individual T1-weighted images were co-registered to the mean functional image using a 6 degrees-of-freedom linear transformation without re-sampling and then segmented. Finally, transformations from individual native space to MNI space were computed. A linear trend was included as a regressor and temporal bandpass filtering (0.01-0.1Hz) was performed on the all-time series. The preprocessing process was more detailed and described in a previous study (Yan et al., *PNAS* , 2019).

**Case-control statistical analysis**

The inherent connectivity states were compared between FEDN patients and healthy- comparison individuals with pseudo two sample t-test ( *p* =0.05, permutation test *n* = 5000, age, sex, years of education, head motions, and sites as covariates) using Statistical Nonparametric Mapping (SnPM) software (http://warwick.ac.uk/snpm). According to our normative model, we excluded the “outliers” and conducted a second case-control analysis.

**Correlation between (GIG-ICA) identified brain components with static FC patterns**

Firstly, we identified the essential nodes for each group-level independent component and extracted the functional connectivity matrix. Secondly, we calculated the overall static FC matrix based on the same nodes. Then we conducted a correlation analysis to investigate the relationships between the ICA components with the static FC pattern. We found that for most of the components, there is a significant correlation between the two matrices (the correlation between the component matrix and the static FC matrix was significant for 19/20 components).

**Linear model validation**

To further validate the method's robustness and the study's main results, we replicated the experiment using the quantile linear regression model. The results were listed in Figure S8-13).

**Results**

***Inherent connectivity states***

ICA guided by group information (GIG-ICA) was adopted to the window-direction concatenated dynamic connectivity patterns to extract the subject-specific independent components (ICs) as inherent connectivity states. Figure S2 lists the identified 20 components.

***Spatial characteristics of inherent connectivity states***

The ICA identified 20 inherent brain networks (see supplementary materials Figure S2), and the conventional case-control differences were analyzed using linear mixed effect modeling including site, age, sex, and head motion as covariates. The identified functional connectivity networks were corresponding to prior established functional networks. We did group comparison in male and female separately. Within the salience network, the connectivity between ACC and SMG, medial frontal gyrus and IFG were significantly increased in male patients with FEDN-MDD. Within DMN, the functional connectivity between postcentral gyrus with PCC, caudate, angular gyrus was increased in male patients. Within the frontoparietal network, increased connectivity between inferior parietal with insula, and superior temporal gyrus were also detected in male patients. The female patients showed fewer abnormal functional connectivity in these three brain networks (for details, see supplementary Figure S3). There are 294 edges in female participants (increased: 210 edges, decreased: 84 edges) and 172 edges in male participants (increased: 117 edges, decreased: 55 edges) that showed significant group differences across the identified 20 networks.

According to our normative model, the “outliers” were identified as the top 5% of the number of deviations for all connectivity in different groups, to guard the results against the influence of the extreme individuals (13 of the FEDN-MDD patients, and 21 of the control participants), we excluded them and conducted a second case-control analysis. Notably, we detected relatively fewer group differences in several inherent connectivity states after excluding the “outliers”, which suggested that these disappeared group differences were substantially affected by the “outliers”. The second case-control analysis revealed only 274 connections passing FDR correction instead of 294 connections in the first case-control analysis in female participants, and 168 connections instead of 172 connections in male participants (see Figure S3). The results remind us that the identified difference in previous studies may be caused by the small subset patients, and can not represent the majority.

**Normative modeling to map the static characteristics heterogeneity in FEDN-MDD**

We placed each individual FEDN-MDD participant in relation to the typical distribution to identify alterations in individual cases with respect to the static functional connectivity within each connectivity state. Figure S4 shows a description of the number of normative deviations in static functional connectivities for the FEDN-MDD and HC cohorts. Specifically, this figure showed the percentage of individuals in each group has a certain number of the deviated functional connectivities after intraindividual FDR correction. There are 14% of FEDN-MDD participants showed about 45-55 deviations in all of the functional connectivities within brain networks. There are almost 80% of the patients, and 78% controls showed extreme abnormalities in at least 55 connectivities. Saliently, in the top 5% deviating individuals, 5.11% patients were identified, and 5.01% healthy control individuals were identified. These results revealed that the distribution of patients and healthy control cohort on static functional connectivity is comparable. However, if we look at the participants with more extreme connectives (above 100 edges), 2.55% of the patients were identified, and only 1.2% controls were identified. Then we modeled males and females separately due to the presence of sex effects in FEDN-MDD. The distribution of the male and female participants with normative deviations was also comparable across the two groups.

We also investigated sex differences within the patient and HC groups. For the network fluctuation characteristic deviations only, there were significant differences between females and males in network 7 (HC group, more females showed deviations, p=0.030), network 19 (HC group, more males showed deviations p=0.034), and network 10 (FEDN group, more male showed deviations, p=0.006). For network static characteristic deviation, there was no significant difference between females and males in the HC group, while network 2 (more females showed deviations, p=0.001), network 5 (more males showed deviations, p=0.006), network 11 (more females showed deviations, p=0.010), and network 13 (more male showed deviations, p=0.015) were different between female and male in FEDN patients.

In Figure S4A and S4B, all extreme deviations were combined together that represented the percentage of extreme positive and negative deviating functional connectivities, expressed relative to the total number of functional connectivities. Next, we profiled the distribution of participants separately for the supra- (increased) and infra- (decreased) functional connectivities separately (see Figure S4C and S4D). In the percentage of extreme supra-normal deviations across groups, healthy individuals differed from patients with FEDN-MDD ( *p* = 0.0043); a higher proportion of extreme super-normal deviations was seen in healthy individuals (73.28% of functional connectivities) than in those with FEDN-MDD (68.87% of functional connectives). For the female and male participants, the distribution of the percentage of patient individuals shifted to left in respect to the super-normal deviations (73.87% for female patients with supra-deviations, and 75.45% for female controls, *p* =0.020, 59.87% for male patients, and 69.95% for male controls, *p* = 0.019). As for the extreme infra-normal connectivities, the two groups showed no significant difference.

We then analyzed the normative deviations regarding static characteristic within identified 20 inherent connectivity states. In the Figure 2E, there are at least 43.3% of the patients and 38.5% of the controls showed extreme deviations in at least 1 out of 20 brain networks. We also modeled male and female separately, 48.9% of the female patients and 44.2% of the female controls, 31% of the male patients and 30.7% of the male controls showed at least deviation in at least 1 brain networks. For the brain network that showed extreme deviations, there is no significant difference was detected between FEDN-MDD and controls. Then we analyzed group differences in each brain network separately (see Figure S4). Particularly, the patients showed decreased static characteristics in component 14 and component 20. The average functional connectivity in executive control networks is 0.08 for the patients and 0.32 for the controls, which suggests a decreased communication in executive control networks in patients. Similar results were also found in fronto-striatal networks (0.05 for patients, and 0.13 for controls). As shown in Figure S4F, the main difference came from the female participants, especially in component 14.

**Brain regions with the most deviated participants for measures of functional connectivity**

Brain renderings show the percentage of individuals with deviations at region-level in inherent functional connectivity states/networks, when analyzing the static functional connectivity. The deviating degree of the brain region was the sum of edges connected to it. The top K regions that contained more than 50% of the deviations for a given network were shown in Figure S6.
