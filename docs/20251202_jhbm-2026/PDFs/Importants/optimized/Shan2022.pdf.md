## Mapping the Heterogeneous Brain Structural Phenotype of Autism Spectrum Disorder Using the Normative Model

Xiaolong Shan, Lucina Q. Uddin, Jinming Xiao, Changchun He, Zihan Ling, Lei Li, Xinyue Huang, Huafu Chen, and Xujun Duan

## ABSTRACT

BACKGROUND: Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by substantial clinical and biological heterogeneity. Quantitative and individualized metrics for delineating the heterogeneity of brain structure in ASD are still lacking. Likewise, the extent to which brain structural metrics of ASD deviate from typical development (TD) and whether deviations can be used for parsing brain structural phenotypes of ASD is unclear.

METHODS: T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging data from the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) II ( n TD = 564) were used to generate a normative model to map brain structure deviations of ABIDE I subjects ( n TD = 560, n ASD = 496). Voxel-based morphometry was used to compute gray matter volume. Non-negative matrix factorization was employed to decompose the gray matter matrix into 6 factors and weights. These weights were used for normative modeling to estimate the factor deviations. Then, clustering analysis was used to identify ASD subtypes. RESULTS: Compared with TD, ASD showed increased weights and deviations in 5 factors. Three subtypes with distinct neuroanatomical deviation patterns were identi /uniFB01 ed. ASD subtype 1 and subtype 3 showed positive deviations, whereas ASD subtype 2 showed negative deviations. Distinct clinical manifestations in social communication de /uniFB01 cits were identi /uniFB01 ed among the three subtypes.

CONCLUSIONS: Our /uniFB01 ndings suggest that individuals with ASD have heterogeneous deviation patterns in brain structure. The results highlight the need to test for subtypes in neuroimaging studies of ASD. This study also presents a framework for understanding neuroanatomical heterogeneity in this increasingly prevalent neurodevelopmental disorder.

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a pervasive neurodevelopmental condition characterized by de /uniFB01 cits in social communication skills and interaction, alongside restricted, repetitive behavior (RRB) and sensory anomalies (1). Individuals with ASD exhibit multilevel heterogeneity across genetics (2,3), brain systems (4), and clinical manifestation (5). This heterogeneity has become the largest obstacle to understanding and treatment of ASD. The identi /uniFB01 cation of homogeneous and biologically de /uniFB01 ned subgroups of ASD may help parse this heterogeneity, thus improving diagnosis and individualized treatment. In recent decades, investigators have attempted to identify ASD subgroups based on behavioral or cognitive characteristics (6 -8). However, subgroups with homogeneous clinical phenotypes may be driven by different neurobiological factors. Therefore, the identi /uniFB01 cation of neuroimaging-based neurophysiological subgroups would provide insights into how different neurophysiological factors may lead to heterogeneity within the broad clinical phenotype (9).

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), a noninvasive tool with high spatial resolution, is a useful modality for probing brain structural organization. Previous studies on brain morphometry have reported atypical neuroanatomy in ASD, with inconsistencies in regional speci /uniFB01 city (10 -12). Neurobiological heterogeneity is a factor that might reconcile con /uniFB02 icting results in the literature. Identi /uniFB01 cation of neurobiologically homogeneous ASD subtypes is imperative to understanding the etiology and treatment of this disorder. At the present time, the literature on subtypes of ASD based on structural MRI is in its infancy. Despite studies varying in methodology, previous results have revealed evidence for two to four ASD subtypes (13). Hrdlicka et al. (14) focused on cortical thickness of the frontal lobe and size of the corpus callosum, hippocampus, caudate, and amygdala for clustering analysis and revealed four ASD subtypes with distinct region of interest -based anatomical pro /uniFB01 les. Hong et al. (15) used a novel analytical framework integrating cortex-wide features (i.e., thickness, tissue contrast, and surface area) and identi /uniFB01 ed three anatomically diverse subgroups by hierarchical clustering. However, in these studies, the features used for clustering analysis were based on prior knowledge or high-dimensional

data, rather than individual quantitative measurements. Recent work from our group identi /uniFB01 ed three subtypes of male ASD by clustering analysis based on coef /uniFB01 cients characterizing the level of structural difference components. This work found different clinical symptom severity and brain functional connectivity across subtypes (16). Our previous work matched a group of most similar healthy control subjects for each ASD participant to compute a structural difference map; as such, the generalizability and clinical utility of the approach is limited. The identi /uniFB01 cation of structural MRI features that have the potential to parse heterogeneity at the individual level is an important direction for ASD research. The purpose of the current study is to develop a parameterized, low-dimensional, and individualized metric to delineate heterogeneity of brain structure in ASD.

Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) has recently emerged as a promising pathway to capture robust and reliable non-negative regional differences in high-dimensional data (17) and has been widely used in MRI analysis (16,18). Using NMF, a matrix can be decomposed into a factor matrix and a weight matrix. The gray matter (GM) map of a single subject can thus be represented as a linear combination of basis factors. The use of NMF can achieve dimension reduction and increase interpretability. Normative model, a statistical technique, is increasingly applied to map variations between cognitive, clinical, behavioral, or demographic variables and quantitative brain readouts (19 -21). In contrast to case-control analysis requiring a consistent difference at the mean level, the normative model allows for detection and mapping of abnormal structural patterns individually (21,22). The normative model is similar to growth charts used in pediatric medicine, in which a child ' s height or weight is compared with a reference population (23). The integration of NMF and normative modeling, decomposition of GM into factors and weights, and modeling the weights of factors can be used to individually measure the extent of ASD difference from typical development (TD). Additionally, this strategy provides a proof of concept for delineating the heterogeneity of ASD by identifying homogeneous subgroups based on individual structural MRI features.

Here, we combined NMF and normative modeling to individually parameterize neurobiological heterogeneity in participants with ASD. We hypothesized that individuals with ASD would exhibit highly heterogeneous deviation patterns from the TD cohort. We predicted that individuals with ASD could be clustered into several subgroups with distinct clinical phenotypes. To test this hypothesis, we decomposed the GM matrix of Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) II TD subjects into latent factors and corresponding weights. To characterize the functional implications of latent factors, we analyzed the relationships between factors and behavioral relevance using the NeuroSynth database (24). Next, we used an NMF backreconstruction algorithm to reconstruct ABIDE I ASD and ABIDE I TD GM matrices to obtain the weights of subjects. Then, we developed a normative model by training a Gaussian process regression (GPR) on the ABIDE II TD subjects to predict the expected weights of factors. Subsequently, we estimated the deviations of weights to characterize the differences between individuals with ASD and the normative range. Finally, we applied clustering analysis to identify ASD

subgroups. Using this strategy, we provide a method to quantitatively estimate the heterogeneity of individuals with ASD, providing a new perspective for understanding heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorders.

## METHODS AND MATERIALS

## Participants

To investigate the heterogeneity of ASD, we downloaded MRI T1-weighted and phenotypic data from the ABIDE databases (25,26). The speci /uniFB01 c imaging protocols are available at http:// fcon\_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/abide/. T1 images were visually inspected for motion artifacts by three experienced personnel, and participants with lower-quality data were excluded (Supplement) (27). Table 1 provides the demographic characteristics of participants, and identi /uniFB01 cation numbers of individual subjects is listed in the Supplement.

## Voxel-Based Morphometry and GM Matrix Decomposition

Structural MRI data of participants were analyzed with CAT12 (http://www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/), a voxel-based morphometry protocol carried out in SPM. The steps used to calculate GM volume (GMV) are described in the Supplement. After computing the GM maps, a GM mask obtained from DPABI, version 4.1 (http://rfmri.org/dpabi) was used to extract the GMV of each voxel. Each map was transformed into one column of the GM matrix (Figure 1B -D).

NMF was employed to decompose the GM matrix (Supplement). NMF was /uniFB01 rst applied on the ABIDE II TD GM matrix, which was decomposed into six latent factors and corresponding weights (Figure 1B; Supplement). Then, the NMF back-reconstruction algorithm was used to reconstruct the ABIDE I ASD and ABIDE I TD GM matrices (Figure 1C, D; Supplement). NeuroSynth was used to decode the psychological and physiological functions of the latent factors (Supplement) (24). Guided by previous work, we selected 26 terms to characterize the function of each factor (28). A twosample t test was used to examine group differences in the weights of ABIDE I ASD and ABIDE I TD.

## Normative Modeling

After obtaining the weights of factors from the ABIDE II TD GM matrix, we developed a normative model by training a GPR on the ABIDE II TD cohort (29), using age and gender as covariates to predict weights (Figure 1E). Brie /uniFB02 y, GPR is a Bayesian nonparametric interpolation method that yields coherent measures of predictive con /uniFB01 dence in addition to point estimates (29). To assess the generalizability of the model, we used 10-fold cross-validation before applying the model trained on ABIDE II TD to a discovery cohort (Supplement). For each participant, the normative model can be used to predict the estimated weight of the factor and corresponding predictive uncertainty. The contours of predictive uncertainty can then be used to model centiles of variation within the cohort. Such measures allow us to parameterize the deviation of weight from the reference cohort.

For each participant, we estimated the deviations of six factors to quantify how much an individual participant deviated from the ABIDE II TD cohort. ## RESULTS

## Latent Factors and Weights

We applied NMF to 564 GM matrices of ABIDE II TD participants and obtained six latent factors (Figure 2). Factor 1 was mainly associated with the medial cortex, including the cingulate gyrus and the medial frontal gyrus. Factor 2 was mainly characterized by the cerebellum. Factor 3 was mainly related to the occipital lobe, the inferior parietal lobule, and the thalamus. Factor 4 was mainly characterized by the parietal lobe. Factor 5 was mainly characterized by the frontal lobe. Factor 6 was mainly characterized by the limbic system (Supplement).

We obtained the weights of ABIDE I ASD and ABIDE I TD participants by using the NMF back-reconstruction algorithm. Given the missing phenotypic data for some participants (handedness and FSIQ), several were excluded. The /uniFB01 nal analysis included 515 TD and 452 ASD participants (Table S1). To exclude the effects of covariates on weights, we used the GLM to regress out age, gender, handedness, FSIQ, and sites. Signi /uniFB01 cant differences in weights were observed between the ABIDE I ASD and ABIDE I TD cohorts (Figure 2). Compared with TD, participants with ASD exhibited signi /uniFB01 cantly increased weight in factors 1, 3, 4, 5, and 6 (false discovery rate [FDR] -corrected p , .05, two-sample t test) (Figure 2A).

## Meta-analytic Decoding of Factors

To investigate the potential psychological and physiological functions of each factor, we performed a meta-analytic decoding of the functions of the factors. This procedure yielded 26 terms that showed the strongest correlations with factors. Each factor showed distinct cognitive functional pro/uniFB01 les (Figure 2B). Factor 1 overlapped with the terms related to affect, such as reward, incentive, and anticipation; factor 2 showed associations with motor control terms, such as motor control and rhythm; factor 3 was strongly associated with perceptual processes, such as visual and perceptual; factor 4 was more related to action terms, such as actions, grasping, and observation; factor 5 showed associations with high-level cognitive terms, such as working memory, monitoring, and cognitive; factor 6 was related to memory and cognitive terms, such as semantic memory, face recognition, and social.

## DISCUSSION

Identi /uniFB01 cation of neurobiologically homogeneous ASD subtypes is critical to understanding the etiology of the disorder. In this study, we mapped the neurobiological heterogeneity of ASD in reference to the normative brain across the life span. Six latent factors and corresponding weights were obtained from NMF. Meta-analytic decoding results revealed potential psychological and physiological processes of the latent factors. Correlation analysis indicated that the weights of factors 1 and 4 were correlated with ADOS communication scores, and the weights of factors 2, 4, 5, and 6 were associated with ADOS social scores. We also ascertained the factors in which individuals with ASD showed abnormal weights. We clustered participants with ASD into three subtypes with distinct clinical symptoms. Our strategy provides an approach to detect neurobiologically de /uniFB01 ned and data-driven subgroups in ASD, providing insights into the understanding of heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorders and a route toward personalized diagnosis and treatment in psychiatry (32).

## Individual Deviations Estimated From the Normative Model

Similar to growth charts, the normative model can map individualized variations relative to the normative range rather than focusing on common effects. On average, we found that individuals with ASD deviated more from the normative model except for factor 2 (Figure 4), which may be caused by the increased weight and high heterogeneity of ASD. Such highly heterogeneous deviation pro /uniFB01 les are increasingly important for understanding the heterogeneity of brain structure (64). As a complementary method to conventional group analysis, normative modeling has shown potential for evaluating individuals with diseased conditions, including nonhuman primates (19,22,65,66). A previous study that used normative modeling reported that only a few brain regions had extreme deviations in more than 2% of individuals with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder (21). This /uniFB01 nding may explain why casecontrol analysis that has dominated the research on mental disorders in general has shown few reliable group differences between patients and reference groups. Thus, the analysis of unique and highly individualized brain deviation patterns allows for a novel approach for the strati /uniFB01 cation of participants based on neuroimaging-derived metrics.

## Three Neuroanatomical Subtypes of ASD

Increasing studies based on brain structure aim to parse heterogeneity by identifying homogeneous subgroups within ASD populations (67 -69). Although methodologically diverse, a consensus indicates that at least two to four distinct subtypes may exist in ASD (13). Our results are in line with this view, which revealed three distinct neuroanatomical patterns across a large sample of ASD individuals. Subtypes 1 and 3 showed all positive deviations, but the deviations of participants with subtype 3 were larger than those of participants with subtype 1. Participants with ASD subtype 2 showed all negative deviations. Previous studies attributed participant age, gender, FSIQ, development, and other factors to the source of this variance (70,71). In our study, after controlling for age, gender, FSIQ, handedness, and sites, distinct anatomical patterns were still found, indicating that interindividual variations exist across the whole age range and play an important role in the heterogeneity of ASD.

Given that brain structure is thought to strongly in /uniFB02 uence and constrain brain function and that speci /uniFB01 c neuroanatomical patterns are associated with clinical symptoms in psychiatry disorders, different neuroanatomical subtypes are characterized by various clinical symptoms (72,73). Notably, in our study, subjects with subtype 2 showed low scores in ADOS communication and ADOS social scores. Compared with TD, subtype 2 showed a decreased GMV, and the other two subtypes showed increased GMV. Thus, an increased GMV implied more severe clinical symptoms. Our /uniFB01 ndings suggest a relationship between distinct GM patterns and core symptoms of ASD, especially for communication and social interaction de /uniFB01 cits. The strati /uniFB01 cation of ASD into subgroups that share neurobiological substrates may guide prognostic and precision medicine (74,75). Future studies need to divide individuals with ASD into homogeneous groups to understand the how distinct brain structural patterns contribute to the diverse clinical phenotypes and their relevance to the other measures of adaptive functioning.

## Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, only age and gender were considered as independent variables to estimate the brain readout in the normative model. Cognitive scores and home environment may also affect the brain index. These covariates also need to be considered for more comprehensive models. Second, although we demonstrated the stability and robustness of the clustering solution in different datasets, we did not /uniFB01 nd any brain-symptom correlations either within or across subtypes. A potential cause is the complex relationship between brain imaging and symptom dimensions, which might not be delineated using straightforward linear strategies. Biologically homogeneous subtypes identi /uniFB01 ed by brain imaging might offer speci /uniFB01 c information of clinical utility, and investigation of the effective interventions suited for different subtypes is greatly needed in future studies. Finally, other modalities, including resting-state functional MRI, diffusion tensor imaging, and genetic data, need to be incorporated to parse the heterogeneity of ASD.

## Conclusions

Our study combined NMF and normative modeling to de /uniFB01 ne a strategy for delineating the heterogeneity of ASD. We identi /uniFB01 ed

three distinct neuroanatomical patterns. We observed that different subtypes showed dissociable clinical symptoms. The data-driven framework we propose can be generalized to other mental disorders with high levels of heterogeneity, such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, which could potentially lead to future biomarker development.
