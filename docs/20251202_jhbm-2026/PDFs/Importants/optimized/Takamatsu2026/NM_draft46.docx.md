**Normative modeling in neuroimaging across psychiatric and neurological disorders: a systematic review and the NORMA reporting checklist**

**Main**

Psychiatric neuroimaging has been built on the case-control paradigm1. Over three decades, studies comparing group averages between patients and healthy controls have produced extensive findings, and large-scale consortia such as ENIGMA have extended this approach through coordinated mega-analyses, identifying regional volume reductions across major psychiatric diagnoses2. The clinical yield, however, has been limited. Effect sizes are modest: hippocampal volume reduction in schizophrenia, one of the largest effects identified by ENIGMA, yields a Cohen's d of approximately \-0.46, with patient and control distributions largely overlapping2. Reproducible brain-wide associations between brain structure and behavioral phenotypes may require tens of thousands of participants3. Cross-disorder comparisons further suggest that these modest effects are not even diagnosis-specific; subcortical volume effect-size profiles for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder correlate at 0.95 to 0.984. These comparisons rest on an aggregation of individual case-control studies, each subject to sampling biases from recruitment procedures and site-specific inclusion criteria5, and mega-analytic pooling propagates rather than corrects such biases. Cross-disorder comparisons introduce additional confounds, as the constituent studies differ in the age, sex distribution, illness duration, and medication exposure of their samples2,4. What these studies do share is the analytic framework itself: a case-control design with linear covariate adjustment. The structural convergence observed across diagnoses may therefore reflect, at least in part, shared methodological constraints rather than shared neurobiology.  
Case-control designs have a more fundamental problem. They compare group means and provide no principled way of characterizing where a given individual falls relative to the population5,6. Diagnostic categories, moreover, do not define biologically coherent groups, limiting the utility of category-based comparisons. Within a single diagnosis, genome-wide association studies have identified hundreds of risk loci for schizophrenia converging on multiple distinct molecular pathways7. Across diagnoses, large-scale analyses have revealed that nearly three-quarters of identified risk loci are pleiotropic, shared across eight major psychiatric conditions8. This has motivated efforts to reconceptualize classification systems, such as RDoC and HiTOP, yet these remain group-level frameworks. As such, analytic approaches capable of modeling biological variation at the individual level are needed. Developmental psychopathology has long recognized this pattern under the terms equifinality and multifinality9, in which distinct pathophysiological processes produce similar clinical presentations and shared risk factors lead to divergent outcomes. More recently, this has been framed as a "many-to-many mapping" problem in biological psychiatry10. These concepts highlight how diagnostic categories can mask underlying biological diversity. This mismatch between biology and clinical phenotype means that within-group biological variance can overwhelm between-group differences. The group average, in such cases, is not a representative summary but a composite of heterogeneous individual profiles. Brain structure also changes nonlinearly across the lifespan11, yet case-control comparisons typically assume that linear covariate adjustment for age adequately controls for developmental and aging effects. The sampling biases outlined above5 compound this problem: when the age and sex composition of patient and control groups differs systematically, linear adjustment cannot disentangle true disease effects from demographic confounds operating along a nonlinear trajectory.

Where case-control designs compare group means, normative modeling characterizes how each individual deviates from population expectations. The approach draws on a long tradition in pediatric medicine; growth charts have been used for over a century to track height and weight against population-derived centiles12. The clinician does not ask whether a group of children is taller than another, but whether this particular child falls within the expected range for their age and sex. Normative models apply the same logic to the brain6,13 (Fig. 1). A reference model is constructed from a normative cohort, estimating the expected distribution of a neuroimaging measure as a function of covariates such as age and sex. Each individual is then positioned within this distribution and assigned a deviation score, expressed as a z-score or centile 11,14,15. These models use flexible nonlinear functions to fit the relationship between age and brain structure, accommodating developmental and aging trajectories that linear covariate adjustments in case-control designs cannot handle.

Normative models have now been applied across several major psychiatric conditions, and the results point to substantial heterogeneity in individual deviation profiles even among patients who share the same diagnosis. A transdiagnostic analysis spanning six conditions found that person-specific deviations co-occurred in the same brain region in fewer than 7% of individuals within any diagnostic group16. Independent studies in individual disorders have confirmed the same pattern. Among 322 patients with schizophrenia, no more than 17% showed infra-normal deviations for any single cortical region or white matter tract, yet 79% showed at least one somewhere in the brain17. The overlap was even sparser for functional connectivity in major depression, where extreme deviations at any given region were shared by fewer than 3% of patients18. If most patients deviate in different locations, the small group-level effects reported in case-control studies may largely reflect the influence of a minority. Consistent with this interpretation, excluding extreme deviators from case-control comparisons of cortical thickness in autism spectrum disorder (ASD) reduced the number of significant group differences by half19. Heterogeneity extends beyond the spatial distribution of deviations. When positive and negative deviations coexist within the same diagnostic group, group averages mask abnormalities in both directions. In ASD, nearly 28% of patients exhibited structural deviations opposite in sign to those of the majority20.   
Convergent evidence from approaches outside normative modeling supports this interpretation. Unsupervised clustering of subcortical volumes across more than 5,600 individuals with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depression, and ASD identified four biotypes that cut across diagnostic boundaries, with 51% of patients with schizophrenia belonging to a biotype characterized by volumes within the normal range21. Similarly, personalized brain circuit scores derived from functional MRI in over 800 patients with depression and anxiety disorders yielded six biotypes that did not map onto diagnostic categories, one of which showed no measurable circuit differences from healthy controls22. 

The normative modeling literature has grown rapidly, with initial findings suggesting clinical predictive value., However, it remains unclear whether deviation scores derived from these models reliably reflect clinically meaningful differences, and but no study has systematically evaluated the methodological choices that shape how these models are built and applied in neuroimaging. Two recent reviews have addressed conceptual and practical aspects of the approach. One outlined the methodological potential and challenges of normative modeling for quantifying individual risk, focusing on model assumptions, uncertainty estimation, and practical recommendations for model building23. The other examined the broader transition from case-control to normative approaches, with particular attention to multi-site harmonization and the integration of distributed data sources5. Neither, however, provided a systematic evaluation of how normative models are being implemented in practice. The literature now spans a wide range of implementation choices. Studies differ in statistical approach, sample composition, harmonization strategy, and validation procedure, making it difficult to compare findings across reports or to identify best practices. This methodological heterogeneity, combined with the absence of standardized reporting, underscores the need for a systematic synthesis. To clarify the state of the field and support its methodological maturation, we present the first systematic review of normative modeling in neuroimaging, covering both psychiatric and neurological applications. We evaluated how normative models are constructed, validated, and applied to clinical populations, and used this evidence to develop the NORMA checklist for methodological transparency and comparability across studies. Our goals were to characterize current practices, identify critical methodological gaps, and propose standardized reporting recommendations.
