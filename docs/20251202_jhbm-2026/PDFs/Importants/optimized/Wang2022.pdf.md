## Disentangling the Heterogeneity of Autism Spectrum Disorder Using Normative Modeling

## Fei Wang

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a pervasive and complex developmental condition that has shown signi /uniFB01 cant heterogeneity in genetic vulnerability, etiology, phenotype, and outcome (1). Its heterogeneity continues to be a major barrier in understanding the neurobiological processes underlying ASD and developing individualized treatment for ASD. Extensive efforts to parse the heterogeneity of ASD over the past decade have had limited yield despite the use of a broad array of approaches. These efforts are further confounded by recent changes in the diagnostic classi /uniFB01 cation of ASD, which was an earlier attempt to disentangle the heterogeneity of ASD. In the DSM-III and DSM-IV, ASD was classi /uniFB01 ed as autistic disorder, Asperger syndrome, and pervasive developmental disordernot otherwise speci /uniFB01 ed. With the 2013 release of DSM-5, the diagnostic criteria of ASD were broadened, and autism-related symptoms were subsequently conceptualized as being along a continuum or spectrum (2). Previous studies have mostly examined behavioral and cognitive features of ASD as the primary unit of analysis, leading to signi /uniFB01 cant efforts in identifying behavioral ASD subtypes. These subtypes are useful in delineating clinical trajectories among different subgroups (3); however, they do not provide suf /uniFB01 cient insight for advancing treatment in ASD, including personalized medicine. For such advances, it is critical to go beyond clinical or behavioral features and to incorporate biological markers. Magnetic resonance imaging, a useful and noninvasive tool to examine brain structure and function, is poised to guide the /uniFB01 eld for treatment innovation and advances in neuropsychiatric disorders such as ASD. Magnetic resonance imaging essentially serves as an intermediary capture of the processes leading from the brain ' s macroscale organization to the individual ' s behavioral manifestation of neural processes. At present, identifying ASD subtypes based on neuroimaging is in its infancy (4). Neural subtyping may lead to important biomarkers of ASD and advance our understanding of ASD in developing more re /uniFB01 ned diagnostic classi /uniFB01 cation and effective, individualized treatment.

In the current issue of , Shan et al. (5) examined low-dimensional, quantitative, and individualized metrics for delineating the heterogeneity of brain structure in ASD, using the ABIDE (Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange) /uniFB01 rst and second releases. They decomposed the whole brain gray matter into 6 factors with corresponding weights and developed a normative model by training a Gaussian process regression to predict the expected weights of factors. They then computed the deviations of the weights to characterize the differences between individuals with ASD and typically developing control subjects. Next, clustering analysis was performed to identify 3 ASD subgroups that corresponded with distinct subsets of clinical symptoms. In combining nonnegative matrix factorization and normative modeling, this study developed a novel and informative framework for understanding the neurobiology of ASD and its links to behavior. Further, these same methods could be applied to the study of other neuropsychiatric disorders that also have substantial heterogeneity. The /uniFB01 ndings of this study are intriguing, and the study should be applauded for its sophisticated analytical approach and its clinical and translational implications.

I will now discuss the key techniques used by Shan et al. (5) in further detail. Normative models are a class of emerging statistical techniques useful for understanding the heterogeneous biology underlying a certain psychiatric disorder at the individual level (6). The normative model is similar to the standard growth and development curve used widely in pediatrics, providing statistical inferences at the individual level based on the extent to which each individual deviates from the normative range of the relationship between a demographic, clinical, or behavioral feature (e.g., age and gender) and quantitative biological measures, which are most commonly derived from neuroimaging (e.g., regional brain tissue volume). Conventional case-control analyses examine patients versus control subjects at a group level and assume an ' average patient ' ; these types of analyses tend to mask interindividual differences in brain biology. However, normative models provide individualized and parameterized metrics for each patient in relation to normative ranges, re /uniFB02 ecting and capitalizing on interindividual differences (6,7). Measuring these differences may prove critical for parsing the heterogeneity of psychiatric disorders and move the /uniFB01 eld toward personalized medicine. Previous studies using normative modeling generally derived the deviation of each patient from the normative range at each speci /uniFB01 c brain locus (e.g., each volume or each vertex) (7 -9). Normative modeling for all voxels (or vertices) may provide more speci /uniFB01 c insight and identify extreme deviations across voxels (or vertices). However, clinical signi /uniFB01 cance may be limited owing to the sheer mass of data inputted for the model. Shan et al. (5) took an important step toward normative modeling at low-dimensional in combination with non-negative matrix factorization. The incorporation of non-negative matrix factorization facilitates the clinical utility of their analytical framework with reduction of variable dimensions.

It is important to consider which covariates are included in normative modeling. Age and sex are commonly used covariates, and Shan et al. (5) used age and sex as covariates in their analyses.
