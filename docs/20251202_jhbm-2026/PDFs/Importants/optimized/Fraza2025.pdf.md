## A B S T R A C T

Keywords: Normative Modeling Neuroimaging Precision Medicine Individualized Risk Psychiatric Disorders

Neurological Disorders

Neurological and psychiatric disorders show significant variability in their onset, progression, and response to treatment. Normative modeling is a statistical method that can help us parse the heterogeneous signals associated with these disorders, bringing us closer to understanding and possible individualized treatment of these brain disorders. Similar to growth charting in pediatrics for height and weight, these models estimate the population means and centiles of variation, allowing for the calculation of deviation scores from this norm for each individual. There has been a considerable increase in the application of normative models for brain disorders, partly due to the recognition that heterogeneity in our patient groups muddles standard case-control analysis. However, most of these applications encounter challenges due to improper use and assessment, often lacking a reliable framework for longitudinal tracking 1 . In this narrative review, we first provide an overview of the objectives and applications  of  current  normative  models  within  neuroimaging,  from  parsing  heterogeneity  and  identifying clusters or subtypes, to mapping deviation scores to clinical and behavioral variables. We then outline a roadmap for future methodological advancements for individual longitudinal tracking, focusing on velocity models and thrive lines.

## 1. Introduction normative modeling: a framework for precision medicine in psychiatry and neurology

Normative modeling is a relatively new approach in neuroimaging, aligning  with  the  precision  medicine  framework  (Marquand  et  al., 2016a, 2019) and the idea of data-driven modeling of disease progression  (Young  et  al.,  2024a),  that  can  create  individualized  deviation scores from an expected norm, considering the (per)centiles of variation within the population (Marquand et al., 2016a, 2019). These models are similar to the growth charts used in pediatric medicine that estimate a child ' s height and weight, comparing these measurements to a normative  reference  curve  (Cole,  2012).  However,  unlike  pediatric  growth charts, normative models for neuroimaging data are frequently more complex,  often  involving  multiple  normative  models  across  different neuroimaging  modalities  and  regions  of  interest  (ROIs),  and  require aggregating data from multiple studies, necessitating mechanisms for accommodating site effects. The multivariate signal derived from such models provides a marker of brain development, which can be used to stratify brain disorders, a task not often performed in pediatric growth assessments (Marquand et al., 2016a, 2019).

While growth charts have long been used in the pediatric domain, their application in neuroimaging is relatively new, presenting critical gaps in the conceptual understanding and practical application of these methods, hindering progress in creating individualized neuroimaging markers. For instance, current normative models, including the widely used Centilebrain (https://centilebrain.org/#/) and PCNportal (https:// pcnportal.dccn.nl/), are based on cross-sectional data and are poorly suited for tracking longitudinal trajectories, despite often being applied as  such. The  existing  literature  often  overlooks  these fundamental limitations, potentially  due  to  a  lack  of  understanding  of  these  methods ' mathematical foundations or model requirements. In this review, we show the potential of normative modeling for personalized medicine in psychiatry and neurology, whilst further demonstrating gaps in our current methodological frameworks, through the following three points: (1) Highlighting  key  objectives  and  applications  of  normative  modeling,  by assessing the current objectives in the literature applying these models. (3) Addressing practical and ethical considerations, such as the importance of representative population samples.

Normative models allow us to go beyond a case-control analysis and identify  subtle  differences  between individuals within one diagnostic category.  This  method  addresses  some  of  the  limitations  inherent  in diagnosis  based  on  the  Diagnostic  and  Statistical  Manual  of  Mental Disorders (DSM) (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 2013) or International Classification of Diseases (ICD) (World Health Organization, 2019), which both have been shown to have large differences in single diagnostic categories (Allsopp et al., 2019; Feczko and Fair,  2020;  Nunes  et  al.,  2020a)  and  do  not  make  use  of  biological measures to differentiate conditions . We can construct brain ' growth ' charts, or brain-based normative models, using diverse neuroimaging features with respect to relevant clinical variables, such as age and sex, as our covariates. A deviation score is estimated per feature, providing an  array  of  atypicality  for  each  individual.  These  high-dimensional scores  can  subsequently  be  clustered,  creating  new  clusters  or  subtypes, or be used to learn latent profiles from the high-dimensional data into a lower-dimensional space that captures essential features of the data, through, for example, autoencoders (Zabihi et al., 2024), bringing us closer to diagnostic labels based on neurobiological fingerprints. This is in line with the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) project that aims to reclassify disorders based on empirical measures such as genomics and neurobiology  (Insel  et  al.,  2010;  Cuthbert  and  Insel,  2013).  These markers can aid us in further creating precision treatment plans that move beyond the standardized treatment approaches currently in place for  most  psychiatric  and  neurological  disorders  (Insel  and  Cuthbert, 2015).

The normative modeling framework has progressed, moving from smaller datasets with 100 -500 participants (Wolfers et al., 2018; Kia and Marquand, 2019) to large-scale models with sample sizes of 10,000 to 100,000 participants (Rutherford et al., 2022a; Ge et al., 2024; Bethlehem et al., 2022; Rutherford et al., 2022b, 2022c; Holz et al., 2023a). This shift has been enabled by advancements in computational power (Kia and Marquand, 2018), scalable methods (Kia and Marquand, 2018), large open-source neuroimaging datasets (Alfaro-Almagro et al., 2018; Casey et al., 2018), and standardization in preprocessing techniques and quality control (Rutherford et al., 2022a; Alfaro-Almagro et al., 2018). Through  these  advancements,  the  normative  framework  has  been applied  to  diverse  neuroimaging  modalities  and  features  (Bethlehem et al., 2022; Rutherford et al., 2022c; Frangou et al.,; Dima et al.,; Young et al., 2024b), including structural (Kumar et al., 2023a; Shao et al., 2023;  Dimitrova  et  al.,  2021a;  Floris  et  al.,  2021;  Liu  et  al.,  2021), functional (Zhang et al., 2021; Savage et al., 2023; Looden et al., 2022; Zabihi et al., 2022; Holz et al., 2023b), diffusion, (Cirstian et al., 2023; Lawrence et al., 2021; Kjelkenes et al., 2022a; Elad et al., 2021; Villal ´ on-Reina et al., 2022), and EEG (Ebadi et al., 2024; Floris et al., 2024) measures, enabling voxel-wise normative models (Holz et al., 2023a; Floris et al., 2021; Fraza et al., 2021a), and improving the accuracy of the  estimation  of  the  outer  centiles  (Fraza  et  al.,  2022),  previously limited by small sample sizes and low data quality. However, the integration  or  pooling  of  data  from  multiple  sites  introduces  site-related variance.  While  harmonization  efforts  by  consortia  such  as  ENIGMA (Thompson et al., 2014) and ABCD (Casey et al., 2018) have been made, residual site effects persist. Methods such as combat can remove this site-related variance as a preprocessing step (Beer et al., 2020). However, ComBat has certain limitations, including the removal of all variance  associated  with  a  site  unless  explicitly  specified  in  the  design matrix (Bayer et al., 2022). This could be problematic in exploratory analysis, where all the factors clinically related to a disorder are not known beforehand. An alternative approach is Hierarchical Bayesian Regression (HBR) (Kia et al., 2022; de Boer et al., 2024), which models site-specific effects in a fully Bayesian framework and supports federated learning, thus eliminating the need for direct access to all site data.

Different  methodologies  have  been  used  to  estimate  normative models,  including  Bayesian  Linear  Regression  (BLR)  (Cirstian  et  al., 2023; Fraza et al., 2021a; Bhome et al., 2023), Generalized Additive Model for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS) (Bethlehem et al., 2022; Dinga  et  al.,  2021a),  Gaussian  Process  Regression  (GPR)  (Marquand et  al.,  2016a;  Kumar  et  al.,  2023a;  Holz  et  al.,  2023b),  polynomial regression (Lawrence et al., 2021; Bethlehem et al., 2020; Jing et al., 2023), quantile regression (Lv et al., 2021; Di Biase et al., 2022), and newer  approaches  using  deep  learning  models  such  as  autoencoders (Zabihi et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2023b; Lawry Aguila et al., 2022; Oliveira-Saraiva and Ferreira, 2023). Key considerations when fitting and evaluating these large normative models are the model choice (e.g., linear vs. non-linear), the type of modality we intend to estimate, and the type of distribution used to model the centiles. For readers interested in exploring specific aspects of normative modeling in more detail, such as model selection, fit metrics, and evaluation criteria, Rutherford et al. (2025) provide a thorough discussion of the advantages and disadvantages  of  various  algorithms.  For  instance,  warped  BLR  (wBLR)  is well-suited for handling non-Gaussian data (Fraza et al., 2021a), while HBR is particularly well-suited for handling possible site effects (Kia et al., 2022; de Boer et al., 2024). The same reference, (Rutherford and Marquand,  2025),  also  discusses  data  selection,  preprocessing,  and model evaluation techniques.

In  psychiatry  and  neurological  research,  normative  models  have been applied to relate deviation scores to psychopathology and brain disorders, such as ADHD (Ebadi et al., 2024; Kia et al., 2020; Wolfers et  al.,  2020;  Tung  et  al.,  2021;  Bu  et  al.,  2024),  schizophrenia, (Rutherford et al., 2022b; Liu et al., 2021; Elad et al., 2021; Lv et al., 2021; Di Biase et al., 2022; Wolfers et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022; Da Costa et al., 2022; Haukvik et al., 2023; Ji et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2023; Giacomel et al., 2023), Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) (Nunes et al., 2020a;  Floris  et  al.,  2021;  Looden  et  al.,  2022;  Floris  et  al.,  2024; Bethlehem et al., 2020; Tung et al., 2021; Zabihi et al., 2019, 2020; Meijer et al., 2023), Alzheimer ' s disease (Kumar et al., 2023c; Loreto, 2024; Pinaya, 2021; Kumar et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2023b; Verdi et al., 2023a), and other neurodevelopmental disorders (Holz et al., 2023b; Kjelkenes et al., 2022a; Zhang et al., 2022; Segal et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2023). The extensive applications of normative models in psychiatry and neurology demonstrate promise for characterizing variability underlying disorders and estimating individual deviation scores. However, most current applications still conclude by aggregating the deviation scores into the predefined case-control groups, effectively performing a simple case-control analysis to establish significant differences between these groups. In other words, these applications stop short of using the individual deviation scores as possible risk markers, addressing which deviations are  clinically  important,  and  assessing  the  relevance  of individual  fluctuations  over  time.  Given  this  explosion  in  available methods, a reevaluation of both the normative modeling practice and its conceptual foundations is necessary.

A challenge concerning the application of normative models is that they  are  often  not  properly  used  and  evaluated  in  the  field,  despite extensive statistical guidelines in the growth charting literature aiding in the assessment of these models (Borghi et al., 2006; Cole, 2022) and established protocols for the application to neuroimaging (Rutherford et al., 2022c). In response to the growing concerns about methodological inconsistencies, different initiatives have proposed relatively standardized frameworks and best practices for normative modeling, (Rutherford

2023) with an emphasis on reproducibility, transparent reporting of the results, and code and data sharing. However, identifying the optimal algorithm for creating normative models has proven difficult, given the complexity of the data and diversity of modeling parameters involved (Dinga et al., 2019). For example, site effects have been shown to have a larger influence on the normative models than previously thought, and could potentially introduce large biases into the deviation scores (Bayer et al., 2022). Most likely, different distributions and phenotype characteristics  need  tailored  approaches,  and  what  might  be  the  optimal model for one feature may not be suitable for another. While evaluations of the existing methods have been performed (Ge et al., 2024), they have focused solely on mean fit measures, ignoring other important model fit criteria pertaining to the shape of the distribution (de Boer et al., 2024; Dinga et al., 2021a). These shape estimates become especially important in relation to estimating the uncertainty of sparse data in the tails of the distribution  (Bozek  et  al.,  2023).  We  anticipate  that  different  neuroimaging features will have slightly varying optimal model settings that consider variations in non-linearity and non-Gaussianity.

Another challenge within the field is the common misconception that normative models (and growth charts) are designed for the longitudinal tracking of individual subjects. Most of these models are based on crosssectional data, which provides accurate estimations of deviation at a specific age but are not optimal for tracking changes over time (Di Biase et al., 2023; Cole, 2012). Rather, normative models describe the evolution  of  the  group-level  centiles  over  time.  This  inherent  limitation means that  cross-sectional  models  are  not  well  suited  to  predict  the future trajectories of individual patients, especially during periods of expected  irregularity,  such  as  puberty.  To  effectively  predict  future adverse deviations and distinguish disorder-associated trajectories from normal fluctuations, it is necessary to extend the normative modeling framework to longitudinal data, giving us the ability to track individuals over time and assess whether they remain within their expected centiles or exhibit significant shifts. These shifts, also called centile crossings, could be indicative of underlying pathology or a brain disorder.

Against this background and drawing from the growth chart literature, which is used to monitor children ' s height and weight, we propose a conceptual framework to guide applications of normative modeling. This consists of three stages: screening, tracking, and monitoring , to get us closer to individual longitudinal tracking (Cameron, 2022), risk assessment, and possible future personalized intervention schemes. Screening is  the  initial  identification  of  individuals  at  risk  based  on  significant deviations from an expected pattern, tracking is used to monitor changes in high-risk groups and test possible interventions, and monitoring is the stage of high-frequency, individualized evaluation (Cameron, 2022). To support this framework, new methodological advancements are needed, extending our cross-sectional models to become suited for longitudinal data. In this review, we will give a few examples of possible methodological advancements, through, for example, velocity-based normative modeling and ' thrive lines ' .

In this review, we further propose a potential pipeline to address some of these challenges. First, we survey the literature on normative modeling in neuroimaging, identifying its four primary objectives: understanding population-level heterogeneity and characterizing individual differences in patients with psychiatric or neurological disorders, identifying patterns of deviation across multiple dimensions and establishing clusters or subtypes based on these deviations, creating brainbehavior mappings between the deviation scores and disease severity markers, and using the identified patterns of deviation or clusters for future  trajectory  prediction  and  treatment  response,  moving  towards precision medicine, see Fig. 1. ## 1.1. Objective 1 normative modeling: parsing heterogeneity

One  of  the  primary  objectives  of  normative  modeling  is  understanding heterogeneity at the population level in order to characterize individual differences, (Marquand et al., 2016a, 2019) acknowledging that individuals with the same diagnosis may show distinct behavioral symptoms (Fried and Nesse, 2015; Fried, 2017) and possibly differ in their underlying neurobiological fingerprint. Heterogeneity in normative modeling is expressed as the continious subject-specific deviation scores,  avoiding  the  inherent  assumption  of  internal  homogeneity within psychiatric disorders. This heterogeneity is partly driven by the variability in clinical presentation, etiology, and  the underlying

The underlying heterogeneity can be attributed to a complex range of genetic, environmental, neurodevelopmental, and behavioral factors. One example, major depressive disorder (MDD), shows that over 200 different symptom profiles  can  result  in  the  same  diagnosis,  showing  that  patients might not share any overlapping symptoms (Fried and Nesse, 2015). Further, the same disorders can result from a range of different causal pathways  (equifinality),  (Cicchetti  and  Rogosch,  1996)  while  single genetic or environmental factors can lead to different clinical outcomes (multifinality) (Cicchetti and Rogosch, 1996). Various other approaches have been made to quantify heterogeneity within psychiatric disorders, as discussed in (Nunes et al., 2020b).

The normative modeling approach estimates the mean and centiles of variation for each neuroimaging feature, enabling the calculation of individual  deviation  scores  from  this  normative  distribution.  Fig.  2A shows a visual representation of a normative model in three dimensions, where deviation scores across two features are modeled for the covariate age. Fig. 2B shows how the interindividual heterogeneity and deviation scores can be visualized using normative probability maps (NPM), which highlight the deviation score from a structural normative model at each brain  region  (Marquand  et  al.,  2016a).  Through  these  plots,  we  can easily identify deviating brain regions at an individual level.

In standard case-control analysis, the assumption is that patients will show overlapping effects in certain brain areas that are significantly different from those in control groups. Normative modeling offers the flexibility to test this assumption while simultaneously capturing individual differences. By summarizing deviation scores into mean, absolute, or sum scores of extreme values (| Z | &gt; 1 . 96 ) , we can compare these scores across individuals or groups, see Fig. 3A. Using these methods, it has  been  shown  that  for  certain  disorders  group  differences  can  be found, but that there remains a high level of heterogeneity within both the patient and control groups. For instance, individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) show unique brain development trajectories (Zabihi  et  al.,  2019),  with  only  a  few  displaying  consistent  atypical cortical thickness compared to controls (Bethlehem et al., 2020). This variability  among  individuals  with  the  same  diagnosis  would  not  be captured using a standard case-control analysis (Janssen et al., 2024). Similarly, people with a genetic predisposition, such as a pathogenic copy number variant (CNV), show a wide range of affected brain areas, which is lost when looking at aggregated deviation scores (Fraza et al., 2023).

This pattern of heterogeneity can also be found in ADHD (Wolfers et al., 2020; Bu et al., 2024), schizophrenia, and patients with psychosis (Liu et al., 2021; Di Biase et al., 2022; Wolfers et al., 2021; Haukvik et al., 2023; Giacomel et al., 2023; Remiszewski et al., 2022; Baldwin et al., 2022), depression (Lin et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023a) dementia, (Verdi et al., 2021), and Alzheimer ' s disease (Loreto et al., 2024; Pinaya et al., 2021; Kumar et al., 2023c, 2022) challenging our notion of an average patient and showing that there are no sharp boundaries between cases and controls in their neurobiological fingerprint. Instead of clear boundaries, individuals with psychiatric symptoms are more likely to show extreme deviations across different brain regions (Marquand et al., 2016a;  Fraza  et  al.,  2022;  Beirlant  et  al.,  2004).  For  example,  in schizophrenia, patients with first-episode psychosis are more likely to lie in  the  tail  end  of  the  distribution  of  cortical  thickness,  whilst  also demonstrating large heterogeneity between patients (Giacomel et al., 2023; Worker et al., 2023). In general, most brain disorders are now thought  to  lie  on  a  (potentially  multi-dimensional)  continuum,  with overlapping  risk  factors  and  symptoms,  lacking  clear-cut-off  values (Adam, 2013) in both behavior and neurobiology.

Identifying neurobiological heterogeneity within patient and control groups  offers  limited  clinical  utility,  beyond  suggesting  that  the behaviorally defined patient groups might not correspond to distinct biological entities. This helps establish the individual deviation scores as potential biomarkers. Although  their  specificity  in  distinguishing  clinical  trajectories  and  predicting  future  treatment  responses remains largely untested, see objective 4.

Thus, if one wants to  answer  the  question ' Can  I  predict  treatment  response  based  on neuroimaging data irrespective of covariates such as age, sex, and site? ' , normative models would be the best option. However, if the question is ' what is the best treatment for a patient, based on all the information available? ' . The raw measures could potentially carry more information.

## 1.4. (Long-Term) objective 4 normative modeling: individualized medicine

One of the long-term objectives of normative modeling is to advance precision or individualized medicine, where we can tailor treatments based  on  an  individual ' s  neurophenotypic,  behavioral,  and  genetic markers (Marquand et al., 2016a, 2019). Precision medicine has already been  applied  widely  in  diverse  fields  outside  of  psychiatry  and neurology,  most  notably  in  oncology  (Le  Tourneau  et  al.,  2015; McCarthy et al., 2013), however, it remains absent in the treatment of neurological and psychiatric disorders. Although precision medicine is frequently cited as an objective of normative modeling (Marquand et al., 2019; Bethlehem et al., 2020; Zabihi et al., 2019; Verdi et al., 2021; Wolfers et al., 2019), surprisingly few studies address the challenge of developing the methodological basis needed to support these inferences. One  small  study  on  multiple  sclerosis  has  made  a  first  attempt  at explicitly estimating longitudinal normative curves, finding that longitudinal  models  could  capture  within-subject  changes  better  than cross-sectional models (Keshavan et al., 2023). Furthermore, there have been a few papers using cross-sectional normative models for longitudinal inference (Bu ˇ ckov ´ a et al., 2024; Gaiser et al., 2024a), using adaptations  to  the  cross-sectional  models  to  allow  for  longitudinal inferences to be made; however, these are not true longitudinal models. Here,  we  make  a  clear  distinction  between  the  two  approaches:  (1)

constructing  longitudinal  normative  models  using  repeated  measures from  healthy  individuals  to  define  normative  developmental  trajectories,  further  described in the 'future methodological developments ' section, and (2) applying cross-sectional normative models to track deviation trajectories over time in clinical populations. To bridge this gap, we propose a framework for improving normative modeling, drawing inspiration from the growth chart literature used to monitor height and weight in children in the clinic, which involves three stages screening, surveillance,  and monitoring ( Cameron, 2022 ) .  To  avoid  negative connotations associated with the term ' surveillance ' , we opt to rename the stages: screening, tracking, and monitoring, see Fig. 4.

In the screening stage, the goal is to identify individuals who are significantly above or below a certain centile, similar to how individuals are classified as having stunted growth if they have a deviation score below GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 2 (de Onis and Branca, 2016). For example, in the context of schizophrenia, we would establish a deviation score cut-off point for specific brain regions associated with the disorder, such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (Callicott, 2000; Weinberger et al., 1988) or the temporal lobe (van Erp et al., 2018). A thickness measure below the 5th centile in these regions might then indicate an individual risk factor for schizophrenia. There is currently no consensus on the optimal thresholds to use as a clinical cutoff point, although often values below the 5th and above the 95th centiles are used. Another difficulty is the dimensionality  of  the  deviation  scores,  which  introduces  the  challenge  of multiplicity. Unlike pediatric growth charts, where typically only one growth chart is used in the assessment of pathology, within normative modeling for brain data, we have a large number of normative curves to consider.  The  numerous  deviation  scores  can  be  reduced  to  a  single measure of risk through for example a simple sum score of the extremes or by clustering the deviation scores, as discussed before.

After  participants  are  screened  and  identified  as  having  high-risk markers for a neurological or psychiatric disorder across multiple modalities, they move to the tracking stage. Here, we periodically reassess groups identified during the screening stage to monitor changes over time.  For  example,  brain  thickness  measures  would  be  regularly  reevaluated  at  specified  intervals,  e.g.,  every  six  months  to  a  year,  to track general developments in brain measures. Typically, the tracking stage is done with smaller groups that differ in their risk scores, rather than the large population cross-sectional models for more detailed data collection  without  placing  a  large  burden  on  resources.  During  the tracking phase, interventions (e.g., therapy, medication, or exercise) can also be considered for high-risk groups or groups currently experiencing psychiatric  or  neurological  disorders.  The  effectiveness  of  these  interventions can then be tracked by observing changes in brain measures,

## 2. Future methodological developments toward precision medicine within psychiatry and neurology

In  the  three  stages  of  screening,  tracking,  and  monitoring,  it  is important to be able to track and evaluate individuals over time. This helps to determine if they stay within their centiles or if they show centile crossing up or down. A large shift in centiles could suggest a pathogenic pattern. However, from the growth charting literature, it is recognized that  centile  shifts  can  commonly  occur  during  certain  age  ranges, particularly  adolescence  (Cole,  2012,  2022).  During  this  time,  there might be a phase shift that can vary significantly depending on the stage of puberty, leading to delayed or accelerated growth. The term ' growth charting ' can be slightly misleading (Cole, 2022), as it implies that the reference models are established longitudinally or intended for longitudinal tracking. However, most growth charts and normative models in use are established through cross-sectional data (Cole, 2012; Lampl and Thompson, 2007). Cross-sectional estimates may not accurately predict future trajectories and can over- or underestimate them (Di Biase et al., 2023). While these models remain useful for assessing current deviation scores, they are not optimized for longitudinal assessments and detecting nontypical growth patterns. Therefore, there is a need for a method that  can  better  assess  whether  an  individual ' s  longitudinal  deviation patterns are typical, slower, or faster than the reference population.

For example, in a brain thickness normative model, the slope of an individual ' s growth curve can be compared to a specific velocity centile in the form of thrive lines, e.g., the thrive lines of the 5th or 95th velocity centile (Cole, 2022). If the slopes of our individual ' s curve and the thrive line ' s slope are parallel, the individual falls within that velocity centile. In Fig. 5, we illustrate this idea with successive measurements of one individual plotted on a normative model for brain thickness. Fig. 5B shows the normative model with thrive lines for the 5th velocity centile, and Fig. 5C for the 95th centile. When we connect the individual dots, we can observe that the individual follows the 95th velocity centile in the first months, indicating relatively rapid growth, and then in the subsequent months falls to the 5th velocity centile, indicating a large deceleration. There are other visualization techniques to illustrate growth history and future growth predictions, such as adaptive growth charts, which include both current growth and future predictions based on past individual measurements (Van Buuren, 2023). However, as more elements are included in the chart, the interpretation becomes more convoluted. Therefore, we aim to strike a balance between the amount of information presented and the expertise required for interpretation.

## 3. Discussion

Current normative models or brain charts cannot answer common questions such as: Is a certain centile crossing pathological or expected for that age range? When does a deviation from the centiles of variation indicate pathology? At which point in a longitudinal deviation trajectory should an intervention be performed? To answer these questions, we proposed  to  develop  longitudinal  reference  models  that  incorporate velocity  measurements  into  our  analyses.  Several  additional  factors should be considered when creating these models, which we will highlight here.

One important consideration is the size and the applicability of the reference cohort. The size of most cross-sectional normative models is currently limited by the number of available samples. When building cross-sectional and longitudinal normative models, the required sample

size depends on several factors, including the sparsity of data in the tails, where accurate estimation demands large samples, and the complexity of the underlying data, such as whether it is Gaussian or non-Gaussian. For example, it has been shown that normative models of volumetric brain  measures  typically  require  over  1000  participants  for  reliable estimation (Bozek et al., 2023). For more complex features like fMRI or DTI measures, most likely larger samples are needed for accurate estimation of the outer centiles, though clear benchmarks are still lacking. Differering from classical power analysis, which determine the number of  participants  needed  to  detect  group-level  differences,  normative modeling aims to asses how much one individual deviates from a reference norm . Larger reference cohorts then improve the precision of these assessments by reducing uncertainty, but a larger model is only useful if the reference cohort aligns with the research questions. For instance, a focused study on Alzheimer ' s in a specific demographic may require a small,  targeted  reference  sample,  while  a  general  model  capturing healthy population variance across the lifespan would demand a much larger and more diverse dataset. Thus, it is unlikely that there is a single ' universal ' reference model; instead, multiple normative curves tailored to different populations and questions will most likely emerge, shaped primarily  by  the  availability  of  data  and  specific  research  questions (Rutherford et al., 2024; H ´ ajek, 2007). We do encourage the field to move  towards  collaborative  data  sharing  and  integration  efforts, through, for example, federated learning (Kia et al., 2022). Such that multiple  research  teams  can  benefit  from  shared  normative  cohorts rather than repeatedly generating isolated models.

Another consideration for longitudinal models is the measurement frequency; without adequate data points, important temporal patterns could be missed (Lampl, 2022). The main tension in longitudinal data collection is balancing the need for dense measurements to identify early patterns of brain deviations indicative of disorders against the risk of overburdening individuals and clinicians.  We can  decide  to  examine longer  periods,  which  show  slower  varying  changes  in  velocity,  or shorter  periods,  which  may  translate  to  more  variable  velocity  measurements.  Further,  we  might  want  to  sample  at  a  higher  frequency during periods where significant inter-individual differences in velocity are  expected,  such  as  during  puberty  (Cole,  2012).  While  repeated measurements for the whole population provide the highest temporal specificity, this approach is also resource-intensive. Potentially requiring large samples of up to 100,000 individuals across multiple time  points,  matching  the  size  of  current  cross-sectional  reference models (Rutherford et al., 2022c). To mitigate costs, mixed longitudinal designs can be considered. These designs involve repeatedly measuring only a subset of individuals and then extrapolating longitudinal information to the entire cohort based on these measurements (Cole, 2022; Tanner, 1951). Another option is to use the cross-sectional models to quantify longitudinal changes (Bu ˇ ckov ´ a et al., 2024). This method has been implemented in schizophrenia (Bu ˇ ckov ´ a et al., 2024), showing the potential to distinguish significant longitudinal brain thickness changes.

Measurement  noise  plays  another  crucial  role  in  the  creation  of longitudinal normative models, which can arise from individual variability, instrument precision, and the measurement process. Accurate measurements are crucial to avoid individual differences in trajectories primarily driven by noise. For different neuroimaging modalities, we can establish  baseline  expected  measurement  error,  as  done  for  MRI modalities (Elliott et al., 2020; Hedges et al., 2022). This baseline can help constrain the levels of expected change within individuals upon reassessment so that we can better assess actual changes in the brain. A key factor is that the errors should be smaller than the magnitude of change between measurements to reliably distinguish biological brain changes  from  measurement  noise  (Lampl,  2022).  If  the  changes  are smaller than the noise, it would be impossible to identify whether the signal  is  driven  by  actual  brain  changes  or  random  noise.  Another method to account for the noise is to assume that the changes in deviation scores between short visits in the control group are due to observation noise, as we expect this group to have a relatively stable signal, both technical and physiological (Bu ˇ ckov ´ a et al., 2024).

Increasingly, the field has focused on creating ever larger models without fully considering which model fit criteria should be optimized, and it would be concerning if this trend continues in the development of longitudinal models. Currently, fit parameters focused on accuracy and the  mean fit  of  the  distribution  are  often  used,  neglecting  shape  parameters  and  residuals  of  the  distribution,  which  are  arguably  more important for assessing the fit of the normative curves to the data (Fraza et al., 2021b; De Boer et al., 2024; Dinga et al., 2021b). Several features have  been  reviewed  by  the  World  Health  Organization  (WHO),  as important when fitting normative curves: ensuring precise estimation of the  centiles,  avoiding  centile  crossing,  continuous  age  smoothing, addressing skewness and kurtosis, and using various diagnostic tools and normality tests (Borghi et al., 2006). Currently, only a few methods, such as the wBRL (Fraza et al., 2021b) and GAMLSS (Dinga et al., 2021a; Stasinopoulos et al., 2017) methods comply with most of the recommendations.  One  step  beyond  these  validation  methods  is  external validation of the deviation scores, which is essential to evaluate their clinical relevance. This can, for example, be done by estimating disease progression  markers,  future  treatment  outcomes,  or  disease  severity markers based on the deviation scores. Without this external validation, the deviations ' score and their trajectories remain merely descriptive.

Another important advancement in the normative modeling framework is the move towards multimodal data integration. Initial efforts have already started to integrate multiple data streams from EEG to MRI into  one  model  to  improve  the  prediction  of  mental  disorders.  For

example,  the  combination  of  multiple  imaging  modalities,  such  as cortical thickness and various DTI measures, has been used for early risk detection (Kjelkenes et al., 2022a). Furthermore, deviation scores from different modalities have been combined through the earlier-mentioned clustering  techniques,  ICA  (Holz  et  al.,  2023b),  PCA  (Fraza  et  al., 2021a), canonical correlation analysis (CCA) (Wang et al., 2020), and other dimensionality reduction techniques. However, ideally, we would move away from the simple combination of deviation scores after the normative  modeling has  already  been  fitted  and  move  towards truly multimodal modeling frameworks. These models would be designed to learn from multiple data types simultaneously, capturing the joint distribution between various modalities and predicting multiple outcomes. Within the deep learning space, different models, such as the multimodal variational autoencoder (Kumar et al., 2023b, 2022) can be used for this purpose. Currently, the theory to create multimodal normative models is present, using, for example, Gaussian Mixture Models; however, this has not been explicitly implemented yet.

Aside from the normative modeling framework, other advancements in machine learning have been used to create so-called brain age models (Cole  et  al.,  2019,  2017).  In  these  types  of  models,  they  reverse  the mapping using brain data to predict age, rather than using age as a covariate to predict brain data. A gap can then be established between the estimated brain age and one ' s true age, which can be useful for assessments of general health. However, one difficulty with these types of models  is  that  two  participants  might  have  the  same  brain  age,  but different underlying brain abnormalities or patterns contributing to this brain age, making it hard to make conclusive inferences about individual differences in neuroimaging features (Marquand et al., 2019).

Broadening the discussion to the general technological convergence happening within precision medicine, we see many new and exciting advancements that will enable more individualized treatments, beyond neurimaging  data  and  normative  modeling.  For  example,  large-scale data  sources  are  increasingly  becoming  available  from  wearable  devices (e.g., sleep, heart rate variability, and physical activity patterns), which  have  been  shown  to  be  potentially  predictive  of  depression (Abd-Alrazaq et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2021). The integration of medical records and real-time monitoring of health markers, through wearables, as  well  as  digital  health  tools  for  cognitive  assessments,  can  all  be incorporated into the same deviation or normed latent space through normative  modeling.  For  instance,  in  cognition,  norms  have  already been widely applied, and the more flexible normative models currently being  used  in  neuroimaging  (HBR,  GALMSS,  BLR)  are  now  being adapted to cognition to create better norms that reflect the population variability (Marquand et al., 2025). One key challenge in bringing these data sources together is that they can vary in scale and timing. We have to  balance  sample  size,  measurement  breadth  (i.e.,  number  of  measurements), and longitudinal follow-up with the potential burden on the individual patient, as  well as  considering the clinical  resources  (Gell et al., 2025). We want to create both population-based as well as individualized models to maximize within-person precision and between-person generisability, and the designs of our research need to be adaptive to fit both needs (Gell et al., 2025).

Within computational psychiatry, there is a further push to include the  dynamic  and  time-dependent  nature  of  psychiatric  disorders (Hitchcock et al., 2022), and there is a growing shift towards real-time adaptive modeling. In areas such as digital phenotyping, using mobile phone data for high-frequency assessments, such adaptive approaches are already being implemented (Gillan and Rutledge, 2021). A similar direction could potentially be explored using normative modeling. One example of model adaptation within normative modeling is the addition of data site-by-site in a federated learning manner (Kia et al., 2022), which showed to converge to a similar model as one trained directly on the whole dataset. We expect the population-level normative models to remain relatively stable, if they have been correctly modelled; therefore, on this level, dynamic updating would not be necessary, unless the underlying population demographics change significantly. However, at an individual  level,  the  estimated  trajectories  could  be  continuously updated as new data becomes available. For example, within pediatric growth charting, new methods are being developed for visualization techniques  that  allow  for  continuous  updating  (Van  Buuren,  2023). Using these advancements and the longitudinal normative models, we could imagine more temporally sensitive assessments. Regarding these updates, certain challenges remain, such as the cost-benefit trade-offs (Najafpour et al., 2021) and feasibility  of  frequent neuroimaging assessments for certain parts of the population. A more realistic approach could  involve  a  combination  of  more  infrequent  neuroimaging  measurements  with  more  frequent  physiological  and  behavioral  assessments, creating a multimodal fingerprint, as shown in Fig. 2.

Finally, in this paper, we have laid out the three potential stages of applying normative modeling in a future precision psychiatry context: screening -tracking -monitoring (Cameron, 2022). Currently, within neuroimaging, we are at the screening and tracking stage, where we create large normative reference curves, evaluate individual deviation scores,  and  assess  which  deviation  scores  are  meaningful  for  the demarcation of health and disease. Thinking beyond these stages towards true clinical usage and the possible integration of these models into healthcare systems, follow-up steps include individualized monitoring  and  intervention  strategies,  which  include  predicting  illness onset, treatment response, and potential risk of relapse at the individual level.

To move closer to the goal of possible clinical integration, several problems  have  to  be  solved.  Ultimately,  we  would  have  a  highdimensional  deviation  score  feature  that  needs  to  be  reduced  to  a comprehensible risk score, through, for example, cut-off values, extreme values (Fraza et al., 2022), or clustering (Di Biase et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022; Zabihi et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2023), such that the information can be used by a clinician. Beyond the methodological details of creating such a score, we also need to consider the clinical implications of the usage of these models in more detail: for example,  is  early  intervention  advisable,  how  can  we  ensure  transparency in our models, and what do these risk scores truly mean? Within the growth-charting literature, there is extensive documentation of both the advantages and disadvantages of using cross-sectional and longitudinal  curves  for  the  risk  assessment  of  delayed  growth  or  increased weight, where it is recognized that these charts are statistical measures of population trends; however, they are often used as a representation of 'optimal ' growth (Zemel, 2023).

Furthermore, ethical considerations are crucial when implementing this framework, as the use of high-dimensional data and sensitive data in psychiatric cohorts, unlike simple measures such as height or weight, demands even greater care in establishing effective clinical integration protocols. While early intervention and prevention are promising for certain disorders, the potential benefits and drawbacks must be carefully considered. For example, early detection of atypical brain development could prompt preventive measures for an at-risk individual, which could have unforeseen adverse effects later in life. Additionally, we might only be able to estimate deviant trajectories or velocities after it is too late for true  therapeutic  targeting.  Furthermore,  when  applying  the  crosssectional  and  longitudinal  normative  models,  potential  biases  in  the model and reference dataset must also be carefully considered. These biases can severely impact the interpretation of the deviation scores, as they might be driven by non-biological factors, such as race or a general mismatch  with  the  reference  population  (Rutherford  et  al.,  2024). Current  neuroimaging  databases  mainly  consist  of  WEIRD:  Western, Educated,  Industrialized,  Rich,  and  Democratic  data  (Henrich  et  al., 2010), making the reference dataset an inaccurate representation of the population, meaning that the deviation scores for certain minorities of the population could be invalid. This problem could become even more apparent  when  tracking  brain  development  norms  across  different groups,  due to  biased  dropout  rates,  particularly  among  less  healthy participants.

A key limitation,  not only  of  normative  modeling  but of  medical

research more broadly, is the lack of clear boundaries between health and  pathology.  This  is  especially  pronounced  in  psychiatry,  where behavioral and neurobiological phenotypes are highly contextdependent. For instance, an elevated stress response might be adaptive during high-pressure periods (e.g., exams), yet the same response in a calm  environment  could  signal  underlying  pathology.  Similarly,  individuals could show large deviation scores in certain brain regions, as an adaptive response to certain environmental stressors; these significant deviations could then be considered benign or non-pathological. This underscores the ambiguity in delineating health from disease and in  defining  diagnostic  categories.  As  a  result,  sensitivity  (correctly identifying individuals with a disorder) and specificity (correctly identifying  those  without)  are  inherently  challenging  when  diagnostic boundaries are not clear-cut. However, we argue that this is not a flaw of normative modeling itself but reflects broader limitations of applying categorical diagnostic frameworks to continuous, heterogeneous phenomena. Normative modeling highlights the individualized,  probabilistic, and context-sensitive nature of neuroimaging and behavioral data, moving beyond simplistic group averages. Nonetheless, continuous deviation scores derived from normative models should always be interpreted  within  the  broader  context  of  an  individual ' s  environment, history, and behavior. These scores are best understood as one informative tool among many in a clinician ' s decision-making process, not as deterministic indicators of diagnosis or outcome.

In summary, using normative modeling with neuroimaging data is an effective approach for analyzing the heterogeneity found within psychiatric and neurological disorders. Findings from these studies highlight the significance of this heterogeneity and underscore the necessity of  considering  it  when  making  predictions  for  individual  patients, outside of the case-control setting. To truly uncover the benefits of the normative framework, several methodological advancements are needed, including the development of longitudinal normative models, velocity-based measurements, and thrive lines that quantify the degree of  surprise  when  individuals  cross  centiles  within  cross-sectional normative  models.  Implementing  these  methodologies  will  make  the application of precision medicine based on normative models feasible in the future.
